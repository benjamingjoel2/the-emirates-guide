You notice the quiet first. Step through the low doorway out of the east coast glare and the air inside is cool and dim, the walls thick and close, the light coming in through small openings rather than windows. In the middle of the room stands a single pillar, and everything above you rests on it.

This is Al Bidya Mosque, and it is one of the smallest buildings you are likely to visit in the UAE, and one of the most significant. The Fujairah Tourism and Antiquities Authority describes it as [the oldest mosque in the United Arab Emirates](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-PlacesOfWorship.html). If you are wondering whether visiting Al Bidya Mosque is worth the detour on a Fujairah trip, it is: it takes little time, it sits right by the coast road, and it shows you a side of the country that the glass towers of Dubai never will.

## Where Al Bidya Mosque is

The mosque stands on the road between Fujairah city and Dibba, on the UAE's east coast. The authority places it [about 35 km north of Fujairah city](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-PlacesOfWorship.html), on the way to Dibba Al Fujairah. Behind it, the bare slopes of the Hajar Mountains rise steeply; in front, the coastal plain runs down towards the Gulf of Oman.

The [UAE government portal](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/the-seven-emirates/fujairah) names the historic Al Bidya Mosque among Fujairah's major tourist attractions, alongside the Wadi Al Wurayah waterfalls and the Heritage Village. Even so, it is small enough that first-time visitors sometimes drive past without realising. Look out for the low, pale, domed building near the road, and for the small watchtowers on the ridge above it.

Al Bidya, often spelled Bidiyah or Badiyah, is a village in its own right. The tourism authority's [overview page](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-Overview.html) lists Al Bidiyah among the main towns and villages of the emirate, alongside Dibba, Masafi and Al Bithna, so you will see the name on road signs well before you reach the mosque.

## How the UAE's oldest mosque was built

Most of what makes Al Bidya special is in how it was put together.

### Stone, clay and no timber

According to the [tourism authority](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-PlacesOfWorship.html), the mosque was built using purely local materials: large and small stones, with burnt clay used to bind them. Unusually, no timber was used to raise the roof. Instead, it depends on the column in the middle of the prayer hall.

That is worth pausing on. In a region where wood was scarce and precious, the builders found a way to roof the space using only what the land around them provided. Compare it with the watchtower at Al Hail, southwest of Fujairah city, where the authority's [museums and forts page](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-MuseumAndForts.html) records that gravel, burnt clay and palm leaves were used together with strong wood for the ceilings. At Al Bidya the domes do the work that beams would do elsewhere.

### Four domes on a single column

The places of worship page explains that the central column holds up the mosque's four domes, set close together. Each dome is made of three domes placed one on top of another: a large dome at the base, then a smaller one, then a third with a tiny head. From outside, this gives the roof its stepped, slightly pointed look, a little like a cluster of rounded hills.

Stand outside and walk slowly around the building. You will see how the domes sit side by side, and how solid and low the walls are. Then go in and look up: from the inside, the logic of the whole structure meets at that single pillar.

### Details to look for inside

The authority lists a few features that reward a closer look:

- **Carved architectural decoration** on the interior.
- **A niche**, the mihrab, which shows the direction of prayer.
- **A small platform**, the minbar, from which the sermon is given.
- **Internal openings for ventilation**, which help keep the room cool.
- **Shelves** set into the walls.

Nothing here is grand. Its appeal is the opposite: plain materials, careful proportions and a sense of how people built for faith and for heat at the same time.

### Another name, and a restoration

The mosque is also sometimes called the [Othmanian Mosque](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-PlacesOfWorship.html), according to the authority. The same page records that Fujairah's Department of Antiquities and Heritage renovated and rehabilitated it as a tourist site, working with Dubai Municipality's Department of Historical Buildings, and that it was [opened by the Ruler of Fujairah in March 2003](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-PlacesOfWorship.html).

Exactly how old it is remains a matter for historians, and you will see different estimates quoted on signs and in guidebooks. We would rather not add one more guess. What is clear is that it belongs to a much older UAE than the one most visitors arrive in. The authority's [facts page](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-FactsAndStatistics.html) lists the mosque among the handful of things that define the emirate, next to its mountains and its position on the Indian Ocean.

## A mosque in a living community

It helps to remember what a small village mosque meant in the past. It was not only a place of prayer but the centre of local life, and often of learning. The tourism authority's [occupations and crafts page](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-OccupationsAndCrafts.html) describes the traditional Quran teacher, the mutawa, who taught the Quran, writing, calligraphy and arithmetic to boys and girls in rooms made of palm fronds, often as a social service paid for with voluntary gifts of money or food.

The same page describes the pottery made in mountain areas from local red, green and yellow clay. Clay, stone and palm were the building blocks of everyday life on this coast, and Al Bidya is one of the few places where you can still see all of that in a single building.

When you visit, you are stepping into that continuing life. Local people still pray here, so the mosque is not a stage set. That is part of what makes a visit feel so different from a museum.

## What to wear and how to behave

Al Bidya is not a museum. It is a working mosque in a small community, so treat a visit the way you would treat a visit to any place of worship.

### Dress code

The UK Foreign Office advises visitors to [dress modestly in public areas](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), with women covering shoulders and knees, avoiding transparent clothing, and notes that some buildings have specific dress codes. For a mosque, go further: loose clothing that covers arms and legs for everyone, and a scarf for women to cover their hair. Shoes come off before you step inside, so wear something easy to slip on and off.

Our guide to [what to wear in the UAE](/stories/what-to-wear-uae-etiquette/) has more on dressing for mosques and heritage sites.

### Behaviour inside

- Speak quietly, and keep phones on silent.
- Do not walk in front of anyone who is praying.
- Avoid visiting during prayer times; if a prayer begins, step outside and wait.
- Keep children close; the room is small and sound carries.

The Foreign Office also reminds visitors to [respect local traditions and religions](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), especially in religious areas and during Ramadan, and not to photograph people without their permission. Photograph the building, not the worshippers.

### Visiting during Ramadan

If your trip falls during Ramadan, the mosque will be busier at prayer times and the area quieter during the day. The Foreign Office notes that it may cause offence to [eat, drink, smoke or chew gum in public in daytime](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) during the holy month, including in your car, or to play loud music. It also warns that driving may be erratic around dusk, when people hurry home to break the fast, which is worth knowing on the coast road. Read [visiting the UAE during Ramadan](/stories/visiting-uae-during-ramadan/) before you travel.

## How a visit unfolds

Most visits are short and simple, which is part of the pleasure. You park near the road, and the first thing you see is the building in its setting: the low domes, the bare hillside behind, the towers on the ridge.

Take a slow walk around the outside before you go in. This is where you see the stepped domes best, and where you can photograph the whole building without anyone in the frame. Then leave your shoes by the door, step inside and give your eyes a moment to adjust to the dim light.

<photo-set layout="inset-right" keys="al-bidya-mosque-guide-5" alts="Round stone watchtower with a crenellated top on a rocky hill, reached by stone steps" caption="One of the watchtowers on the ridge above the mosque."></photo-set>

Inside, there is not much floor space, so let one group look around before the next goes in. Find the mihrab and minbar, look up at the domes, and notice how cool the room feels compared with outside. Afterwards, if the season and your fitness allow, climb the path to the watchtowers for the view over the mosque, the palms and the sea.

### Practical notes

- **Accessibility.** The doorway is low and the interior is small, and the path to the ridge is steep and uneven. Visitors with limited mobility can still see the mosque well from outside.
- **Families.** Children usually enjoy the climb to the towers more than the interior. Hold hands on the path, and bring water and hats.
- **Photography.** The Foreign Office warns that [it is illegal to photograph some government buildings](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) and that you should not photograph people without permission. A simple rule: the building yes, people no.
- **Facilities.** Do not count on shops or cafés right beside the mosque. Bring water and plan lunch elsewhere on the coast.

## When to go

Arrive early or later in the afternoon. The light is softer on the clay walls, the stone outside is cooler underfoot when you take off your shoes, and you are more likely to have the place to yourself.

Season matters too. The Fujairah Tourism and Antiquities Authority describes summer, from [May to September](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-Overview.html), as hot and sometimes humid, and the months from October to April as very pleasant. In the hotter months, a short visit straight from an air-conditioned car is fine, but leave the walk up the ridge for the cooler season.

We have not been able to confirm official visiting hours for the mosque. Hours and access can change, especially around prayer times and religious holidays, so check locally or ask your operator before you set out.

## What catches people out

- **Driving past it.** The mosque is small and close to the road. Watch for the domes and the towers on the hill above.
- **Arriving at prayer time.** The busiest moments are the prayers themselves. Plan to arrive between them.
- **Shoes and socks.** In the warmer months the ground outside can be hot. Socks help, and so do shoes you can slip on quickly.
- **Expecting a large site.** This is a single small room. The value is in the detail and the setting, not the size.

## Making a morning of it on the east coast

Al Bidya on its own is a short stop, so pair it with other places on the coast.

### Head south to Fujairah's forts

Fujairah city has the emirate's best-known fort. The authority's [museums and forts page](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-MuseumAndForts.html) describes Fujairah Fort with three round towers and a fourth square tower, built from local stone, gravel, clay, hay and gypsum, which makes an interesting comparison with the mosque's materials. Fujairah Museum, nearby, shows traditional crafts, weapons and household objects that help you picture the communities who built and used both.

For a contrast in scale, the authority's places of worship page also describes the much newer [Sheikh Zayed Mosque in Fujairah](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-PlacesOfWorship.html), modelled on its namesake in Abu Dhabi and described as the second largest mosque in the UAE.

### Or continue north to Dibba

North of the mosque, the coast road reaches Dibba, where a town is shared between three territories. [Visit Sharjah](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/regions/east-coast/dibba-al-hisn/) explains that Dibba Al Hisn belongs to Sharjah, Dibba Al Baya to the north is part of Oman, and Dibba Muhallab to the south belongs to Fujairah. It mentions mosques, produce markets, a souk and a restored fort, as well as boat trips along the coast.

### Or spend the afternoon in the water

The beaches near Al Bidya are the starting point for many snorkelling trips. Our guide to [snorkelling and diving on the east coast](/stories/snorkelling-diving-uae-east-coast/) explains how the boat trips work and what to check first.

## Quick answers

**Is Al Bidya Mosque the oldest mosque in the UAE?** The Fujairah Tourism and Antiquities Authority describes it as the oldest mosque in the United Arab Emirates.

**Where is Al Bidya Mosque?** On the east coast road between Fujairah city and Dibba, north of Fujairah city, with the Hajar Mountains rising behind it.

**Can non-Muslims visit Al Bidya Mosque?** It was restored and opened as a tourist site, but it remains a place of worship. Dress modestly, take your shoes off, and avoid prayer times.

**What is special about its design?** Its four domes rest on a single central column, each dome is made of three stacked domes, and no timber was used to raise the roof.

**How long does a visit take?** Not long. It is a single small prayer hall, so most people combine it with Fujairah Fort, Dibba or a beach on the same day.

## Questions to ask your operator

- When is the mosque open to visitors on our date, and which prayer times should we plan around?
- Is there someone on site, or a guide you can arrange, who can explain the building and its history?
- What should each of us wear, and should we bring scarves?
- Is the walk up to the watchtowers on the ridge safe for us in the season we are travelling?
- How can we combine the mosque with Fujairah Fort or Dibba without spending too long in the car?
