Step off the road into the first lane and the traffic noise drops away almost at once. What is left is wind moving through empty doorways, the crunch of sand underfoot and, somewhere above you, a pigeon shifting on a crumbling parapet. The houses still have their doorframes. Many have lost their roofs, so the sky pours straight into rooms where families once ate, slept and waited for the pearling boats to come home.

This is Al Jazirat Al Hamra, the abandoned pearling village on the Ras Al Khaimah coast. It is one of the most atmospheric heritage sites in the UAE, and one of the easiest to visit: open-air, free to wander and close to the main highway. Come in daylight, bring water and shade, and treat it gently, because it is fragile.

## What Al Jazirat Al Hamra is

You will see the name spelled several ways, including Al Jazeera Al Hamra and Al Jazirah Al Hamra. It means "Red Island". [Visit Ras Al Khaimah](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/discover/attractions/al-jazeera-al-hamra/) calls it the only remaining historical pearling village in the entire Gulf region, explaining that the others were demolished after the discovery of oil. The [UAE government portal](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/the-seven-emirates/ras-al-khaimah) describes it as one of the best-preserved traditional fishing villages in the Arabian Gulf.

The tourism board adds that the village is on the UNESCO World Heritage Cultural Tentative List.

## A village built from the sea

### Coral, shells and mangrove wood

Look closely at any wall and you will see what the sea gave the builders. According to [Visit Ras Al Khaimah](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/discover/attractions/al-jazeera-al-hamra/), the houses were made from local materials: coral blocks and fossilised beach rock, mangrove beams, date palm trunks, palm matting and rope, with layers of seashells for drainage.

The result is a pale, porous stone that weathers to warm honey and pink tones in the late light. In places the plaster has fallen away and you can see the coral itself, pitted and full of tiny chambers.

### Every part of a working town

This was not a scattering of huts. The tourism board lists a fort and watchtowers, a mosque, a souq and courtyard houses of different designs, from small simple homes to two-storey buildings and a large courtyard residence that belonged to a wealthy pearl merchant. Narrow alleys connected the houses to the souq and the village mosque.

Look up for the decoration: the same page mentions plain and ornamental archways and elaborate plaster screens.

### The wind tower house

One building stands out. The tourism board says the wind tower residence has a textbook example of the barjeel, a windcatcher that funnels breezes down into the rooms below as a passive cooling system. Stand underneath it on a breezy day and you can still feel the idea working.

## The people who lived here

The village's story is a pearling story. [Visit Ras Al Khaimah](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/discover/attractions/al-jazeera-al-hamra/) says that at the turn of the last century there were some 500 houses here, home to the Zaab tribe, who kept a fleet of 25 pearling boats as well as sheep and cattle until the pearl industry crashed in the 1920s.

The village was once a tidal island in two parts, the tourism board explains: the smaller northern quarter of Umm Awaimir and the southern quarter of Manakh. Its ruler in 1820 was one of the signatories of the original treaty between the Trucial States and the British, which followed the battle at Dhayah Fort in 1819. Archaeologists have also recorded an older mosque with 20 domes, shown on a British map from the 1820s.

Then oil changed everything. The tourism board says the residents left the village between 1968 and 1971.

The ground here is older still. Visit Ras Al Khaimah's [history pages](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/about-ras-al-khaimah/history-culture/) mention large ruins of early structures found not far from Al Jazirat Al Hamra, along with pottery resembling that of ancient Mesopotamia, evidence of very early trade.

## Walking Al Jazirat Al Hamra: rules and safety

The tourism board is clear about how to visit. It describes the site as an [unmanned open-air museum](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/discover/attractions/al-jazeera-al-hamra/) and says:

- Many buildings are ruined or in disrepair, so do not climb on them or go inside, as they may be unstable.
- Removing anything from the protected site is prohibited.
- Visit only in daylight, as there is limited lighting and no safety staff on site.
- Supervise young children closely because of the uneven ground and unstable structures.

It suggests the most comfortable time to visit is between 8am and 6pm, notes there is minimal shade, and recommends sunscreen, sunglasses, a hat, water and light snacks. It says there is no entry fee, though guided tours and events may charge.

### What to wear

Comfortable walking shoes matter more than anything else, because the lanes are sandy and uneven. The tourism board also suggests breathable, modest clothing, in line with its general advice that visitors are [expected to dress modestly](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/plan-your-trip/cultural-awareness/) out of respect for local culture. See our guide to [what to wear in the UAE](/stories/what-to-wear-uae-etiquette/).

### Photography

The tourism board says [photography is allowed](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/discover/attractions/al-jazeera-al-hamra/) and encouraged. The light is best early and late, when shadows fill the doorways. ## Getting there

Visit Ras Al Khaimah says the village is just off the E11 highway, about 20 minutes from central Ras Al Khaimah, with signage and parking near the entrance. You can drive or take a taxi, and local tour operators often include it in cultural tours.

## Before or after: the shore and the museum

The coast next to the village is worth a short detour. Visit Ras Al Khaimah notes that the stretch of beach beside Al Jazirat Al Hamra [has hosted thousands of great cormorants](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/blog/five-best-beaches-in-ras-al-khaimah/) and other birds on their seasonal migration, so bring binoculars in the cooler months.

To fill in the story, visit the [National Museum of Ras Al Khaimah](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/discover/attractions/national-museum-of-ras-al-khaimah/) in the old town, which the tourism board says covers heritage such as pearl diving and date farming. Then head north to [Dhayah Fort](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/discover/attractions/dhayah-fort/), where the 1819 battle that led to the 1820 treaty took place. Our guide to [climbing to Dhayah Fort](/stories/dhayah-fort-guide/) covers it.

## Quick answers

**Is Al Jazirat Al Hamra free to visit?** Visit Ras Al Khaimah says there is no entry fee, though guided tours or events held there may charge.

**When was Al Jazirat Al Hamra abandoned?** The tourism board says its residents left between 1968 and 1971, after the discovery of oil.

**Can I go inside the buildings?** No. The tourism board asks visitors not to climb on or enter the buildings because they may be unstable.

**When should I visit?** In daylight only. The tourism board suggests between 8am and 6pm and advises against visiting after dark.

**Is it suitable for children?** Yes, according to the tourism board, as long as young children are closely supervised on the uneven ground.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Does your guide know the village's history, including the Zaab tribe and the pearling fleet?
- What time of day would give us the best light and the least heat on our date?
- Can you bring shade, water and seating for older members of our group?
- Is there a heritage festival or event on at the village during our visit?
- Can we combine the village with the National Museum or Dhayah Fort on the same day?
