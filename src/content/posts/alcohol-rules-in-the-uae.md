A cold drink on a hotel terrace as the sun slides into the Gulf. For plenty of visitors, that is part of the holiday picture, and they are often surprised to learn it is entirely possible in the UAE.

It is also where the confusion starts. Alcohol rules in the UAE vary by emirate, depend on where you are standing, and come with a few lines that are firmly enforced. The short version: as a tourist aged 21 or over, you can drink in licensed venues in most of the country, but never in public, never before driving, and never in Sharjah.

This guide sets out the detail, drawn from UK government travel advice and official UAE sources. Rules can change and enforcement can vary, so check the linked pages before you travel.

## The short answer for visitors

- **Where:** licensed venues, such as hotel bars, restaurants and clubs.
- **Who:** adults aged 21 and over.
- **Not:** in public places, anywhere in the UAE.
- **Not at all:** in the emirate of Sharjah.
- **Never:** before driving, in any amount.

## Where you can drink: licensed venues

Picture the places that serve alcohol and you will mostly be picturing hotels. Visit Abu Dhabi says alcohol is [available to purchase in licensed areas](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/faqs), mainly within hotel bars and restaurants, and that you can only buy alcohol for personal use. It also notes that hotel outlets serve alcohol alongside many unlicensed restaurants, so do not assume a restaurant will have a drinks list.

The UK Foreign Office says that in the emirates where alcohol is available, non-residents can [buy and drink alcohol in licensed venues](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) such as hotels, restaurants and clubs, without a licence.

If a venue serves alcohol, it holds a licence for it. If you are unsure, ask the staff before you order.

### What an evening out actually looks like

For most visitors, a drink in the UAE happens in a fairly predictable setting: a hotel bar, a restaurant inside a hotel or resort, a beach club attached to a hotel, or a licensed lounge or club. Visit Abu Dhabi's point about unlicensed restaurants is worth remembering when you plan dinner: plenty of good cafes and restaurants serve no alcohol at all. If the evening hinges on a glass of wine, check the venue first; if it hinges on the food, you may find the best meals are alcohol-free.

The other thing that shapes an evening is the journey home. Because drinking in public and drink-driving are both illegal, the natural pattern is to arrive by taxi, stay inside the venue, and leave by taxi.

## The age limit

Bring your passport or photo ID if you look young. The Foreign Office says the legal age for [purchasing and drinking alcohol is 21](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) in Abu Dhabi, Dubai and all the Northern Emirates except Sharjah. Visit Abu Dhabi gives the same rule: [you must be over 21](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/faqs) to buy or consume alcohol.

That matters for families travelling with older teenagers or young adults. A drink that is legal at home at 18 is not legal here until 21. It is worth agreeing the plan before the trip rather than at the bar, so nobody is caught out or embarrassed when staff ask for ID.

## Public places: the line not to cross

This is the rule that gets people into trouble. The Foreign Office says it is [illegal to drink or be under the influence of alcohol in a public place](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) anywhere in the UAE.

Read that carefully: being under the influence counts, not just holding a drink. The Foreign Office notes that British nationals have been arrested and charged under this law, often after coming to the attention of the police for a related offence, such as disorderly or offensive behaviour.

So:

- No drinks on the beach, in parks or on the street.
- No open bottles in taxis.
- Keep celebrations inside the venue, and leave quietly.
- Book a taxi home rather than walking unsteadily through a public area.

### Behaviour after a drink

Alcohol lowers inhibitions, and several UAE rules are about exactly the behaviour that follows. The Foreign Office says it is illegal to [swear and make rude gestures](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), including online, that showing affection in public is frowned upon and kissing in public can lead to arrest, and that financial crimes, including non-payment of bills, can lead to prison or a fine. A loud argument in a hotel lobby, a disputed bar bill or a rude gesture at a taxi driver can each become a police matter. If something is wrong with a bill, settle what you agree with, query the rest calmly with the manager, and keep the receipt.

### Look after your drink, and each other

The Foreign Office also warns against accepting drinks from strangers or leaving a drink unattended, as [drinks can be spiked](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) by criminals who then assault or rob their victims. It adds a sobering point about sexual assault: UAE law places a high burden of proof on the victim to show that sexual relations were not consensual, especially when the victim had been drinking. Stay with friends, arrange your journey home before you go out, and use a reputable taxi rather than accepting a lift.

## Sharjah: a different rule altogether

Sharjah sits right next to Dubai, and many visitors cross into it without noticing. The law changes at the boundary. The Foreign Office says [drinking or possessing alcohol is illegal in the Emirate of Sharjah](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), which it describes as lying 30km north of Dubai.

Possession matters as well as drinking. If you have bought alcohol elsewhere and your route passes through Sharjah, or you are staying there, think about that before you pack it. Sharjah is a wonderful place for museums, heritage and the old souks, and it is simply a dry emirate. Our guide to [visiting Sharjah](/stories/sharjah-rules-for-visitors/) explains its decency and alcohol rules in more detail, and our [day trip guide](/stories/sharjah-day-trip-from-dubai/) shows how to spend a day there.

### Practical tips for routes through Sharjah

- If you are staying in Sharjah, plan drinking evenings in a neighbouring emirate and travel back by taxi, without carrying alcohol.
- On road trips heading north or to the east coast, check whether your route crosses Sharjah territory before you buy anything to take with you.
- Airport transfers for flights into Sharjah are another moment to think about duty-free bottles.

## Dubai's liquor licence, explained

You may have heard that you need a licence to drink in Dubai. For most visitors, that is not the case in practice, but the detail is worth knowing.

The Foreign Office says liquor licences are [not required in Dubai to buy and drink alcohol in licensed venues](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) such as hotel bars, restaurants and pubs. A licence is required for drinking in private places like homes or private parties, and while that may not be strictly enforced, it remains a legal requirement.

Outside licensed venues in Dubai, the Foreign Office says you can only buy alcohol with a liquor licence from the two official liquor distributors. Tourists can get a temporary licence, valid for one month, from either one, and it is only for use in Dubai. Dubai residents who want to buy alcohol to drink at home need a liquor licence. In the other emirates where alcohol is available, the Foreign Office says licences are not required for non-residents, and are no longer required for residents either.

If you are staying in a private rental in Dubai and want to drink there, that is the situation the licence is designed for.

## Driving: zero means zero

There is no safe amount here. The Foreign Office says it is [illegal to drink and drive, no matter how small the amount](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), and that your insurance is likely to be invalidated in the event of an accident.

If you are hiring a car, plan evenings around taxis or a designated non-drinking driver. If you are doing a road trip, keep the drinking for the night you arrive and the car is parked, and remember that the morning after can still count. Our guide to [driving in the UAE as a visitor](/stories/driving-in-the-uae-as-a-visitor/) covers the other road rules.

### Getting home by taxi

Taxis make the whole system work. The Foreign Office advises using only [licensed taxis or recognised transport](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), including ride-hailing apps. It says authorised taxis in Dubai are cream with different coloured roofs, with pink-roofed taxis driven by women, and that taxis in Abu Dhabi are silver or grey. There are also special taxis for women and children, which can be hailed or booked. Visit Abu Dhabi notes that taxis in the capital are plentiful and can be flagged down at the roadside or [booked by phone](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/faqs).

## Families, groups and mixed tables

Plenty of groups include people who do not drink, for religious, health or personal reasons, and the UAE makes that easy. Hotel restaurants that serve alcohol also serve a full range of soft drinks, mocktails, fresh juices and coffee, and unlicensed restaurants are often where the most interesting food is. If your group is mixed, choose venues that work for everyone, and do not press anyone to join a round.

For families, the rules are simple to explain to children and teenagers: nobody under 21 drinks, nobody drinks outdoors in public, and the driver does not drink at all. Visit Abu Dhabi describes the emirate as a [very family-friendly environment](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/faqs), with play areas and children's menus common in restaurants, so an evening built around a family dinner rather than a bar is easy to arrange.

### Pork and other items on licensed menus

Alcohol is not the only item that is handled differently. Visit Abu Dhabi says non-Muslims can eat pork in [certain restaurants](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/faqs), where dishes containing pork are prepared separately and clearly marked on the menu. Bringing pork products into the country is a different matter: the Foreign Office says it is [illegal to bring pork products](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/entry-requirements) into the UAE.

## Good evenings without a drink

Some of the best UAE evenings have nothing to do with alcohol. A night in a desert camp, a waterfront walk along a corniche, a late visit to a souk or a sunset dhow trip are all alcohol-free by nature, and often more memorable than another hotel bar. Our guide to [overnight desert camping](/stories/overnight-desert-camping-uae/) is a good place to start if you want a night under the stars.

## Bringing alcohol through customs

The UAE government portal lists a duty-free allowance, citing a Government of Dubai guide, of [alcoholic beverages not exceeding 4 litres](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/visiting-and-exploring-the-uae/Tourist-services-and-information/Customs-clearance) or two cartons of beer, each of 24 cans of no more than 355 ml. It says customs duty and VAT are charged on quantities above the limits. The same page lists alcoholic drinks among restricted goods, which need the approval of the authority concerned.

Remember that an allowance at the airport does not change the rules on the ground. Alcohol you bring in still cannot be drunk in public, and possessing it in Sharjah is illegal.

## During Ramadan

Alcohol does not disappear during the holy month, but the atmosphere changes. Visit Abu Dhabi says licensed hotels, bars and restaurants [serve alcohol in Abu Dhabi during Ramadan](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/article-hub/abu-dhabi-ramadan-questions-answered), usually in designated indoor areas rather than public-facing spaces, and that service may start later in the evening, after iftar. It suggests checking with your venue, because house rules vary.

The same guide says tourists and non-Muslims are not expected to fast, and that visitors can still eat in restaurants, hotels and designated areas during the day. The courtesy is to avoid eating, drinking or smoking in public outdoor spaces during daylight hours. Evenings are busier than ever, and many visitors find that an iftar meal or a late walk through a lit-up souk becomes a highlight of the trip. ## Quick answers

**Can tourists drink alcohol in the UAE?** Yes, in licensed venues such as hotel bars, restaurants and clubs, if you are 21 or over, except in Sharjah, where drinking and possessing alcohol is illegal.

**What is the drinking age in the UAE?** 21, according to the Foreign Office and Visit Abu Dhabi.

**Do tourists need a liquor licence in Dubai?** Not to drink in licensed venues. A licence is required to buy alcohol from the official distributors and to drink in private places, and tourists can get a temporary one valid for a month.

**Can you drink on the beach in the UAE?** No. Drinking or being under the influence of alcohol in any public place is illegal.

**Is alcohol served during Ramadan?** In Abu Dhabi, licensed venues serve alcohol during Ramadan, usually in indoor areas and sometimes only after iftar, according to Visit Abu Dhabi.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Does any part of our itinerary pass through or stay in Sharjah, and what does that mean for alcohol in our luggage?
- Are drinks included on any of our tours or evenings, and are they served in a licensed setting?
- Can you arrange transfers back to our hotel after evenings out, so nobody needs to drive?
- If we travel during Ramadan, how will that affect drinks service at the venues on our itinerary?
- Are there good alcohol-free evening options you would recommend for non-drinkers in our group?
