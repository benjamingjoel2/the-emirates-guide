You have barely sat down when someone appears with a tall brass pot and a stack of tiny cups. A thin stream of pale gold Arabic coffee is poured, just enough to cover the bottom, and the cup is held out to you before anyone has asked what you would like.

This is the moment that confuses most visitors. Do you take it? With which hand? Will it keep coming? How do you stop it without seeming rude?

The short answer: accept it with your right hand, drink it in a few sips, take a cup or two more if you like, and gently shake the empty cup from side to side when you have had enough. That small gesture is the whole of Arabic coffee etiquette that most visitors need. The rest of this guide explains why, how the coffee is made, how dates fit into the ritual, the mistakes visitors most often make, and where you are likely to be offered a cup.

## Why the cup arrives before the question

In the UAE, coffee is not a menu item. It is a greeting. Visit Abu Dhabi describes the traditional serving of Arabic coffee as [one of the best ways to be welcomed](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/arabian-hospitality), sometimes with a plate of dates, whether at your hotel, a resort or someone's home.

UNESCO, which lists Arabic coffee as intangible cultural heritage of the UAE and several neighbouring countries, says it [highlights an important aspect of generosity in Arab societies](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/arabic-coffee-a-symbol-of-generosity-02111) and is served at weddings and at tribal gatherings, often alongside poetry and conversation. When someone pours for you, they are offering hospitality, not selling a drink.

Visit Abu Dhabi adds that the UAE, along with Saudi Arabia, Oman and Qatar, [added Arabic coffee to UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage list in 2015](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/coffee-and-dates). That shared listing is a useful reminder that the ritual you meet in the UAE belongs to a wider Gulf tradition, so the same etiquette will serve you well across the region.

## What is actually in a cup of Arabic coffee

Arabic coffee, or gahwa, will not taste like the coffee you know. Visit Abu Dhabi says it [comes blended with cardamom and saffron](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/coffee-and-dates) and is usually served in tiny handleless cups. The UAE government portal names those cups [finjans](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/culture/food), and gives the Arabic words for the pair: gahwa for coffee and tamr for dates.

Expect something light in colour, aromatic and slightly bitter, with the cardamom more noticeable than the coffee itself. There is no milk, and it is not sweetened. That is what the dates are for.

### The dallah and the fire

The pot is the dallah. Visit Abu Dhabi describes how Bedouins [brewed their coffee over a fireplace dug into the ground](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/coffee-and-dates), later replaced by a clay hearth, and says that today the dallah sits at the centre of every gathering, often beside the majlis fire. UNESCO's entry on the majlis mentions the same thing: [usually a stove or fire to prepare coffee](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/majlis-a-cultural-and-social-space-01076) and other hot drinks.

## How the coffee is made

You will rarely see the whole process, but knowing it helps you appreciate the cup. Visit Abu Dhabi describes making gahwa as an art of [roasting, grinding and brewing the beans](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/coffee-and-dates) to create a rich, aromatic flavour. The same page explains that the old fireplace in the ground gave way to the kuwar, a clay pit with a stove made from pebbles and stone plates, found in the majlis of houses or tents with a firewood container and a place beside it for the person preparing the coffee.

The spices go in during brewing, which is why the cardamom is so present. The result is poured from the dallah through its long spout into the waiting cup. If you are at a cultural centre or a heritage demonstration, this is the moment to watch: the pot, the pour and the way the server handles the cups are all part of the craft.

## The rules of the cup

UNESCO sets out the core etiquette clearly: [older people and guests are served first](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/arabic-coffee-a-symbol-of-generosity-02111), the coffee is poured with the right hand, and the person drinking shakes their cup to signal they are done. Here is what that looks like from your side of the cup.

### Take it with your right hand

Reach for the cup with your right hand. Visit Ras Al Khaimah's etiquette guide advises that you [take plates, cups and food with your right hand](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/blog/all-social-customs-and-etiquette-every-female-traveler-should-know/), because using the left is considered disrespectful. If you are holding a phone or bag, swap it to the other hand before the pot reaches you.

### Expect a small pour

Do not mistake the small amount for stinginess. Visit Abu Dhabi notes that each cup is [poured just a quarter full](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/coffee-and-dates). The coffee is meant to be drunk while it is hot, in a few sips, and then refilled.

### Have more than one, but not endlessly

The same Visit Abu Dhabi page says guests enjoy [one to three cups as a sign of respect](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/coffee-and-dates). Taking at least one is the polite minimum. Visit Ras Al Khaimah's etiquette guide notes that locals generally [find it offensive when guests refuse food and drink](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/blog/all-social-customs-and-etiquette-every-female-traveler-should-know/), and that if you have a medical reason, you should explain it to your host.

### Hold the cup, not the saucer

There is usually no saucer and no handle. Hold the small cup between your thumb and fingers near the rim, which keeps your fingers away from the heat and makes the shake easier when you are done. If the coffee is very hot, simply wait a moment rather than blowing on it.

### Shake to stop

This is the part people forget. While your cup is empty and upright, the server will keep refilling it, because hospitality assumes you want more. When you have had enough, hold the cup by the rim and give it a small wiggle from side to side as you hand it back. Per [UNESCO](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/arabic-coffee-a-symbol-of-generosity-02111), that shake signals you are done. A quick "shukran" (thank you) completes it.

## Mistakes visitors often make

None of these will cause a scene, and hosts are used to guests from everywhere. Still, they are easy to avoid:

- **Putting the empty cup down and waiting.** An empty cup placed on the table or held out still reads as an invitation to refill. Shake it as you hand it back instead.
- **Asking for milk or sugar.** Gahwa is served as it is. The dates provide the sweetness.
- **Taking the cup with the left hand,** usually because the right is holding a phone. Free your right hand before the pot arrives.
- **Draining a cup in one gulp and holding it straight out.** It works, but it can look hurried. A few sips with conversation in between is the natural pace.
- **Refusing outright with no explanation.** If you really cannot drink coffee, accept the cup, take the smallest sip or simply hold it a moment, then shake it and hand it back with thanks.
- **Expecting a menu.** In a hospitality setting there is nothing to order and nothing to pay for. Just accept what is offered.

## Where the dates come in

A bowl of dates usually sits alongside the coffee. Visit Abu Dhabi calls dates [a symbol of hospitality, heritage and nourishment](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/coffee-and-dates), traditionally served with Arabic coffee and still a staple in Emirati homes and gatherings.

The pairing works because of taste. A sip of bitter, cardamom-scented coffee followed by a soft, sweet date is the balance the ritual is built around. A few practical points:

- **Take dates with your right hand too,** one at a time.
- **Have a napkin or saucer ready for the stone,** and place it discreetly rather than leaving it in your hand.
- **Alternate.** Coffee, date, coffee is the natural rhythm, and nobody will mind if you take more dates than cups.

For more on varieties and where to buy them, see our guide to [dates in the UAE](/stories/uae-dates-guide/).

## Coffee in a majlis: the wider etiquette

The most traditional setting for coffee is the majlis. Visit Abu Dhabi explains that the word means [sitting place or council](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/majlis), and that many homes still have one for hosting visitors. UNESCO describes it as [a large space with carpets on the floor and cushions against the wall](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/majlis-a-cultural-and-social-space-01076).

A few habits make you an easy guest:

- **Greetings.** Visit Abu Dhabi notes that interaction between men and women is more reserved, and that [a woman will offer her hand if she wishes to shake](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/majlis). If you are unsure, a smile and a nod are always fine.
- **Sitting.** Avoid pointing the soles of your feet at anyone when sitting on the floor. Loose clothing makes this much easier.
- **Pace.** Coffee comes with conversation. Do not hurry to finish, and let your host lead.
- **Order of serving.** Remember that older people and guests are usually served first, so do not be surprised if the pot reaches you early, and do not reach for it before it is offered.

Visit Abu Dhabi notes that the majlis was traditionally a tented meeting spot where guests were [welcomed with coffee and conversation](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/majlis) before important discussions. ## If you cannot drink coffee, and coffee with children

Caffeine, pregnancy, medication or simple dislike are all fine reasons to go easy. The polite route is to accept the cup, take a token sip if you can, and then shake it. If you cannot take even that, explain briefly, as Visit Ras Al Khaimah's guide suggests for medical reasons, and your host will understand. Dates are always a welcome alternative, and taking one or two shows that you are accepting the hospitality, even if you skip the coffee.

Children are usually offered dates rather than coffee, and they are welcome in most hospitality settings. It helps to explain the cup shake to older children in advance, as they often enjoy doing it. Keep small children away from the dallah, which is hot and heavy, and from any open fire or stove in a traditional majlis.

## Where visitors are offered coffee

You do not need an invitation to someone's home to experience this. Coffee and dates often greet you at hotel check-in, at cultural centres and at heritage sites. In the Heart of Sharjah, Visit Sharjah suggests reviewing your purchases [over a cup of Arabic coffee at the café](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/shopping/souq-al-arsah/) in Souq Al Arsah, where shops also sell copper coffee pots. Visit Sharjah describes the souk as a covered market with air-conditioned alleyways, coral brick walls and hanging lanterns, close to the Heart of Sharjah along the corniche. Our [Heart of Sharjah guide](/stories/heart-of-sharjah-guide/) covers the old souks in more detail.

A hosted cultural breakfast or meal is another reliable setting, as coffee and dates are almost always part of it. Our guide to [an Emirati breakfast](/stories/emirati-breakfast-guide/) explains the dishes you are likely to meet alongside the cup.

If you want to take the ritual home, Visit Abu Dhabi lists the [dallah and its small cups](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/what-to-buy-and-souvenirs) among quintessential Emirati souvenirs, sold in souks and souvenir shops, and notes that supermarkets sell a range of Arabic coffee. The same guide describes the pots as often intricately designed in brass, copper or silver.

When you shop for a set, think about how you will use it. A decorative pot makes a good display piece, while a working dallah needs to be sound, easy to clean and comfortable to pour from. Pick it up, check the lid fits and the handle feels secure, and ask the seller whether it is meant for brewing or for display. Buy the coffee itself in a sealed pack if you are flying home, and check your own country's rules on bringing food and drink products back.

## During Ramadan

Dates play a special role in Ramadan. The UAE government portal says the fast is [broken with dates and laban (buttermilk)](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/public-holidays-and-religious-affairs/ramadan), following the example of the Prophet Muhammad. If you are offered coffee and dates during daylight hours in Ramadan, accept them in private rather than in public, and see our guide to [visiting the UAE during Ramadan](/stories/visiting-uae-during-ramadan/) for what changes. You can find more on local customs in our [food and culture](/food-culture/) section.

## Quick answers

**How do you say no to more Arabic coffee?** Gently shake your empty cup from side to side as you hand it back. UNESCO describes this as the customary signal that you are done.

**How many cups of Arabic coffee should I drink?** Visit Abu Dhabi says guests enjoy one to three cups as a sign of respect, and taking at least one is polite.

**Which hand should I use?** Your right hand, for the cup and for the dates.

**Why is the cup only partly filled?** Visit Abu Dhabi notes each cup is poured just a quarter full, so it is drunk hot and refilled.

**What does Arabic coffee taste like?** It is blended with cardamom and saffron, served without milk, and lighter and more aromatic than most coffee, with dates to balance it.
