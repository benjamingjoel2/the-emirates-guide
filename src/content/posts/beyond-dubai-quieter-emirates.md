There is a fort in Ras Al Khaimah you can only reach on foot, up 239 zigzagging steps, and from the top you look across date palms towards Oman and down to the sea. Many people who land in Dubai never make the climb, or even know it is there.

Drive north out of the city early and you can be there by mid-morning. The towers thin out, the billboards give way to low warehouses, and somewhere past Sharjah the horizon fills with a long grey-brown wall of mountains. Then you are standing in a palm garden where the only sounds are birds and the trickle of an irrigation channel, with mud-brick walls on the hill above you.

That is the UAE many visitors miss. The northern emirates and the east coast are not short on things to do, but they move at a different speed. Lunch is grilled fish by a harbour rather than a reservation. Heritage sites are often open-air and unstaffed, so you wander them in near silence. Roads climb into the Hajar range and drop down to a second coastline facing a different sea.

This guide goes deeper on Ras Al Khaimah, Fujairah, Umm Al Quwain, Ajman and Sharjah's lands beyond its city centre. For the quick overview of all seven emirates first, start with our [first-timer's guide](/stories/uae-first-timers-guide-seven-emirates/).

## Ras Al Khaimah: a hill fort, a ghost village and a mountain road

### The climb to Dhayah Fort

Of everything in the north, this is the climb we would tell a friend to make first. [Visit Ras Al Khaimah](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/discover/attractions/dhayah-fort/) describes Dhayah Fort as the only hill fort remaining in the UAE, set on a hill in the middle of an oasis between date gardens and the mountains, and reached by 239 zigzagging steps. The reward at the top is that view: palms, then mountains, then the sea.

Plan the practical side before you set off. The same official page notes there is no lighting and the climb is not accessible to prams, wheelchairs or other mobility aids. So go in daylight, wear shoes with grip, and carry water. Aim for the morning: the light on the mud-brick walls is softer and the steps are cooler underfoot.

### Al Jazeera Al Hamra, where the pearlers left

On the Gulf coast lies a place that feels frozen mid-sentence. [Visit Ras Al Khaimah](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/discover/attractions/al-jazeera-al-hamra/) describes Al Jazeera Al Hamra as the only remaining historical pearling village in the Gulf region, built from coral blocks, beach rock and mangrove beams, and left by its residents after oil was discovered. Its name means "Red Island".

Walk in and the noise of the road falls away. You find courtyard houses open to the sky where their roofs have gone, a wind-tower house still showing how the barjeel funnelled breezes indoors, a fort, a mosque and the outline of a souq. Wind moves through empty doorways; your footsteps sound loud. The official page notes the site is open-air and unstaffed, so treat it with care: stay off crumbling walls and bring your own shade.

### Up Jebel Jais, down to the old port

The [UAE government portal](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/the-seven-emirates/ras-al-khaimah) traces Ras Al Khaimah's story back some 7,000 years and recalls Julfar, a medieval port that was one of the prominent trading posts of the Gulf. Today the emirate's most famous road is the one up Jebel Jais, all hairpins and widening views. Beyond the viewpoint, [Visit Ras Al Khaimah](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/location/place/jebel-jais/) lists six designated mountain trails totalling 16 km, for walkers of different experience levels.

The rhythm that works: a short trail in the morning, then back down for a late lunch near the coast while the heat peaks. Take a warm layer for the top and more water than you think you need.

## Fujairah: the other coast and its wadis

Crest the Hajar range and the road tips you down towards a different sea. The [UAE government portal](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/the-seven-emirates/fujairah) describes a coastline on the Gulf of Oman running from Fujairah city north to Dibba, backed by rugged mountains, valleys and oases. In the late afternoon the light here feels different, because the sun drops behind the peaks rather than into the water, and the shore falls into shadow while the mountains still glow.

### A mosque with no timber, and a string of forts

North along the coast road, the small Al Bidya Mosque is worth slowing down for. The [Fujairah Tourism and Antiquities Authority](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-PlacesOfWorship.html) explains that its four close-set domes rest on a single central column, with no timber holding up the roof. Step inside from the glare and it is cool and dim. Treat it as the place of worship it is: dress modestly and visit quietly, away from prayer times.

Fujairah also has a string of forts. The authority's [museums and forts page](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-MuseumAndForts.html) describes Fujairah Fort, with its three round towers and one square tower, as one of the largest in the emirate, and lists smaller forts such as Al Hail and Al Bithna in the valleys inland. Fujairah Museum has halls on traditional work such as farming, fishing and trade, a good way to make sense of the forts and villages you have been driving past.

### Wadis: beautiful, and worth respecting

The wadis are the real reason many people come. Boulders, pale gravel, the occasional pool reflecting the cliffs. The authority's [natural sites page](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-NaturalSites.html) describes Wadi Al Wurayah as a boulder-strewn valley protected for its flora and fauna, and notes that Wadi Ham, usually dry, fills with flowing water after rain.

That last point matters more than any view. Rain in the mountains can turn a dry channel into a fast stream quickly, so check the forecast and local advice, and never drive or camp in a wadi bed when rain is possible.

The beaches are tempting too. The UK government's advice warns that rip currents can occur at any beach and advises swimming only at approved beaches with lifeguards and flags ([FCDO safety and security](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security)).

## Umm Al Quwain: a lagoon at first light

Early on, the lagoon is so still it holds the sky. Wading birds stalk the shallows, and the islands sit low and green on the horizon. Umm Al Quwain rewards people who like water and quiet. The [UAE government portal](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/the-seven-emirates/umm-al-quwain) describes rich coastal mangroves and a group of islands east of the mainland, and says the emirate plans to greatly expand its mangrove cover. The town itself sits on a narrow peninsula, so the water is never far away.

Make the early hours count: paddle or walk the lagoon edge while the air is still. Later, the waterfront keeps the unhurried feel of a fishing town. It is an easy overnight stop between Dubai and Ras Al Khaimah, and staying the night is what lets you be on the water at dawn.

## Ajman: a fort, a corniche and valleys you would never guess at

Ajman's coast is short, but the emirate has more depth than its size suggests. In town, Ajman Fort has been restored and turned into a museum displaying archaeological finds and objects from daily life, according to the [UAE government portal](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/the-seven-emirates/ajman). It makes a good hour before the evening walk along the corniche, when families come out to the beach and the heat eases.

The surprise is inland. Ajman also governs Manama and Masfout, which the same portal describes as areas of valleys and mountains, with Masfout known for agriculture and for its castle. They are a world away from the coast and work best as part of a mountain drive with a guide or driver who knows the roads.

## Sharjah, far from the souks

Most visitors know Sharjah for its museums and souks. Fewer realise how much of the emirate lies out in the desert and on the far coast.

### Mleiha: a desert with a past

Out here the ground is pale, the rock formations catch the low sun, and the silence is broad. In the desert of central Sharjah, Mleiha mixes landscape and archaeology. [Visit Sharjah](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/adventure/mleiha-national-park/) places it near Jebel Fayah, 65 km south-east of Sharjah city, and describes a circular Bronze Age Umm an-Nar tomb, an archaeological centre, and Fossil Rock, a huge outcrop full of marine fossils. Activities range from guided site tours and hiking to evenings in the desert.

Mleiha suits people who want a desert day that is about more than the dunes. Go in the cooler months, start early, and pair a site tour with a sunset over the rock formations. Our guide to [whether a desert safari is worth it](/stories/is-a-desert-safari-worth-it/) helps you decide what kind of desert experience you want. The UK government's advice notes that desert excursions can be dangerous without a properly equipped four-wheel-drive and recommends travelling with other cars, water and a phone ([FCDO safety and security](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security)), which is one more reason to go with an experienced guide.

### Khorfakkan and Kalba on the Gulf of Oman

Sharjah also reaches the Gulf of Oman. [Visit Sharjah's East Coast guide](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/regions/east-coast/) describes a stretch where mountains meet beaches and mangroves, including the seaside towns of Khorfakkan and Kalba. In Khorfakkan, a Roman-inspired amphitheatre faces the bay beside a 45-metre man-made waterfall cut into natural rock, which [Visit Sharjah](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/culture/khorfakkan-amphitheater-and-waterfall/) notes is often lit up at night. It is worth staying on after dark to see it.

Further south, Kalba is the gentler choice. The [Khor Kalba Mangrove Centre](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/nature-and-wildlife/khor-kalba-mangrove-centre/) sits within what Visit Sharjah calls the oldest mangrove forest in the UAE, and visitors can look for birds such as the Arabian collared kingfisher. Bring binoculars and patience; the birds come to those who sit still.

## Small things that make the trip

- **Drive yourself or hire a driver.** These areas are easiest to explore by car, so most visitors do one or the other. Mountain and wadi roads reward a careful, unhurried pace, and leave time to stop when something catches your eye.
- **Dress for local places.** Forts, villages and mosques are part of community life. The UK government's advice says women should cover shoulders and knees in public ([FCDO safety and security](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security)), and modest dress suits everyone here; our [etiquette guide](/stories/what-to-wear-uae-etiquette/) has more.
- **Mind the season.** Mountain trails and open-air ruins are far more pleasant outside the hottest months. Our [month-by-month guide](/stories/best-time-to-visit-uae-month-by-month/) helps you pick dates.
- **Plan your day around the heat.** Climb and walk early, drive and lunch in the middle of the day, and save the coast for late afternoon.
- **Bring cash and snacks.** Small villages and heritage sites may have few services.
- **Taste as you go.** Harbour-side fish and local bakeries are part of the trip; see our guide to [Emirati food](/stories/emirati-food-what-to-eat-where/).

## Questions to ask your operator

- Can you plan a loop through Ras Al Khaimah, Fujairah and Sharjah's east coast that avoids long drives in the heat of the day?
- Which wadis are safe to visit on our dates, and how will you check rain and water levels on the day itself?
- Can you arrange a guide for Dhayah Fort, Al Jazeera Al Hamra or Mleiha who can explain the history on site?
- Which Jebel Jais trail suits our fitness, and what should we carry?
- Which east-coast beaches have lifeguards and flags, and is snorkelling realistic in the conditions?
- Is an overnight in Umm Al Quwain or Kalba worth adding, and what can you arrange there?
