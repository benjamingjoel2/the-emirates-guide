The suitcase is nearly packed. Sun cream, adaptor, a paperback, and then the small bag of tablets you take every day without thinking. Painkillers, something to help you sleep on the flight, the prescription that keeps you well.

At home, none of that needs a second glance. Bringing medicines into the UAE is different, because some everyday medicines are tightly controlled there, and the rules apply at the border whether or not you knew about them. The good news is that most medicines cause no trouble at all, and the ones that do can usually be travelled with legally if you sort the paperwork before you fly.

This guide explains how the system works, what to check, and where to find the official answer for your own medicines. It is not medical advice. Speak to your doctor or pharmacist about your prescriptions, and check the official sources linked here before every trip, because the rules can change.

## Why ordinary medicines can be a problem

The UK Foreign Office puts it directly: there are [strict rules about what medications you can bring into the UAE](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/entry-requirements). Its health advice adds that in the UAE, [some UK prescribed and over-the-counter medicines are considered controlled substances](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/health).

That is the key idea. A medicine you buy freely at home, or one your doctor prescribes routinely, may sit in a controlled category in the UAE.

The UAE government explains that [narcotic and psychotropic drugs are controlled medicines](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/health-and-fitness/Health-and-wellbeing/drugs-and-controlled-medicines), and that controlled drugs of class A or B are neither freely available in the UAE nor freely imported. It adds that the majority of medicines used worldwide are available in community pharmacies and hospitals in the UAE.

### How the UAE sorts controlled medicines

The same government page sets out the categories. Narcotic and psychotropic medicines are classed as controlled drugs, class A, and class B drugs are described as semi-controlled. Both fall under the controlled or restricted items category for import. The UAE is a signatory to international conventions on narcotic and psychotropic substances, and the government says those conventions aim to make such substances available only for medical and scientific purposes.

You do not need to learn the categories. What matters is the practical result: if a medicine falls into either class, you should treat it as needing approval before you travel, and check the quantity rules below.

## The approval process, step by step

### Step one: find out whether your medicine is controlled

Start by listing every medicine you plan to carry, including over-the-counter ones, by its generic name rather than the brand. Then check each against the UAE's lists. The government portal links to a [list of controlled medicines](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/health-and-fitness/Health-and-wellbeing/drugs-and-controlled-medicines) published through Dubai Customs, and the Foreign Office says a list of narcotic, psychotropic and controlled drugs, the allowed quantities and the documents required can be found on the [UAE Ministry of Health website](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security).

If you are unsure what is in a medicine, ask your pharmacist. Combination products, such as some cold and flu remedies or painkillers, can contain ingredients that are controlled.

### Step two: get approval before you travel

For medicines the UAE classes as narcotic, psychotropic, controlled or semi-controlled, the Foreign Office says [you will need approval from the UAE authorities](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/entry-requirements), and that approval must come in advance from the UAE Ministry of Health. You can apply online. The Foreign Office says the UAE authorities advise that applications are normally processed within five working days, so do not leave it until the week of departure.

The government portal lists the Ministry of Health and Prevention's [request for permission to import medicines for personal use](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/health-and-fitness/Health-and-wellbeing/drugs-and-controlled-medicines) among its services.

If you have questions about a particular medicine or the process, the Foreign Office gives contact details for the UAE Ministry of Health: email info@moh.gov.ae or telephone +971 800 11111. Keep a copy of any approval you receive, printed and on your phone, and pack it with the medicine it covers.

### Step three: respect the quantity limits

Even with approval, there are limits. According to the Foreign Office, you can bring up to [three months' supply of medication as a visitor](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/entry-requirements), reduced to one month's supply if the medication is on the list requiring approval.

For other prescribed medicines, the government portal says you may bring them in [for personal use in normal quantity](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/health-and-fitness/Health-and-wellbeing/drugs-and-controlled-medicines). Customs rules list medication for personal use, [in compliance with the applicable regulations](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/visiting-and-exploring-the-uae/Tourist-services-and-information/Customs-clearance), among items exempt from duty. The same customs page lists medicines, drugs, and medical equipment, devices and tools as restricted goods that fall under the Ministry of Health and Prevention, alongside cosmetics and personal care products and e-cigarettes under other authorities.

## A planning timeline that works

The rules are easier to follow if you work backwards from your departure date.

- **As soon as the trip is booked:** list every medicine, with generic names and doses, and check each against the UAE lists. TravelHealthPro also recommends a [medical check-up](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/factsheet/43/medicines-abroad) before travel if you take regular medication, to discuss fitness to travel and make sure you have enough supplies.
- **At least eight weeks before:** the Foreign Office suggests checking vaccine recommendations [at least eight weeks before your trip](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/health), which is a natural moment to raise medicines with your doctor or practice nurse too.
- **Several weeks before:** apply for UAE approval for anything controlled, leaving plenty of margin beyond the five working days the authorities advise.
- **The week before:** collect your prescription copies, a letter from your prescriber and your approval, and check the packaging and labels.
- **Packing day:** split supplies between hand luggage and hold, as described below.

### If you are travelling from the UK with a controlled drug

There is a second set of rules at the UK end. TravelHealthPro says travellers taking certain [controlled drugs may need a UK export licence](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/factsheet/43/medicines-abroad) to carry set quantities, usually three months' supply or more, into or out of the UK, and that the application should be made at least ten days before travel. It gives examples of drugs controlled in the UK, including codeine, diazepam, morphine, temazepam and Ritalin. For smaller supplies, it says the Home Office advises carrying a letter from your GP or prescriber detailing the medicines.

That UK requirement is separate from UAE approval. You may need both.

## What happens if you skip it

This is the part worth reading twice. The Foreign Office warns that if you arrive [without evidence of prior approval](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/entry-requirements), the medication will not be allowed into the UAE and you may be prosecuted.

The UAE takes a firm line on drugs generally. The government portal describes a [zero-tolerance policy for recreational use of drugs](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/health-and-fitness/Health-and-wellbeing/drugs-and-controlled-medicines), and says federal law criminalises the import, possession and transport of narcotic and psychotropic substances unless done as part of supervised and regulated medical or scientific activity.

TravelHealthPro notes more generally that medicines for recognised health conditions [may come under intense scrutiny](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/factsheet/43/medicines-abroad) at border controls, and that this can mean delays, disruption or confiscation if the correct paperwork has not been arranged. Having everything in order is what turns a tense conversation into a routine one.

## Changing planes in the UAE

Many people never leave the airport. For them there is some relief: the Foreign Office says you [do not need to complete this approval process](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/entry-requirements) if you are only changing planes and not passing through UAE immigration.

That does not apply to illegal substances. The Foreign Office notes that the technology used to detect illegal items, including cannabis, is [also used to scan the baggage of transiting passengers](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), and that you can be arrested for carrying drugs, even residual amounts. It also says the authorities count the presence of drugs in the bloodstream as possession.

If a long layover tempts you into the city, remember that stepping through immigration changes the picture: from that moment, the approval rules apply.

## CBD, skincare and vape refills

These are the items people forget are medicines, or forget might contain something restricted.

The Foreign Office says some [herbal highs and products containing CBD are illegal in the UAE](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security). It also warns that some skincare products and e-cigarette refills may contain ingredients that are illegal there, such as CBD oil. If you are found with them they will be confiscated, and you could face criminal charges.

TravelHealthPro, the UK's travel health service, makes the same point: CBD products that are legal in the UK are banned in many countries, and [even transiting through airports in certain countries such as UAE](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/factsheet/43/medicines-abroad) with them could have serious implications. It adds that medicinal cannabis, prescribed in the UK for a limited number of conditions, varies in legality from country to country. The simplest approach is to leave anything containing CBD or cannabis extracts at home, and to read the ingredients on balms, oils, gummies and vape liquids before they go in your bag.

## Packing your medicines the sensible way

TravelHealthPro's general advice for carrying medicines abroad is a good checklist for any trip, and especially this one. It suggests that you:

- Take medication in its [original pharmacy packaging](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/factsheet/43/medicines-abroad), correctly labelled.
- Carry a copy of your prescription and a letter from your prescriber listing your medicines by generic name.
- Keep medication in your hand luggage, with some extra in the hold in case a bag goes missing.
- Pack enough to cover the whole trip plus possible delays.
- Check airline rules for liquids, as limits apply to what you can carry in hand luggage.
- Check storage needs, and contact the airline in advance if you travel with oxygen, insulin or other injectable medicines.

It also notes that medicines bought abroad may look similar to those at home but contain different amounts or types of active ingredient, so take care if you need to replace something while away.

### Family and group notes

If you are travelling as a family, make one list for the whole group and include children's medicines, inhalers, allergy treatments and anything kept for emergencies. Each person's medicines are best packed and labelled under their own name, with their own prescription copy, so nothing gets muddled at a security check. If an older relative manages their own tablets, a quick check together before departure saves trouble at the airport.

For long days out, such as a desert safari or a mountain drive, carry the day's doses with you rather than leaving them at the hotel. Our [heat safety guide](/stories/uae-heat-safety-guide/) explains why that matters in warmer months, as some medicines affect how your body copes with heat.

## Buying medicines once you are there

The government says most medicines used worldwide are available in community pharmacies and hospitals. What you cannot assume is that a controlled medicine can be replaced quickly. The government portal explains, for example, that in the emirate of Abu Dhabi a narcotic can be [prescribed only by a licensed physician for an in-patient](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/health-and-fitness/Health-and-wellbeing/drugs-and-controlled-medicines), and that private outpatient narcotic supply is not usually allowed. So bring enough of anything important, within the quantity limits, rather than planning to top up locally.

The same page describes Tatmeen, a national track-and-trace platform launched by the Ministry of Health and Prevention, which uses a serialised barcode so consumers can verify medicines at the point of purchase. For fake or harmful medicines, it gives the ministry's hotline, 80011111. TravelHealthPro adds a wider warning about [falsified medicines](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/factsheet/43/medicines-abroad), which can look like the real product, and advises getting the medication you need before travel wherever possible.

In Abu Dhabi, Visit Abu Dhabi says the government portal keeps an [updated list of 24-hour pharmacies](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/faqs) and medical services, and that without internet access you can call the toll-free number 800 555.

## If you fall ill while you are there

Healthcare in the UAE is good, but it is not free for visitors. The Foreign Office advises checking that [you have appropriate travel insurance](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/health) for local treatment and medical evacuation, which matters even more if you have a health condition or are pregnant. It says you may be prevented from leaving the UAE if you cannot pay a medical bill. In an emergency, dial 998 and ask for an ambulance.

Visit Abu Dhabi says that under UAE federal law and Abu Dhabi law, [all visitors must have medical insurance cover](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/faqs). It adds that emergency treatment to stabilise a patient is free, and that other treatment must be covered by a cash payment or an insurance card. It names Sheikh Khalifa Medical City among the Abu Dhabi hospitals with accident and emergency units.

When you get home, TravelHealthPro suggests seeing your regular doctor if you needed extra supplies or started a new medicine while abroad.

## Quick answers

**Can I take painkillers to the UAE?** Many medicines are fine for personal use in normal quantities, but some painkillers sold freely at home are controlled in the UAE. Check each one against the UAE's controlled lists before you fly.

**Do I need approval to bring prescription medicine into the UAE?** For medicines classed as narcotic, psychotropic, controlled or semi-controlled, yes. The Foreign Office says approval must be obtained in advance from the UAE Ministry of Health, and you can apply online.

**How much medication can I bring into the UAE?** The Foreign Office says visitors can bring up to three months' supply, reduced to one month's supply for medicines on the list requiring approval.

**Do I need approval if I am only transiting?** Not if you are changing planes without passing through immigration, according to the Foreign Office. Illegal substances, including CBD products, can still lead to arrest in transit.

**Is CBD legal in the UAE?** Some products containing CBD are illegal, and the Foreign Office warns they will be confiscated and could lead to criminal charges.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Will any day of our itinerary take us far from a pharmacy or clinic, for example in the desert or the mountains?
- Can tour timings allow for medicines that need to be taken with food or at fixed times?
- Is there somewhere cool to store medicines on long excursions or overnight camps?
- Do your guides carry a first aid kit, and what is the plan if someone becomes unwell on a tour?
- Can you help us find a pharmacy or clinic near our hotel if we need one?
