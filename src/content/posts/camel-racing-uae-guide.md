The first thing you hear is not the camels. It is the engines: a line of 4x4s rolling alongside the track, owners leaning out of the windows, radios crackling, all keeping pace with a pack of long-legged animals running far faster than seems possible.

Camel racing is one of the most surprising things you can see in the UAE. It looks nothing like a horse race and nothing like a tourist show. It is a working sport with deep roots in desert life, and visitors are welcome to watch. If you want to see it, the simple answer is to head to a dedicated track early in the morning during the racing season, check that races are on, and give yourself time to take in the paddocks and the people as well as the racing itself.

This guide explains what you will see, where to go, and how to make the most of a morning at the track.

## Why camel racing in the UAE is more than a sport

Before the cars and the cameras, there were camels, and in the desert they meant survival. Racing grew out of that bond.

UNESCO recognised camel racing as intangible cultural heritage in 2020, in a joint listing by the UAE and Oman. Its description calls camel racing [a popular social practice](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/camel-racing-a-social-practice-and-a-festive-heritage-associated-with-camels-01576) in the communities concerned, and a fundamental part of their nomadic lifestyle and a source of inspiration in poetry and singing. It links the sport's importance and continuity in Bedouin society to the prominent role camels play in the desert.

The same entry notes that knowledge passes through the combined efforts of community representatives, government institutions, specialised centres and racing federations and clubs, and that children and young people pick up the skills gradually through observation and oral tradition. When you see families at the track, that is part of the point.

## What happens at a camel race

A first visit can be confusing, because the action moves fast and much of it happens away from the stands. Here is what to look for.

### Preparing the camels

Racing camels are chosen and raised with real care. UNESCO describes how they are [selected based on type, origin and age](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/camel-racing-a-social-practice-and-a-festive-heritage-associated-with-camels-01576), given a special diet, and trained on the track in groups before they take part in races. A preparation committee verifies each camel's origin.

If you arrive early, you may see camels being walked, warmed up or gathered before their round. Watch quietly from a distance and follow any instructions from staff.

### The race itself

According to UNESCO, races take place on specially designed fields under the supervision of specialised community committees. There are usually [between fifteen and twenty camels in each round](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/camel-racing-a-social-practice-and-a-festive-heritage-associated-with-camels-01576), and the racing distance depends on the camels' age.

The camels do not carry human riders. Instead, small robotic jockeys sit on their backs, controlled remotely by owners and trainers who follow in vehicles on a road beside the track. That convoy of cars is the strangest and most memorable part of the scene for many visitors.

### Speed you will not expect

Visit Abu Dhabi puts it well: you will be [pleasantly surprised at the impressive speeds](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/camel-racing) that these seemingly ungainly creatures can reach. Camels look gangly standing still. Running, they are something else entirely.

## Where to watch camel racing near Abu Dhabi and Al Ain

Visit Abu Dhabi points visitors to two tracks:

- **Al Wathba Camel Race Track**, [about 45km east of Abu Dhabi on Al Ain Road](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/camel-racing). It is the easier option if you are staying in the capital.
- **Al Maqam Track near Al Ain**, about an hour and a half from the capital. It pairs well with a day exploring the oasis city; our [Al Ain day trip guide](/stories/al-ain-day-trip/) shows how to build the rest of the day around it.

Dubai also has major racing venues on the edge of the desert, and racing takes place in other emirates too. Wherever you go, check the day's programme locally, because race days vary through the season.

### Timing your visit

The tourism board's advice is simple: [try to get to the racetracks as early as possible](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/camel-racing) to soak up the atmosphere in full. Races generally run in the morning, and the racing calendar follows the cooler part of the year. Confirm that racing is scheduled before you set off, since there is little to see on a quiet day.

## Getting there and what to bring

Both tracks sit outside the city, so plan how you will get there and back.

- **By car.** A hire car gives the most flexibility for an early start. Our guide to [driving in the UAE as a visitor](/stories/driving-in-the-uae-as-a-visitor/) covers the basics.
- **With a guide.** A guided visit removes the guesswork about race days and lets someone explain what you are seeing.
- **By taxi.** Possible, but arrange your return in advance, as taxis may be scarce at the track.

Pack for a morning outdoors in the desert:

- Water, sunglasses, a hat and sunscreen, even in the cooler months.
- A light layer, as early mornings in the desert can feel cool.
- Comfortable, modest clothing. The track is a local, family space, not a beach.
- A zoom lens if you enjoy photography, since the action can be some distance away.

## Photos and etiquette at the track

The racing community is generally welcoming, but this is their sport and their space. Keep a few habits in mind.

- **Ask before photographing people.** The UK Foreign Office advises visitors [not to photograph people without their permission](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security). Photographing the race and the camels from public areas is usually fine; owners, trainers and families are a different matter.
- **Stay out of the way of vehicles.** The road beside the track can be busy and fast during a race. Never step onto it.
- **Keep your distance from camels.** They are large, strong animals. Do not approach or touch them unless invited by their handler.
- **Keep the volume respectful.** Cheering is part of the fun; shouting at animals is not.

If someone offers to explain what is going on, accept. A few minutes with a knowledgeable local can transform the experience.

## Quick answers

**Can tourists watch camel racing in the UAE?**
Yes. Visit Abu Dhabi describes a trip to the races as one of the most memorable and often surprising highlights of a visit, and suggests the Al Wathba and Al Maqam tracks.

**Where is the camel race track near Abu Dhabi?**
Visit Abu Dhabi says Al Wathba Camel Race Track is about 45km east of Abu Dhabi on Al Ain Road, while Al Maqam Track is near Al Ain, about an hour and a half from the capital.

**What time should I arrive?**
Visit Abu Dhabi advises getting to the racetracks as early as possible to soak up the atmosphere. Check locally that races are scheduled on your chosen day.

**Is camel racing part of UAE heritage?**
Yes. UNESCO inscribed camel racing as intangible cultural heritage in 2020, in a joint listing by the UAE and Oman, describing it as a fundamental part of the nomadic lifestyle.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Are races scheduled on the day we plan to go, and how will you confirm it?
- Which track do you recommend from where we are staying, and how early do we need to leave?
- Will a guide explain the racing, the robotic jockeys and the preparation of the camels?
- Where can we stand safely, and are there shaded or seated areas?
- What is the photography etiquette at the track, and who should we ask before taking pictures?
- Can the visit be combined with Al Ain, a falconry display or another nearby sight on the same day?
