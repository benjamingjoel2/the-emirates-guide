The first steps are easy, wide and shallow, and you take them quickly. By the middle of the climb you are slowing down, counting, and the palm gardens below have started to spread out like a green carpet. Then the path turns one last time and the fort's mud-brick walls are right in front of you, glowing the colour of honey.

Climbing to Dhayah Fort is one of the most rewarding short outings in Ras Al Khaimah. It is a single climb up a stepped path, with no ticket desk, no crowds most of the time and a view that takes in mountains, date palms, Oman and the sea. Go in daylight, wear shoes with grip and take water, and almost anyone with reasonable mobility can do it.

## Why Dhayah Fort matters

[Visit Ras Al Khaimah](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/discover/attractions/dhayah-fort/) describes Dhayah Fort as the only hill fort remaining in the United Arab Emirates, and notes that it is on the UNESCO World Heritage Tentative List. The [UAE government portal](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/the-seven-emirates/ras-al-khaimah) also singles it out as the only hilltop fort still in existence in the country.

It sits at the heart of an oasis. The tourism board says the fort stands on a hill about 70 metres high, rising between the date palm gardens and the Jebel Jais mountains, and that the oasis's original watering system, which collected run-off from the mountains after the infrequent rains, has stayed unchanged for centuries.

## A hill with a very long memory

### Bronze Age beginnings

The hill was in use long before the fort you see. According to [Visit Ras Al Khaimah](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/discover/attractions/dhayah-fort/), the site dates back to the Late Bronze Age, roughly 1600 to 1300 BC, when people used it for settlement and defence.

At the foot of the hill, the tourism board says, lie 12 large tombs from the Wadi Suq period, between about 2000 and 1300 BC. Four have been excavated so far, and the Ras Al Khaimah Department of Antiquities and Museums intends to turn the area into an archaeology park.

This corner of the emirate is rich in such sites. The tourism board's [history pages](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/about-ras-al-khaimah/history-culture/) describe Wadi Suq graves at Shamal, which it calls the largest prehistoric cemetery there, and at Al Rams, Ghaleelah and Khatt, with finds displayed in the National Museum. They also mention a Late Bronze Age settlement at Shamal whose people relied on shellfish, fish, dates and farming, which gives you a sense of how long people have lived from this same mix of sea and palm garden.

### The fort and the battle of 1819

The twin-peaked mud-brick fort itself, the same page explains, was built in the 19th century and restored in the late 1990s. The [UAE government portal](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/the-seven-emirates/ras-al-khaimah) dates it to the 18th century, so you will see both dates quoted. What is not in doubt is that it was the scene of the 1819 battle between British troops and the local Qawasim tribes.

That battle had consequences far beyond this hill. The tourism board's page on [Al Jazeera Al Hamra](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/discover/attractions/al-jazeera-al-hamra/) notes that the treaty of 1820 between the British and the Trucial States followed the battle at Dhayah. The tourism board's [guide to historic places](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/blog/insider-tips-on-the-best-historic-places-in-ras-al-khaimah/) adds that the peace treaty was signed in the summer of 1820 at Falayah, an 18th-century summer residence of the ruling Qawasim family surrounded by date gardens. The [National Museum of Ras Al Khaimah](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/discover/attractions/national-museum-of-ras-al-khaimah/) has galleries on the Qawasim and on the British attack of 1820, so it makes a good companion visit.

### Who the Qawasim were

To understand why British troops came to this hill at all, it helps to know who was defending it. The tourism board's [history pages](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/about-ras-al-khaimah/history-culture/) say the Qawasim clan united much of the northern emirates and built one of the strongest fleets in the region, and that this coast was occupied or challenged over the centuries by Sasanians, the Portuguese, the Dutch and the British. The [National Museum](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/discover/attractions/national-museum-of-ras-al-khaimah/), itself housed in the old Qawasim fort, says that building was destroyed at least twice, by the Portuguese in 1621 and by the British in 1820. Dhayah was one stronghold in a long contest for control of the sea lanes at the mouth of the Gulf.

### The fort at the bottom

Do not miss what is below you. At the base of the hill stands a slightly larger fort, which [Visit Ras Al Khaimah](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/discover/attractions/dhayah-fort/) says was used by the people who lived and worked in the palm gardens, who would retreat inside with their animals in times of danger. Single watchtowers dotted through the oasis kept communication going between them.

## The climb to Dhayah Fort, step by step

### What the path is like

<photo-set layout="inset-left" keys="dhayah-fort-guide-2" alts="Dhayah Fort's mud-brick walls and towers seen from below on the rocky hillside" caption="The fort seen from the slope below."></photo-set>

The tourism board puts the climb at 239 zigzagging steps. It describes the way up as uneven and rocky, and says it is not accessible to prams, wheelchairs or other mobility aids. There is no lighting.

Take it steadily. The steps vary in height, some are worn smooth, and there are few places to sit. Children should be kept close, and anyone unsteady on their feet may prefer to enjoy the lower fort and the oasis from the ground. Most reasonably fit visitors will reach the top without trouble, but in warm weather even a short exposed climb is tiring, so pause on the turns and drink as you go.

### At the top

The reward is the view. The tourism board describes it running from the mountains across palm trees and green land to neighbouring Oman and down to the sea. Walk slowly around the fort walls and you can pick out the patchwork of gardens, the scattered towers and the mountains rising sharply behind.

Look at the walls themselves, too. The mud brick has been patched and smoothed by restoration, but the shapes are old: thick walls, narrow openings and a layout built for watching the plain. Treat the fort with care. It is a restored historic monument, so stay off the walls and leave everything as you found it.

## How a visit unfolds

Do not rush it. Allow time for the climb, the top and a slow look at the lower fort. Arrive, take a few minutes at the bottom to see the larger fort and get your bearings, then start up. At the top, give yourself time to sit and look rather than photograph and leave: the view changes as the light moves across the gardens.

On the way down, go more slowly than on the way up. Worn steps are easier to slip on in descent, especially in the last of the afternoon light. Then walk a little way along the edge of the gardens to see the watchtowers and the green of the palms from ground level.

## When to go and what to bring

Because there is no lighting, the tourism board advises [visiting in daylight](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/discover/attractions/dhayah-fort/). Early morning is our pick: the steps are cool, and low sun warms the mud brick. Late afternoon works too, as long as you leave time to come down before dusk.

The season matters. Visit Ras Al Khaimah says the best time to visit the emirate is [October to April](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/faqs/), and that summer can be extremely hot, with temperatures that can exceed 45°C, especially inland. On an exposed staircase with little shade, that heat is felt quickly.

Bring:

- Water, more than you think you need.
- Shoes with good grip, not sandals.
- A hat and sunglasses.
- Modest clothing. The tourism board says visitors are [expected to dress modestly](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/plan-your-trip/cultural-awareness/) out of respect for local culture, and Dhayah is a lived-in village as well as a heritage site.
- A charged phone. In an emergency, the tourism board lists [999 for the police and 998 for an ambulance](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/location/place/wadi-shawka/).

## Getting there

Dhayah is in the north of the emirate, where the date gardens meet the foot of the mountains. Visit Ras Al Khaimah says getting around the emirate is [easiest by car](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/faqs/), that taxis are metered and that ride-hailing apps work here, so a hire car, a driver for the day or a taxi all make sense. If you take a taxi, agree how the driver will wait or return, because this is not a spot where you can expect to flag one down. The tourism board lists no visitor facilities at the fort, so do not count on toilets, shops or shade there. Use the facilities before you set out, and carry everything you need for the climb.

## Respecting a lived-in village

Dhayah is not a museum enclosure. The gardens around the fort are worked, and people live nearby. Park considerately, keep voices down, do not walk into private gardens and do not climb walls or irrigation channels. The UK Foreign Office reminds visitors [not to photograph people without their permission](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), and that applies to residents going about their day as much as anywhere else.

### Photography

The fort photographs best in low, warm light, when the mud brick turns gold and the palms below are in shade. From the top, a wide view takes in the gardens and the mountains; from the bottom, the hill and fort make a strong silhouette against the sky. Midday light is harsh and flat here, and the walls lose their colour, which is one more reason to come early or late. A phone camera is plenty; you will want both hands free on the steps anyway. ## Families and accessibility

Older children usually enjoy the climb, because it is short, has a clear goal and ends with a fort to explore. Hold younger ones' hands on the steeper sections and at the top, where the ground is uneven. The tourism board is clear that the path is not accessible to prams, wheelchairs or other mobility aids, so families with small babies should use a carrier, and anyone who cannot manage steps can still see the lower fort and the gardens from the base.

## What catches people out

- **Leaving it too late.** With no lighting, a sunset visit means a descent in fading light. Start up with plenty of time.
- **Underestimating the heat.** It is a short climb, but it is exposed. Outside the cooler months, go early.
- **Footwear.** Sandals and smooth soles slip on worn steps.
- **Missing the lower fort.** It is easy to rush past on the way up. Look at it first.

## Putting Dhayah Fort into a day

Dhayah sits in the north of the emirate, between the coast and the mountains, so it fits neatly with other sights. A good loop is the Jebel Jais road in the morning, lunch back on the coast, and Dhayah Fort in the late afternoon. Our [Jebel Jais guide](/stories/jebel-jais-guide/) explains the drive.

For a heritage-heavy day, pair it with the [National Museum](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/discover/attractions/national-museum-of-ras-al-khaimah/) in the old town and the empty coral-stone houses of [Al Jazeera Al Hamra](/stories/al-jazirat-al-hamra-guide/). The [history pages of Visit Ras Al Khaimah](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/about-ras-al-khaimah/history-culture/) note that Wadi Suq period graves have been found at several other sites in the emirate, and that finds from them are displayed in the National Museum.

Nearby Al Rams, the emirate's northernmost neighbourhood, makes another good pairing. The tourism board lists a [working pearl farm](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/discover/activities/suwaidi-pearls/) there, with boat tours through the mangroves that it says usually last about 2.5 hours and should be booked ahead. Just beyond, [Al Rams Beach](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/discover/attractions/al-rams-beach/) and Saraya Island have shallow, calm water, though the tourism board says you need a four-wheel drive, or a guide to drive you, to reach the island.

## Quick answers

**How many steps are there to Dhayah Fort?** Visit Ras Al Khaimah says the climb is 239 zigzagging steps.

**Is Dhayah Fort suitable for wheelchairs or prams?** No. The tourism board says the uneven, rocky climb is not accessible to prams, wheelchairs or other mobility aids.

**Can I visit Dhayah Fort at night?** It is not advisable. There is no lighting, and the tourism board recommends visiting in daylight.

**How old is Dhayah Fort?** The tourism board says the fort was built in the 19th century and restored in the late 1990s, while the UAE government portal dates it to the 18th century. The site itself goes back to the Late Bronze Age.

**Why is Dhayah Fort important?** It is the only hill fort remaining in the UAE, the site of the 1819 battle between British troops and the Qawasim, and it is on the UNESCO World Heritage Tentative List.

## Questions to ask your operator

- What time would you suggest for the climb on our date, so we have shade and soft light?
- Does your guide know the history of the 1819 battle and the Bronze Age tombs at the foot of the hill?
- Is the climb realistic for older travellers or children in our group, and what can they do if they stay below?
- Where is the nearest place to park, and are there toilets or shops nearby?
- Can we combine Dhayah Fort with the National Museum or Al Jazeera Al Hamra on the same day?
