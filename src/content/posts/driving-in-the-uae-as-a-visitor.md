The highway opens up as you leave the city: six lanes, smooth tarmac, gantries overhead, and a line of towers shrinking in the mirror. An hour later the road starts to climb, the rock turns red and you are winding up into the mountains with nobody else in sight.

That freedom is the case for hiring a car. The east coast, the mountain roads and the quieter emirates are all easier with your own wheels. Driving in the UAE as a visitor also comes with its own rules, its own habits on the road and a few laws that are much stricter than many visitors expect. Here is what to check before you pick up the keys.

## Driving in the UAE as a visitor: can you use your licence?

It depends on where your licence was issued, so check before you book a car.

For UK licence holders, the UK Foreign Office says you can use a [UK photocard driving licence](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) to drive in the UAE. If you still have an old paper licence, it says you may need to update it to a photocard licence or get the correct version of the international driving permit (IDP) as well. The UK government's IDP page lists the UAE under the [1968 convention](https://www.gov.uk/driving-abroad/international-driving-permit), and also advises checking whether your car hire company says you need an IDP.

For other nationalities, the rules vary. The UAE government says some visitors have the right to [drive with their national driving licence](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/transportation/Driving-and-licensing/get-a-driving-licence), and may exchange it for a UAE licence if they become residents, under the Markhoos initiative. Check with your own licensing authority and your hire company before you travel, and carry your licence, passport and any permit with you when you drive.

### Picking up a hire car

A little preparation makes the handover smoother. The UK government's driving abroad guidance says your hire company may ask to see your driving licence information when you collect the car, and that UK licence holders can share it by getting a [licence check code](https://www.gov.uk/driving-abroad) up to 21 days before the trip. It also notes that insurance is included when you hire a car, but that you should check what you are covered for with the hire company.

Before you drive away, walk round the car and photograph any existing damage, check the air conditioning works properly, and ask how tolls, fines and fuel are handled. Make sure you know the hire company's breakdown and accident number, and save it in your phone.

If you are moving to the UAE rather than visiting, the Foreign Office says a UK licence can be used [until your residence permit is issued](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), after which you need a UAE licence.

## The basics of the road

Some things will feel familiar, others less so.

- **Side of the road.** The UAE government's fact sheet confirms that motorists [drive on the right](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/fact-sheet).
- **Signs.** The same page says all road and traffic signs are in [Arabic and English](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/fact-sheet).
- **Speed limits.** They are clearly posted, and the Foreign Office notes the [widespread use of speed cameras](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security). Limits can change between stretches of the same road, so keep watching the signs.
- **Local rules apply to you.** The UK government's general advice on driving abroad is to [follow local speed limits and drink driving laws](https://www.gov.uk/driving-abroad), because you may otherwise be breaking the law.

### The main highways

The [fact sheet](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/fact-sheet) describes the key routes. The E11 is the longest road in the country, running from Al Silah in Abu Dhabi to the boundary of Ras Al Khaimah, and is known locally as Sheikh Zayed Road in Dubai. The E311, now Sheikh Mohammed bin Zayed Road, links Dubai to the other emirates, and the Sheikh Khalifa highway links Dubai and Fujairah. Our [UAE road trip planning guide](/stories/uae-road-trip-planning/) suggests how to string them together.

### Tolls

Both Abu Dhabi and Dubai have road tolls. According to the [fact sheet](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/fact-sheet), Abu Dhabi charges vehicles passing toll gates during morning and evening peak hours from Monday to Saturday, and Dubai charges a toll, called salik, at its toll gates. Ask your hire company how tolls on your rental are charged and passed on to you.

## The laws visitors most often underestimate

UAE traffic law is strict, and the consequences can be serious. The country applies [Federal Decree-Law No. 14 of 2024 on traffic regulation](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/justice-safety-and-the-law/Safety/road-safety), which the UAE government says came into effect on 29 March 2025.

### Alcohol

The Foreign Office says it is [illegal to drink and drive, no matter how small the amount](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), and that your insurance is likely to be invalidated if you have an accident. The only safe amount is none.

### Gestures and language

Road rage has real consequences here. The Foreign Office warns that [offensive gestures and bad language](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) directed at other drivers can lead to fines, a jail sentence and possibly deportation. Whatever happens on the road, keep your hands on the wheel and your thoughts to yourself.

### Rules of the road in the new traffic law

The UAE government's summary of the federal traffic law lists several rules that apply to every driver and pedestrian. Among them, it says that [parking, stopping or repairing vehicles](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/justice-safety-and-the-law/Safety/road-safety) on roads or sidewalks is prohibited, that vehicles causing excessive noise are not permitted, and that the use of vehicle horns within city limits is restricted to emergencies. The same page lists driving under the influence of alcohol or drugs, driving with a suspended licence and fleeing the scene of an accident among the serious violations that carry strict penalties.

### Impounding

The same page says police have the power to [impound your car](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) for traffic violations that may be considered minor in the UK, with a substantial fee and a fine to get it back.

### Fines

Traffic fines can be checked and paid online, and the UAE government lists the [official channels](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/justice-safety-and-the-law/Safety/road-safety), including the Ministry of Interior app and police and transport authority platforms. If you are driving a hire car, ask the rental company how they handle any fines.

## How people drive

The Foreign Office is frank that [driving standards are not always as disciplined](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) as in the UK, that there is a high rate of traffic accidents, and that speeding is common despite the cameras. It also notes that flashing headlights can mean a driver is coming through, not that they are letting you go.

A few habits make life easier:

- Stay out of the far left lane unless you are overtaking.
- Leave a generous gap and expect sudden lane changes.
- Check your mirrors often for fast cars coming up behind.
- Avoid driving tired, particularly after a long flight.

During Ramadan, the Foreign Office says [driving may be erratic around dusk](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) as people hurry home to break their fast, and the UAE government notes that [paid parking hours change](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/public-holidays-and-religious-affairs/ramadan) during the month, with details on the parking meters.

## Pedestrians, crossings and city streets

Even if you never drive, you will cross roads, and the rules are stricter than many visitors expect. The Foreign Office says you should only cross at [designated pedestrian crossings](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), that crossing elsewhere is illegal and can lead to a fine or even imprisonment, and that vehicles often do not stop at zebra crossings marked on the roads. The UAE government adds that pedestrians are [prohibited from crossing roads](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/justice-safety-and-the-law/Safety/road-safety) where the speed limit exceeds 80 km/h.

As a driver, the flip side is simple: expect people on foot near malls, souks and bus stops, especially in the evening, and slow down near crossings even when nobody seems to be waiting.

## If you have an accident

Stay calm and stay at the scene. The Foreign Office says all drivers involved in an incident must stop as soon as it is safe, and that it is [illegal to leave the scene](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) before the police arrive. It also notes that the rules on moving your car differ between Abu Dhabi, Dubai and the other emirates.

The UAE government's advice is to [call the police on 999](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/justice-safety-and-the-law/Safety/handling-emergencies), give your location and wait for instructions. If the accident is minor and no one is hurt, you can move your car to the shoulder lane and wait for the police at a distance from the car. If anyone is injured, leave the vehicles where they are and wait for an ambulance. Minor accidents can also be [reported through official apps](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/justice-safety-and-the-law/Safety/road-safety), including the Ministry of Interior, Abu Dhabi Police and Dubai Police apps.

### The rules differ between emirates

The Foreign Office sets out how the rules on moving your car vary. It says that in Abu Dhabi, if no one is hurt and the damage is minor, you should [move your vehicle to the side of the road](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), and otherwise not move it; in Dubai, only move it if it is causing an obstruction; and in the other emirates, only move it if the accident is minor and both parties agree who is responsible. In all cases, call the police.

The UAE government also describes an emergency call system, known as eCall, launched with Abu Dhabi and Dubai Police, that [automatically calls the police](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/justice-safety-and-the-law/Safety/road-safety) after a collision in equipped vehicles and opens a two-way voice link to the nearest emergency centre. If your car has an emergency call button, ask the hire company how it works.

Call your hire company as soon as it is safe, and keep every document the police give you.

## Desert and mountain driving

Do not take a normal hire car onto the sand. The Foreign Office warns that desert excursions can be dangerous unless you are in a [properly equipped four-wheel-drive vehicle](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), and advises travelling with other cars, taking water and a phone, and leaving your plans with someone. For most visitors, a guided trip is the better choice; our guide to [whether a desert safari is worth it](/stories/is-a-desert-safari-worth-it/) explains the options.

Mountain roads to places like Jebel Jais and Hatta are paved but steep and winding. Use lower gears on the descent, take breaks, and check the weather first, since heavy rain can affect these routes. Our [rain and flooding advice](/stories/uae-rain-and-flooding-advice/) covers what to do in a storm, and our [Jebel Jais guide](/stories/jebel-jais-guide/) has more on the road to the top.

On long rural stretches, plan your fuel and rest stops before you set off rather than counting on the next town, and keep water in the car. Watch for slow vehicles on climbs, and for animals near roads that cross open desert, particularly around dawn and dusk.

## How a self-drive day usually goes

A typical day out from the city starts early. Leave before the morning rush if you can, and the first hour on the highway will be the easiest of the day. Stop for breakfast and fuel once you are clear of the city, then aim to reach the mountains, forts or beaches while it is still cool. Lunch is a good time to rest out of the sun, and the drive back is best timed to avoid both the evening commute and driving into a low sun.

A few small things make the day easier:

- Keep sunglasses, water and a phone charger within reach.
- Download offline maps in case signal drops in the mountains.
- Avoid eating or drinking at the wheel during Ramadan daylight hours, which the Foreign Office notes may cause offence [even in your car](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security).
- Never leave children or anyone else waiting in a parked car, even briefly; the heat builds very quickly.
- If you are travelling with young children, ask the hire company for suitable child seats when you book.

## What catches visitors out

- **Speed limits that change.** Limits can differ between stretches of the same road, and cameras are widespread.
- **Fast traffic in the left lane.** Keep right unless you are overtaking.
- **Gestures and horn use.** A moment of road rage can have legal consequences.
- **Moving the car after a bump.** The rules vary between emirates, so check before you move it.
- **Fines arriving later.** Tolls and fines on a hire car may be passed on after you return it.

## Not sure you want to drive?

Plenty of visitors never get behind the wheel. In the cities, metro, taxis and ride apps cover most needs, and a private driver can handle longer days out. If you use taxis, the Foreign Office advises using only [licensed taxis or recognised transport apps](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), and notes that authorised taxis in Dubai are cream with different coloured roofs, while those in Abu Dhabi are silver or grey. Our guide to [getting around Dubai](/stories/getting-around-dubai/) explains the public transport options, and our [travel tips](/travel-tips/) section has more practical planning help.

## Quick answers

**Can I drive in the UAE with a UK licence?**
The UK Foreign Office says you can use a UK photocard licence. If you have a paper licence, you may need to update it or get the correct international driving permit.

**Which side of the road do they drive on in the UAE?**
On the right, according to the UAE government fact sheet. Road signs are in Arabic and English.

**Is there any alcohol limit for driving in the UAE?**
No. The Foreign Office says it is illegal to drink and drive, no matter how small the amount.

**What should I do after a car accident in the UAE?**
Stop, call the police on 999 and wait for instructions. It is illegal to leave the scene before the police arrive.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Would a self-drive car or a private driver suit our itinerary better, given the distances and roads involved?
- Does your driver or vehicle hold the right licences and insurance for intercity, mountain and desert routes?
- For desert trips, are the vehicles properly equipped four-wheel drives, and do they travel in convoy?
- How are tolls, parking and any traffic fines handled if you arrange a vehicle for us?
- Which routes on our plan are best avoided in heavy rain, fog or at peak commuting times?
