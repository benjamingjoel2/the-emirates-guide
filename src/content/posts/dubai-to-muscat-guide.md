The towers of Dubai fall away in the rear-view mirror, the sand turns redder, and then the Hajar Mountains start to rise ahead, dark and jagged against a pale sky. Somewhere among them is a border post, and on the other side a capital that feels a world away from the one you left that morning.

Getting from Dubai to Muscat is one of the most rewarding moves you can make on a Gulf trip, and also one of the most confusing to plan. You can fly, drive or take a bus, and each option has its own rules at the border.

The short version: flying is the simplest and quickest; driving gives you the mountains and the freedom to stop, but only works if your hire car is allowed into Oman; and a bus or guided overland trip sits in between. Whichever you choose, check Oman's entry rules for your passport first. Here is how each option works, how the day on the road actually unfolds, and what to do once you arrive.

## Which option suits you?

Before the detail, a quick way to narrow it down.

- **Short trip, small children or tight schedule:** fly. It is the least tiring way to make the move, and there is no land border.
- **You love driving and want the mountains:** drive, but only once your rental company has confirmed in writing that the car and its insurance can cross.
- **Solo, flexible and happy to sit back:** take the intercity bus and keep your passport on you.
- **You want the scenery without the paperwork:** book a private transfer or guided overland trip.
- **A longer trip with time to spare:** combine them. Drive or take the bus one way and fly the other, so you see the mountains once without doing the long road twice.

## Before you choose: Oman's entry rules

Every route to Muscat ends at an Omani immigration desk, so start here.

Rules depend on your nationality. For British citizens, the UK Foreign Office says you can [visit Oman for up to 14 days without a visa](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/oman/entry-requirements) and that your passport must be valid for at least six months after you arrive. To stay longer, you need a visa, and the Foreign Office suggests applying online before you travel, choosing the "unsponsored visit visa" option as a tourist.

It also says that at Omani border control you must be able to show:

- a ticket for return or onward travel
- proof of accommodation, such as a hotel booking
- proof of your travel health insurance

The UAE government portal adds a reminder that works in both directions: all visitors arriving by land need [the correct exit permit from the country of origin and a UAE entry permit or visa](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/visiting-and-exploring-the-uae/Arrival-and-accommodation/how-to-get-to-the-uae), so make sure your paperwork covers the return as well as the outward leg.

Print copies or keep them offline on your phone, because signal can be patchy at a mountain border. Medicines need care too: the Foreign Office notes that some UK prescription and over-the-counter medicines are [illegal in Oman](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/oman/entry-requirements), and advises carrying a copy of your prescription and checking with Oman's Ministry of Health well before you travel. Our guide to [bringing medicines into the UAE](/stories/bringing-medicines-into-uae/) covers the UAE side.

## Dubai to Muscat by air

If you have a short trip or are travelling with small children, flying is usually the easiest choice. Flights between Dubai and Muscat are short, and you avoid the long drive and the land border altogether.

Flying also lets you build an open-jaw trip: fly into Dubai, travel overland or fly to Muscat, and fly home from there. That saves backtracking and works well with our guide to [combining the UAE and Oman in one trip](/stories/uae-and-oman-one-trip/).

Do bear in mind the wider picture. The Foreign Office warns that people travelling in the region should be [prepared for possible flight cancellations, airspace closures and travel disruptions](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/oman/warnings-and-insurance) because of regional tensions. Check the latest advice before booking, and again before you fly.

## Dubai to Muscat by road

### The route and the crossing

The drive takes you east out of Dubai towards the Hajar Mountains, through a land border and on through northern Oman to the capital. The crossing most travellers use on the Dubai to Muscat road is at Hatta on the UAE side and Al Wajajah on the Omani side, but confirm before you go that it is open to you. The UAE government portal says there are [several entry points along the border with Oman](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/visiting-and-exploring-the-uae/Arrival-and-accommodation/how-to-get-to-the-uae), and that some are reserved for GCC citizens.

Leave plenty of time. Border queues vary, and the formalities on both sides can take longer than you expect at busy times, such as public holidays. Hatta itself makes a good stop on the way; see our [Hatta guide](/stories/hatta-guide/).

### A stop at Hatta Heritage Village

<photo-set layout="inset-right" keys="dubai-to-muscat-guide-4" alts="The welcome sign at the entrance to Hatta Heritage Village, in Arabic and English, beneath date palms" caption="Hatta Heritage Village, restored with traditional materials, is an easy break before the border."></photo-set>

If you want to stretch your legs before the border, Hatta Heritage Village is the obvious stop. Dubai Municipality says the village lies [115 kilometres southeast of Dubai](https://www.dm.gov.ae/historic_item/hatta-heritage-village/) and contains about 30 buildings restored with the same materials used in the past, such as clay and straw. Among them are an old mosque and a fort built in 1896 by Sheikh Maktoum bin Hasher Al Maktoum, with a tower that now displays examples of firearms once used in the area. Two towers built in 1888 overlook the village from the surrounding hilltops. There is also a falaj, a channel that carries water down from the mountains to the palms.

An hour here breaks the drive nicely and gives children something to explore. Keep it relaxed: the border is the part of the day that is hardest to predict, so leave plenty of slack after your stop.

### How the day on the road unfolds

The first stretch out of Dubai is fast, flat motorway. The landscape changes as you approach the mountains, and the road begins to climb and curve. At the border you pass two sets of formalities, leaving the UAE and entering Oman, and you may be asked for every document on your list, so keep them in one folder within reach rather than scattered through your bags.

Beyond the border the road continues through northern Oman towards the coast and the capital. Fill up with fuel, buy water and take a proper break before the long final stretch, and aim to arrive in Muscat in daylight if you can. Driving into an unfamiliar city after dark, tired from a long day, is when mistakes happen.

### Can your hire car cross into Oman?

This is the question that sinks most self-drive plans. Do not assume a car hired in Dubai can be taken across the border. Ask the rental company, before you book:

- whether cross-border travel into Oman is allowed
- whether the insurance is valid in Oman
- what paperwork you need to carry at the border

Get the answers in writing. The Foreign Office lists [uninsured vehicles](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/oman/safety-and-security) among the situations that count as a serious accident in Oman, where you must stay with the vehicle and call the Royal Oman Police on 9999.

### Licences

On the UAE side, the Foreign Office says you can use a [UK photocard driving licence](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) and that holders of a paper licence may need to update it or get an international driving permit (IDP). In Oman, it says a UK photocard licence can be used to drive a rental car [for the first 6 weeks after you arrive](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/oman/safety-and-security), and notes that hire companies often have stricter rules, such as a higher minimum age, a year of driving experience or holding an IDP. If you are driving back into the UAE from Oman, the UAE government portal says visitors driving into the UAE need [an international driving permit and valid vehicle insurance](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/visiting-and-exploring-the-uae/Arrival-and-accommodation/how-to-get-to-the-uae). For more, see [driving in the UAE as a visitor](/stories/driving-in-the-uae-as-a-visitor/).

### On the road in Oman

The Foreign Office describes Omani roads as generally good but warns that [driving can be dangerous outside Muscat](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/oman/safety-and-security), with a risk of hitting wandering camels and goats. It says rain can cause sudden, severe floods in dry riverbeds and on roads that cross them, and that wadis are prone to flash floods after heavy rain from October to March. Among the rules of the road it lists:

- all passengers must wear seatbelts
- children aged 3 and under must use child car seats
- using a mobile phone while driving is illegal

It adds that drink-driving is a serious offence and that any alcohol in your system can bring a fine and possible imprisonment. Plan the drive in daylight, carry water and take breaks.

## Dubai to Muscat by bus

If you would rather not drive, intercity buses run between the two cities. The UAE government portal notes that you can arrive in the UAE by [buses operated by national transportation companies](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/visiting-and-exploring-the-uae/Arrival-and-accommodation/how-to-get-to-the-uae). The bus stops at the border while passengers go through immigration, so keep your passport and documents with you rather than in the hold.

A bus is a good fit for solo travellers and anyone on a relaxed timetable. It is less suited to families with young children or anyone short of time, since the border stop adds to the journey.

## A guided overland trip

A private transfer or guided tour sits between driving and the bus. Someone else handles the car, the border and the route, and you can stop for photos in the mountains. It also solves the hire car problem neatly. Ask whether the vehicle and driver are permitted to cross, and who handles the paperwork at the border.

## Coming back into the UAE

Remember the return leg. For British citizens, the Foreign Office says a visitor visa is [issued free on arrival in the UAE](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/entry-requirements) and is valid for up to 90 days within a 180-day period, counted from your first entry. Time in Oman does not reset that clock, so keep track of your days if you are on a long trip. Other nationalities should check their own rules; our [UAE tourist visa guide](/stories/uae-tourist-visa-guide/) is a good starting point.

Oman's own tourism site notes that entry procedures [differ based on the visitor's country of origin](https://experienceoman.om/visa) and recommends getting a visa before travel where needed, rather than relying on arrival.

## What to see when you arrive

Once you reach Muscat, the slower pace is the reward. Oman's tourism site lists the [Sultan Qaboos Grand Mosque, Mutrah Souq, the National Museum and the Royal Opera House](https://experienceoman.om/destinations/muscat) among the capital's highlights, along with forts overlooking the bay and seafront promenades. The same site describes the National Museum as housed in 14 galleries telling Oman's own story, and Al Alam Palace, the ceremonial palace, as known for its blue and gold facade. Find more route ideas on our [itineraries page](/itineraries/).

### Visiting the Sultan Qaboos Grand Mosque

If you only plan one visit on your first morning, make it the Grand Mosque, and plan around its hours. Oman's tourism site says non-Muslim visitors are welcome [from Saturday to Thursday between 8 and 11am](https://experienceoman.om/destinations/muscat/sultan-qaboos-grand-mosque), as long as they dress modestly. It asks visitors to cover shoulders and knees, and women to cover their heads; abayas and headscarves are available at the entrance if you need them. Shoes come off before the prayer hall, and visitors are asked to keep their voices low and avoid blocking worshippers. The site lists the mosque as wheelchair accessible.

That narrow morning window is the reason to arrive the day before rather than on the morning itself. A late arrival after a long drive, followed by an early start, is far easier than trying to fit the mosque around a border crossing.

### Mutrah Souq and the Corniche

Later in the day, head for Mutrah. Oman's tourism site describes Mutrah Souq as [a traditional Arab market with a maze of alleys](https://experienceoman.om/destinations/muscat) filled with shops, and gives the best time to visit the souq as [September to May](https://experienceoman.om/destinations/muscat/mutrah-souq). The seafront promenade outside is the place to watch the evening light on the harbour.

## Timing your trip

The cooler months are the easiest for both the drive and sightseeing, and Oman's tourism site gives the best time to visit the Grand Mosque as [December to March](https://experienceoman.om/destinations/muscat/sultan-qaboos-grand-mosque). Weather can still affect the road. The Foreign Office says tropical cyclones from the Indian Ocean [sometimes affect Oman in July and August](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/oman/safety-and-security), and that extreme heat has [caused deaths in Oman due to dehydration and heat exhaustion](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/oman/health). Carry more water than you think you need, and do not set off on a long drive in a car with weak air conditioning.

Ramadan changes the rhythm of the day too. The Foreign Office advises against [eating, drinking or smoking in public in the daytime](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/oman/safety-and-security) in Oman during the holy month, including in your car, so plan meal stops accordingly. Our guide to [visiting the UAE during Ramadan](/stories/visiting-uae-during-ramadan/) explains how the month feels on the UAE side.

## What catches people out

- **The hire car.** Assuming a Dubai rental can cross is the most common mistake on this route.
- **Documents in the boot.** At the border, and on the bus, you need your passport and papers to hand.
- **Visa day counts.** Time spent in Oman does not reset your UAE visitor allowance.
- **Unpaid fines.** The Foreign Office says foreign nationals must [pay all debts and traffic fines before leaving Oman](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/oman/safety-and-security), so settle any you pick up before you head back.
- **Night driving.** Wandering animals and unfamiliar roads make the last stretch harder after dark.

## Getting help on the way

Save the emergency numbers for both countries before you set off. In Oman, the Foreign Office lists [9999 for ambulance, fire and police](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/oman/getting-help). In the UAE, it lists [999 for police and 998 for an ambulance](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/getting-help). It also says British nationals must use private healthcare in Oman, and that visitors without insurance or the means to pay may be stopped from leaving until the bill is settled, so check that your policy covers both countries.

## Quick answers

**What is the easiest way to get from Dubai to Muscat?**
Flying is the simplest and avoids the land border. Check the Foreign Office's latest regional advice on possible flight disruption before you book.

**Do British citizens need a visa for Oman?**
Not for short stays. The Foreign Office says British citizens can visit for up to 14 days without a visa, but must show onward travel, accommodation and travel health insurance at the border.

**Can I drive a Dubai hire car to Muscat?**
Only if the rental company allows it and the insurance is valid in Oman. Get both confirmed in writing before you book.

**Can I use my UK licence in Oman?**
The Foreign Office says a UK photocard licence can be used to drive a rental car in Oman for the first 6 weeks after you arrive, though hire companies may ask for more.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Which border crossing will we use, and is it open to every passport in our group?
- What documents should each of us carry for Omani entry and for coming back into the UAE?
- Is your vehicle and driver permitted to cross into Oman, and who handles paperwork at the border?
- How long do you allow for the border at busy times, such as weekends and public holidays?
- What happens if the border is closed, roads flood or flights are disrupted on our travel day?
- Can you split the journey with a stop in Hatta or the mountains on the way?
