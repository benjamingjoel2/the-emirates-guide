The quad bike crests the dune and, for half a second, there is nothing in front of you but sky. Then the nose tips down, the sand rushes up, and you discover exactly how steep the other side is. Whether that moment ends in a whoop or a tumble depends almost entirely on decisions made before you set off.

Sandboarding, quad biking and dune buggy rides are some of the most popular add-ons to a UAE desert trip, and they can be brilliant fun. They are also where many desert injuries happen. Staying safe on the dunes comes down to three things: choosing a reputable operator, riding within your ability, and respecting the heat. This guide covers how each activity works and what to check before you climb on.

## Why dune sports need more care than they look

Sand looks soft. It is not always. Dunes have a gentle windward side and a steep slip face on the other, and from the top of a dune you often cannot see how sharp the drop is. Wind reshapes them constantly, so yesterday's safe line may not exist today. Add heat, glare, loose sand and other vehicles, and the desert becomes a place where small mistakes add up.

The UK Foreign Office is clear on this. It warns that [desert excursions can be dangerous](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) unless you are in a properly equipped 4-wheel-drive vehicle, and says that if you join a tour for desert driving or quad biking, you should make sure you go with a reputable company with adequate health and safety procedures, and always check that the activity is covered by your insurance.

### Reading a dune

A little sand sense goes a long way. The windward side of a dune, facing into the prevailing wind, is usually long, firm and gently sloped. The slip face on the lee side is steeper and softer, and the sand there can give way under a wheel or a board. The sharp edge where the two meet is the crest, and it is the most dangerous place on any dune, because from the approach you cannot see what lies beyond it.

Light makes a difference too. In the middle of the day, flat overhead sun removes shadows and makes slopes look gentler than they are. Early and late in the day, low light picks out the ridges and hollows, which is one more reason to ride at those times. If the sand looks featureless, slow down: that is when drops and holes are hardest to judge.

## Dune sports safety, activity by activity

### Sandboarding

Sandboarding is the gentlest of the three and the one most often included in standard safari packages. The Dubai Desert Conservation Reserve lists [sandboarding](https://www.ddcr.org/what-to-do/) among its activities, describing riders strapping on a board and heading down to the valley floor.

How to do it well:

- **Start on a short, gentle slope.** Learn to stop before you try anything steep.
- **Sit before you stand.** Riding down seated or on your front is perfectly respectable and much safer for beginners.
- **Check the run-out.** Make sure the bottom of the slope is clear of people, vehicles and bushes.
- **Keep your mouth closed and wear sunglasses.** Sand goes everywhere.
- **Climb back up slowly.** Walking up a dune in heat is harder work than it looks.

Most sandboarding injuries come from going head first, from trying to stand on a slope that is too steep, or from colliding with someone at the bottom. Wait until the person ahead has cleared the run-out before you set off, and if a vehicle is anywhere near the base of the slope, stop until it has gone. Boards are often waxed to help them slide; if yours barely moves, ask the guide rather than launching yourself off a steeper face to compensate.

### Quad bikes

Quad bikes are where the real risks sit. They are heavy, they tip on steep side slopes, and a rider who panics often grabs the throttle instead of the brake.

What to insist on:

- **A helmet that fits,** fastened, every time. Goggles or sunglasses help too.
- **A proper briefing** on throttle, brakes, body position and what to do if the bike starts to tip.
- **A guide riding with the group,** setting the pace and choosing the route.
- **A practice area** before you head into bigger dunes.
- **Closed shoes,** long trousers and something covering your arms.

How to ride:

- **Ride straight up and straight down dunes,** not across steep faces, where the bike can roll.
- **Keep your distance** from the rider in front.
- **Never ride over a crest you cannot see beyond.** Slow down and let the guide show you the line.
- **If the bike starts to tip, step off on the uphill side** and let it go. A bike can be fixed.

### Dune buggies

Dune buggies sit between a quad and a 4x4. They have a roll cage, seatbelts and usually two seats, so they feel more protected, and that can make people drive faster than their skill allows. Wear the harness and helmet provided, keep your arms and hands inside the cage, and follow the guide's line. If you are the passenger, you can ask the driver to slow down at any time.

Buggies throw up a lot of sand, so goggles or a face covering make the ride far more comfortable. Fasten loose items before you set off; anything left on the seat or the floor will end up in the desert. If you are driving for the first time, treat the first few minutes as practice, and keep the gap to the buggy in front generous, because you will lose sight of it as it drops over each crest.

### Dune driving in a 4x4

Most visitors meet the dunes from the back seat of a 4x4 on a safari. Here the driver carries the responsibility, but you still have choices: keep your seatbelt on, sit back in the seat, and ask for a gentler drive if anyone feels unwell. In the Dubai Desert Conservation Reserve, dune drives follow [predefined routes](https://www.ddcr.org/what-to-do/). Our guide to [whether a desert safari is worth it](/stories/is-a-desert-safari-worth-it/) describes what the drive feels like.

## Choosing where to ride

Not all desert is equal. Large, busy riding areas see many vehicles moving at once, sometimes in different directions. Quieter, managed areas tend to be calmer and better organised.

The Dubai Desert Conservation Reserve shows what a managed desert looks like. It says it gives [limited access to designated areas to selected tour operators](https://www.ddcr.org/about/), trains tour guides in best practice for desert excursions, and places strict limits on all activities that have an environmental impact. Ask your operator where you will ride, how many groups share the area, and how routes are chosen.

In a busy area, the risk comes as much from other vehicles as from the sand. Drivers coming over a crest cannot see you any more than you can see them, so stay with your group, keep to the line your guide sets, and never stop just below a crest where someone arriving from the other side would not have time to react. Spectators should stand well back from slopes in use, on the side of the dune rather than at the bottom.

If you are visiting Liwa, be aware that Visit Abu Dhabi describes the Liwa International Festival each December, with [three weeks of desert motorsports](https://www.visitabudhabi.ae/en/where-to-go/regions/al-dhafra), cultural experiences and concerts. Expect the dunes to be far busier then. Our [Liwa and Empty Quarter guide](/stories/liwa-empty-quarter-guide/) has more on the region.

## How a typical session unfolds

Most quad and buggy sessions follow a similar pattern. You arrive at a base camp or meeting point, sign a waiver, and are fitted with a helmet and any other gear. A guide then gives a briefing on the controls and on hand signals for slowing, stopping and regrouping. After a short practice on flat or gently rolling sand, the group heads out in single file behind the guide, with regular stops to regroup and drink water.

Pay attention to that waiver and briefing. If you are asked to sign something you do not understand, ask, and if the briefing is rushed or skipped, that tells you something about the operator. A good guide checks that every rider can stop and steer before the group sets off, and is happy to send a nervous rider back or swap them to a passenger seat.

### What to wear

Dress for the sand rather than for photographs:

- **Closed shoes** with socks; sandals fill with hot sand and offer no protection if you put a foot down.
- **Long, light trousers** and a long-sleeved top, which protect against sunburn and grazes.
- **Sunglasses or goggles** and a scarf or buff to pull over your nose and mouth.
- **Nothing loose.** Tie back long hair, remove dangling jewellery and leave hats that could blow off in the vehicle.

Outside the activity itself, dress modestly around camps and meeting points, as you would elsewhere in the UAE.

## Heat, health and who should sit out

### Heat is the hidden risk

Dune sports combine physical effort, full sun, protective gear and sand that reflects heat back at you. The NHS advises [drinking more cold drinks, wearing light-coloured, loose clothing and avoiding the sun between 11am and 3pm](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/heat-exhaustion-heatstroke/), and lists symptoms of heat exhaustion including headache, dizziness, nausea, cramps and excessive sweating. It describes heatstroke as a medical emergency. Ride early or late in the day, drink before you feel thirsty, and stop the moment anyone feels unwell. Our guide to [staying safe in the UAE heat](/stories/uae-heat-safety-guide/) has more.

### Medical conditions and pregnancy

Jolts, sudden stops and falls are part of these activities. If you have back or neck problems, a heart condition, recent surgery or are pregnant, get medical advice and consider sitting out the motorised activities. The Foreign Office stresses that [appropriate travel insurance for local treatment or unexpected medical evacuation](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/health) is particularly important if you have a health condition or are pregnant.

### Children

Operators set their own rules on ages, engine sizes and whether children ride alone or as passengers. Ask before you book, and do not let a child ride a machine they cannot physically control. Sandboarding on a small slope is often a better fit for younger children, and watching from a safe spot while older family members ride is a perfectly good role. Keep children well away from the bottom of slopes and from any area where vehicles are moving or parking.

### Back, neck and motion sickness

Even as a passenger in a 4x4 or buggy, the repeated jolts of dune driving can aggravate back and neck problems, and many people feel motion sick on the dunes. Eat lightly beforehand, sit facing forward, keep your eyes on the horizon and say so early if you feel unwell. A good driver will slow down or stop without fuss.

### Insurance

Some travel insurance policies treat quad biking and similar activities as adventure sports, with exclusions or extra conditions. Read your policy before you ride, not after. Our guide to [travel insurance for the UAE](/stories/uae-travel-insurance-guide/) covers what to check.

## If something goes wrong

The UAE government lists [998 for an ambulance and 999 for police](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/justice-safety-and-the-law/Safety/handling-emergencies), and notes that an air ambulance may be sent if needed. Your guide should carry a first aid kit and know how to describe your location. Before you set off, ask how they would get help to you in the dunes.

The same page lists 997 for the fire department and 995 for search and rescue, and notes that in Abu Dhabi the police AD999 app can be used to request an ambulance. If someone is hurt, keep them still and in the shade, stay with your group rather than walking off to find help, and let the guide coordinate. Note any landmarks you can see; in rolling dunes, they make it much easier for help to find you.

## Common mistakes to avoid

Most dune accidents come from a short list of avoidable errors. Riding too fast too soon, before you have a feel for the machine. Following another rider too closely and losing sight of them over a crest. Riding across a steep slope instead of straight up or down. Taking off a helmet because it is hot. Pushing on when you are tired, thirsty or dizzy. And, for families, letting a child ride something they cannot control. If you avoid those, you remove most of the risk.

## Quick answers

**Is quad biking in the UAE desert safe?** It can be, with the right operator and gear. The Foreign Office advises going with a reputable company with adequate health and safety procedures and checking that your insurance covers the activity.

**Is sandboarding suitable for beginners?** Yes, on a gentle slope with a clear run-out. Riding seated or on your front is safer while you learn.

**What number do you call in an emergency?** The UAE government lists 998 for an ambulance and 999 for police.

**Can pregnant travellers do dune sports?** Get medical advice first and consider sitting out motorised activities. The Foreign Office says insurance is particularly important if you are pregnant.

**When is the best time of day to ride?** Early morning or late afternoon, avoiding the strongest sun. The NHS advises avoiding the sun between 11am and 3pm in hot weather.

## Questions to ask your operator

- What safety gear do you provide for each activity, and do you have helmets in all sizes, including for children?
- Is there a briefing and a practice area before we ride into bigger dunes?
- How many riders does each guide lead, and does a guide ride with the group at all times?
- What are your age and experience rules for driving quads and buggies alone?
- Where exactly do we ride, and how many other groups use the same area?
- What first aid do your guides carry, and how would you get help to us in the dunes?
