Early on the first morning of Eid, the streets are strangely still. Then the prayers end, and the country seems to burst open: families in new clothes heading to visit relatives, children clutching small gifts, and the smell of coffee and sweets drifting out of open doors.

Eid is the UAE at its most joyful, and visiting during it is a pleasure as long as you know what to expect. There are two Eids each year, Eid Al Fitr at the end of Ramadan and Eid Al Adha a couple of months later, and both are public holidays. For visitors, Eid in the UAE means a festive mood, busier attractions and roads, some changed opening hours, and dates that are only confirmed shortly beforehand. Plan with a little flexibility and you will enjoy it.

## Two Eids, two meanings

Both festivals are about family, generosity and gratitude, but they mark different moments.

Abu Dhabi Culture describes Eid Al Fitr and Eid Al Adha as [the two most important religious holidays in the United Arab Emirates](https://abudhabiculture.ae/en/Cultural-Heritage/InTangible/Heritage-Register/Social-Practices/Celebrating-Eid), reflecting Islam's central role in Emirati life.

### Eid Al Fitr

Eid Al Fitr celebrates the end of Ramadan, the month of daytime fasting. Abu Dhabi Culture notes that families prepare by cleaning and arranging the house. If you have travelled during Ramadan, the shift is striking: daytime eating and drinking in public return to normal, and the city's rhythm swings back towards the day. Our guide to [visiting the UAE during Ramadan](/stories/visiting-uae-during-ramadan/) explains the month that leads up to it.

### Eid Al Adha

Eid Al Adha, which Abu Dhabi Culture translates as the "festival of the slaughter", [comes at the end of the annual Haj pilgrimage to Mecca and celebrates the faith of the prophet Ibrahim](https://abudhabiculture.ae/en/Cultural-Heritage/InTangible/Heritage-Register/Social-Practices/Celebrating-Eid). Traditionally, sheep, goats or cows are slaughtered and the meat shared with family, friends and those in need. The day before, Arafah Day, is also a public holiday in the UAE.

## Eid traditions, old and new

Many of the customs you see today have deep roots, and Abu Dhabi Culture's account of how Eid used to be celebrated adds colour to a modern visit.

### Dressing for Eid

New clothes have always mattered. Abu Dhabi Culture explains that in the past men would [buy a new kandoora or dye an existing one using walnut oil and cardamom](https://abudhabiculture.ae/en/Cultural-Heritage/InTangible/Heritage-Register/Social-Practices/Celebrating-Eid), while women dressed in special Eid clothes and prepared their hair with perfumes and braids. Today you will notice crisp white kandooras, elegant abayas and children in their best outfits, especially on the first morning.

### Breakfast, meat and sharing

The same source notes that among Bedouin and rural communities, Eid was celebrated more modestly, beginning the first day with a small snack of dates and milk before the men and boys went to prayers and returned for a family breakfast. For Eid Al Adha, a group of neighbours or members of an extended family would often buy one animal together and share the meat, distributing it to family, friends and the needy. The emphasis on sharing has not changed, and charity remains central to both festivals.

Both holidays, Abu Dhabi Culture adds, last at least three days. That makes Eid less a single event than a few days of visits, meals and outings.

### Henna and dance

Henna is part of the celebration for many women and girls. Visit Abu Dhabi describes Arabic henna as [large, floral patterns](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/henna-painting) that are painless and wash off within a week, and says visitors can have henna applied at heritage villages or on desert safaris. A henna design is a lovely, low-key way to share in the festive mood.

You may also see traditional dance at heritage sites and public events. Visit Abu Dhabi describes Al Ayyala as a dance [performed by men and boys at weddings, festivals and national celebrations](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/ayala-dancing), with two rows of performers holding thin bamboo canes and moving to the beat of drums, and notes that UNESCO recognised it in 2014. Watch respectfully and ask before filming performers up close.

## How Eid dates are set

This is the part that trips visitors up. Eid does not fall on the same Gregorian date each year, and the exact day is not fixed until the moon is sighted.

The UAE government's list of public holidays gives:

- **Eid Al Fitr:** [1 to 3 Shawwal](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/public-holidays-and-religious-affairs/public-holidays), with an extra holiday on the 30th day of Ramadan if the month lasts 30 days.
- **Arafah Day:** 9 Dhu Al Hijjah.
- **Eid Al Adha:** 10 to 12 Dhu Al Hijjah.

The portal notes that Islamic holidays are determined according to moon sighting. In practice, that means the final dates are announced only a day or so before.

### Projected dates for 2027

Astronomical projections give a useful guide, though they are not confirmed:

- **Eid Al Fitr 2027:** The National reports that the UAE's Astronomy Centre projects Ramadan to begin on 8 February 2027, with [Eid Al Fitr expected on 9 or 10 March 2027](https://www.thenationalnews.com/news/uae/2026/10/06/ramadan-2027-uae-dates-eid-al-fitr-holiday/), subject to moon sighting.
- **Eid Al Adha 2027:** Khaleej Times reports the Astronomy Centre's calculation that [Eid Al Adha is likely on 16 or 17 May 2027](https://www.khaleejtimes.com/uae/ramadan-eid-al-fitr-eid-al-adha-2027-likely-dates), with Arafah Day likely on 15 or 16 May.

Gulf News adds that, unlike some other public holidays, [Eid holidays cannot be moved to the beginning or end of a week](https://gulfnews.com/living-in-uae/ask-us/ramadan-2027-eid-al-fitr-eid-al-adha-uae-public-holidays-1.500712923) to create a longer weekend, because they follow the Hijri calendar. It stresses that these are tentative dates until confirmed after crescent sightings.

If your trip touches these windows, build in a day of slack on either side for anything time-sensitive.

## What Eid looks like on the ground

You will feel Eid long before you understand the details. The atmosphere is warm and generous, and the cities fill up.

### Family, new clothes and generosity

Abu Dhabi Culture describes the shared heart of both festivals: [visiting family to offer Eid greetings, giving charity to the poor and needy, special meals and the wearing of new clothes](https://abudhabiculture.ae/en/Cultural-Heritage/InTangible/Heritage-Register/Social-Practices/Celebrating-Eid). Both holidays begin with early morning prayers on the first day. Women and girls apply henna, children receive new clothes and small gifts, and traditional dances such as Al Razfah are still performed.

It also notes that during Eid most majlises offer sweets, dates, fruit, coffee and tea to visiting guests. If you are lucky enough to be invited somewhere, accept the coffee; our guide to [the majlis and Emirati hospitality](/stories/majlis-emirati-hospitality/) explains the etiquette.

### Busier everywhere

For visitors, the practical effects are predictable:

- **Crowds.** Malls, parks, beaches and attractions get busy, especially in the afternoons and evenings, as residents enjoy time off.
- **Traffic.** Roads between emirates are busier as families travel to visit relatives.
- **Hotels.** Demand rises for hotels and desert stays, since many residents take short breaks within the country.
- **Opening hours.** Government offices close for the holiday, and some businesses keep reduced or holiday hours. Attractions often stay open but may change timings.

Book popular attractions, restaurants and desert trips early, and confirm timings directly in the days before Eid.

## How an Eid day unfolds for a visitor

The first morning starts early and quietly. Prayers take place at mosques and open prayer grounds soon after sunrise, and roads can be busy near them for a short while before going calm again. This is a good time for a peaceful walk or a slow hotel breakfast.

By late morning, families are visiting relatives, so residential areas buzz while some tourist spots stay relatively quiet. From the afternoon onwards, the picture flips: malls, parks, beaches and corniches fill up, and the evenings are the busiest of all, often with events or fireworks in the bigger cities.

The following days of the holiday tend to be busy throughout, as families take day trips and short breaks. If you want to see a popular attraction, aim for opening time on the first or second day, and save relaxed activities such as the pool or the beach for the crowded afternoons.

### Eid Al Fitr and Eid Al Adha compared

For a visitor, the two holidays feel similar, but there are differences worth knowing.

- **Eid Al Fitr** follows a month of Ramadan routines, so the change in rhythm is dramatic. Daytime cafés and restaurants return to normal hours, and there is a real sense of release.
- **Eid Al Adha** comes with Arafah Day just before it, which is also a public holiday. Because it follows the pilgrimage season, many residents travel, and the meat-sharing tradition shapes family meals.
- **Timing in the year** moves with the lunar calendar, so check how the dates fall during your trip rather than relying on last year's experience.

## What catches visitors out

- **Booking fixed dates too early.** A one-day shift in the moon sighting can move the holiday. Choose flexible tickets for anything that depends on the exact date.
- **Assuming everything shuts.** Most malls, restaurants and attractions stay open, but government offices close and some services change hours.
- **Underestimating traffic.** Roads between emirates are busy as families travel, so leave more time for transfers and airport runs.
- **Leaving desert stays to the last minute.** Camps, mountain retreats and east coast hotels are popular with residents over the holiday.
- **Forgetting cash for small purchases.** Markets and stalls at holiday events may prefer cash, so carry a little.

## Families and accessibility

Eid is a family festival, so children fit right in. Parks, beaches and malls often run activities, and the evening events are usually aimed at families as much as adults. Pace the days with a quiet morning, an outing in the afternoon and an early night after any fireworks, since late evenings in crowds can tire younger children quickly. Keep a close eye on them in crowds and agree a meeting point in busy places.

If anyone in your group uses a wheelchair or has limited mobility, plan visits for quieter mornings, when lifts, ramps and parking are easier to reach. Book accessible taxis or transfers early, since demand rises over the holiday.

## Eid etiquette for visitors

Eid is relaxed and welcoming, and nobody expects visitors to know every custom. A few habits help.

### Greetings

"Eid Mubarak", meaning blessed Eid, is the standard greeting, and visitors are welcome to use it with hotel staff, guides and anyone you meet. It is always appreciated. If someone greets you first, a simple "Eid Mubarak" back, with a smile and a hand on the heart, is a perfect reply. Many hotel and tour staff are far from their own families over the holiday, so a friendly word means more than you might think.

### Dress

Eid is a time when people dress smartly, and modest clothing fits the mood. The UK Foreign Office advises visitors to [dress modestly in public areas such as shopping malls](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), with women covering shoulders and knees, and swimwear kept for beaches and pools. Our [what to wear guide](/stories/what-to-wear-uae-etiquette/) has practical outfit ideas.

### Photography

With so many families out, photos need care. The Foreign Office advises [not to photograph people without their permission](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security). Fireworks, decorations and landmarks are fair game; strangers and their children are not, unless you ask.

## Is Eid a good time to visit?

For most visitors, yes. You get a festive atmosphere, generous hospitality and a glimpse of family life you will not see at other times. The trade-offs are crowds, traffic and the need for flexible plans.

Eid suits travellers who enjoy a lively atmosphere and are happy to book ahead. If you want quiet attractions and empty roads, aim for the weeks either side. ## Quick answers

**When is Eid in the UAE?**
Eid Al Fitr runs from 1 to 3 Shawwal and Eid Al Adha from 10 to 12 Dhu Al Hijjah, according to the UAE government. Both depend on moon sighting, so exact dates are confirmed shortly beforehand.

**When is Eid Al Fitr 2027?**
The National reports that it is projected for 9 or 10 March 2027, subject to moon sighting.

**When is Eid Al Adha 2027?**
Khaleej Times reports the Astronomy Centre's calculation of 16 or 17 May 2027, with Arafah Day likely on 15 or 16 May.

**Are attractions open during Eid?**
Many attractions stay open but get much busier, and some timings change. Confirm hours directly with each venue in the days before.

**What should I say to people during Eid?**
"Eid Mubarak" is the usual greeting, and visitors are welcome to use it.

## Questions to ask your operator

- If Eid dates shift by a day after the moon sighting, how will that affect my bookings?
- Which attractions and areas are likely to be busiest over Eid, and when is the quietest time to visit them?
- Will any of my tours run at different times, or be unavailable, during the holiday?
- How much extra time should I allow for transfers between emirates on Eid days?
- Are there Eid events, fireworks or cultural performances you would recommend for visitors?
- Should I book desert trips or restaurants further ahead than usual?
