The first plate of an Emirati breakfast often looks like a dessert: a tangle of golden vermicelli, glinting with sugar and flecked with saffron. Then your fork reaches the thin layer of egg on top, and the whole thing turns savoury in your mouth.

That is balaleet, and it is the best possible argument for skipping the hotel buffet one morning. An Emirati breakfast is warm bread, sesame, honey, date syrup, cardamom and saffron, served slowly and usually with coffee. Nothing is fiery, nothing is heavy in the way a fry-up is heavy, and almost every dish is something you will not have eaten anywhere else.

The three dishes to know by name are balaleet, khameer and chebab (also spelt chabab), and below we explain what each is, how to eat it, how to order it, how to behave if you are someone's guest, and where visitors can reliably find it.

## Why breakfast is the easiest way into Emirati food

Emirati food grew from a short list of ingredients. The UAE government portal explains that because of the harsh climate, [agricultural produce was limited in the olden days](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/culture/food), and it notes that bread and pancakes were popular before rice arrived. In other words, the breakfast table holds some of the oldest dishes in the cuisine. The same page adds that in the past, dates were fermented in the sun to act as a raising agent for bread, a reminder of how closely the palm and the oven were linked.

For a visitor, that makes breakfast a gentle introduction. The flavours are distinctive without being challenging, and the dishes are kind to a jet-lagged stomach. Visit Ras Al Khaimah describes the morning meal as [freshly baked bread, rich spreads and spiced beverages](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/blog/emirati-flavors-traditional-food-uae/), which is about right.

There is a practical reason too. Lunch and dinner menus can be long and built around large shared platters of rice and meat, which are easier to tackle once you know what you like. A breakfast table is smaller, easier to experiment with and easy to share, so it is a low-risk way to discover whether saffron and cardamom are flavours you want more of later in the trip.

## Balaleet: sweet noodles under a savoury lid

Balaleet is the dish people talk about afterwards. Visit Ras Al Khaimah describes it as [vermicelli noodles cooked with sugar, cardamom and saffron, topped with a thin layer of egg](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/blog/emirati-flavors-traditional-food-uae/), and calls the mix of sweet and salty comforting and unique to Emirati breakfast tables.

How to eat it:

- **Take both layers in one forkful.** Eaten separately, the noodles taste like a pudding and the egg like an omelette. Together, they make sense.
- **Go slowly at first.** If you are used to savoury breakfasts, the first bite can be a surprise. By the third, most people are converted.
- **Share it.** Balaleet works well as one plate among several, so everyone at the table can try it next to the breads.

What to look for: the noodles should be soft but separate, with some strands toasted a shade darker than the rest, and the egg should be thin enough to lift with the noodles rather than sit on top like a separate dish. Presentation varies. Some kitchens lay a flat omelette over the noodles, others fold it to one side, and home versions can be sweeter or plainer depending on the cook.

## Khameer: the bread named after yeast

A basket of soft, round, slightly puffed bread, scattered with sesame. The UAE government portal explains that [khameer is the Arabic word for yeast](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/culture/food), and that the bread is made from yeast, flour and powdered milk, sprinkled with sesame seeds and served with cheese or honey. Visit Ras Al Khaimah adds that it is [slightly sweet and fluffy](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/blog/emirati-flavors-traditional-food-uae/), and often served with date syrup, honey or cream cheese.

Tear it by hand, warm, and use it to carry whatever else is on the table. Khameer with cheese and a drizzle of date syrup is the combination we would steer a first-timer towards: salty, sweet and soft in one mouthful.

In modern cafés you will also see khameer split and filled like a sandwich, with egg, cheese, olives or greens. That is a newer way of serving it, and perfectly good, but if you want the traditional version, ask for it plain and warm with the spreads on the side.

## Chebab: the Emirati pancake

Chebab looks familiar, which is part of its charm. The UAE portal describes chabab as [the Emirati pancake, made with flour, eggs, sugar and spices like cardamom and saffron](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/culture/food), served with date syrup. Visit Ras Al Khaimah describes them as [thin pancakes flavoured with saffron and cardamom](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/blog/emirati-flavors-traditional-food-uae/), typically served with date syrup or cheese.

The spices are the difference. Where a pancake at home might taste of butter and sugar, chebab smells of cardamom and has the faint golden colour that saffron gives. They usually arrive as a small stack, and they are best eaten quickly, while they are still soft.

## Date syrup, coffee and the rest of the table

The supporting cast matters as much as the stars. Dibs, or date syrup, is described by the UAE portal as [a dip to relish both savoury and sweet dishes](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/culture/food), and you will find it next to most of the dishes above. It is dark, thick and less sharp than honey, and a little goes a long way.

Then there is gahwa, Arabic coffee. Visit Abu Dhabi describes it as [blended with cardamom and saffron and usually served in tiny handleless cups](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/coffee-and-dates), and notes that dates are traditionally served alongside. The same page explains that the coffee is poured just a quarter full, with guests enjoying one to three cups. It is lighter and more fragrant than the coffee most visitors drink at breakfast, so think of it as part of the meal rather than a caffeine fix. Our guide to [Arabic coffee and dates](/stories/arabic-coffee-etiquette/) explains how to accept and decline a cup politely, and our guide to [dates in the UAE](/stories/uae-dates-guide/) covers the fruit itself.

## How an Emirati breakfast unfolds

If you have only eaten breakfast standing at a buffet, the pace is the first thing to adjust to. Plates tend to arrive together and stay on the table, and people help themselves over a long, unhurried conversation. Bread is torn and shared rather than portioned out. Coffee may come at the start, the end or throughout, and in a hosted setting someone will keep an eye on your cup.

A rough shape for the meal looks like this:

- **Coffee and dates first, often.** A cup of gahwa and a date or two is the traditional welcome, so do not be surprised if this comes before the food.
- **Breads and spreads in the middle.** Khameer, chebab, cheese, honey and date syrup spread across the table for everyone to reach.
- **Balaleet as the centrepiece.** It is usually one plate to share, and it holds its heat well, so there is no rush.
- **Tea to finish.** In many places a pot of sweet tea follows, which is your cue that the meal is winding down.

Allow more time than you would for breakfast at home, and more again for a hosted meal with conversation.

## Where to find an Emirati breakfast

We do not name restaurants, because menus, chefs and standards change. Instead, here are the kinds of places where an Emirati breakfast reliably turns up.

### A hosted cultural breakfast in old Dubai

In the Al Fahidi Historical Neighbourhood, the Sheikh Mohammed bin Rashid Al Maktoum Centre for Cultural Understanding runs [cultural meals](https://cultures.ae/program/cultural-meals), and breakfast is one of them. Traditional food is served while Emirati presenters talk about culture, the influence of Islam on local traditions and daily life, and take questions. The page says booking is required, so check the current schedule before you plan around it. You can find more on the area in our [Al Fahidi Historical Neighbourhood guide](/stories/al-fahidi-historical-neighbourhood/).

<photo-set layout="inset-right" keys="emirati-breakfast-guide-4" alts="Visitors seated on red carpets and cushions around a long cloth laid with dishes in a sand-coloured courtyard, listening to a host in a white kandura" caption="A cultural meal in a heritage courtyard, with guests seated on the floor around shared dishes."></photo-set>

It is a particularly good first stop, because you will order with far more confidence afterwards. The meal may be served on the floor, majlis style, around a cloth laid with shared dishes, so wear something you can sit cross-legged in. The format suits people who like to ask questions: there is no such thing as a silly one in this setting, and the presenters expect to be asked about food, dress, faith and family life.

### A breakfast in someone's home

In Abu Dhabi, the tourism board lists experiences where you can be [a guest in a local home](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/article-hub/traditional-emirati-experiences), with traditional hospitality, Arabic coffee and dates, and a choice of breakfast, lunch or dinner personalised for your group. The listing adds that the visit includes displays of spices, perfumes and jewellery, and ends with the burning of fragrant bokhoor. This is as close as most visitors come to a family table, and it is worth asking a local operator about.

### Restaurants and cafés with Emirati menus

Across the cities, a number of restaurants and cafés serve Emirati recipes, and some of them specialise in breakfast. You do not need a list. Look for a menu that names balaleet, khameer and chebab, ideally with luqaimat for afterwards, and you are in the right place.

## How to order like you have done it before

A few words go a long way on an Emirati breakfast menu:

- **Balaleet:** the sweet vermicelli with egg. One plate between two or three is plenty.
- **Khameer:** the soft sesame bread. Ask for it plain if the menu also offers filled versions.
- **Chebab or chabab:** the spiced pancakes, served with date syrup or cheese.
- **Dibs:** date syrup. If it is not on the table, it is fine to ask for it.
- **Gahwa:** Arabic coffee. Expect small cups and a fragrant, pale brew.
- **Luqaimat:** small fried dough balls with date syrup, more often a sweet or snack than breakfast, but worth knowing.

For two people trying it for the first time, one balaleet, one basket of khameer, one plate of chebab and a pot of coffee is a balanced order that covers all the essentials without leaving you too full to walk afterwards.

## Dietary notes and what catches people out

An Emirati breakfast is generally vegetarian, but it is not automatically suitable for every diet.

- **Eggs are everywhere.** They are in balaleet and in chebab, so egg allergies need flagging before you order.
- **Dairy turns up quietly.** Khameer is made with powdered milk according to the UAE portal, and cheese and cream cheese are common accompaniments.
- **Gluten is hard to avoid.** Vermicelli, khameer and chebab are all wheat-based.
- **Sugar levels vary.** Balaleet, date syrup and honey make this a sweet meal overall. If you need to limit sugar, ask for syrup on the side.
- **Nuts may appear in desserts and garnishes.** If you have a nut allergy, say so clearly, especially in a hosted setting where dishes are prepared in advance.

The other common surprise is simply the sweetness of balaleet. Some visitors expect a savoury noodle dish and find it confusing. Go in knowing it is meant to be both, and it makes far more sense.

## Breakfast with children

Children often take to an Emirati breakfast faster than adults. Pancakes and soft bread with honey are familiar territory, and the sweetness of balaleet tends to win over young eaters. A floor-seated cultural meal can be harder for very small children who struggle to sit still, so a café breakfast at a table may be the easier option for toddlers.

## How to be a good guest at breakfast

If your breakfast is hosted, a few habits help.

- **Use your right hand.** Visit Ras Al Khaimah's etiquette guide advises that you [take plates, cups and food with your right hand](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/blog/all-social-customs-and-etiquette-every-female-traveler-should-know/), as using the left is considered disrespectful.
- **Accept what is offered.** The same guide notes that refusing food and drink can cause offence. If you have an allergy or a medical reason, explain it, and your host will understand.
- **Start small.** Modest first helpings leave room for the second round that will almost certainly be offered.
- **Dress for sitting.** If you are invited into a home, loose clothing that covers shoulders and knees is comfortable and respectful. Our guide to [what to wear in the UAE](/stories/what-to-wear-uae-etiquette/) covers the detail.
- **Ask before photographing people.** Photographing the food is usually welcome. Photographing your hosts or other guests is a separate question, so ask first.

## Breakfast during Ramadan

If your trip falls during Ramadan, the rhythm of the morning changes. The UAE government portal explains that [capable Muslims abstain from eating and drinking from dawn to dusk](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/public-holidays-and-religious-affairs/ramadan), that non-Muslims are not required to fast, and that some restaurants may adjust their hours or close during the day. The same page notes that non-Muslims can find dining options in shopping malls, where restaurants and food courts provide designated areas away from those who are fasting, and that the two main meals of the month are suhoor, eaten before sunrise, and iftar, which breaks the fast.

The FCDO advises that it may cause offence to [eat, drink, smoke or chew gum in public in the daytime](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), that you should check opening hours of shops and restaurants, and that hotels and restaurants serving food in fasting hours may separate you from Islamic guests, for example with screens.

In practice, eat breakfast at your hotel or in a venue serving during the day, and save your Emirati food exploring for the evening, when it comes out in force. Many of the same dishes appear at iftar and suhoor tables. Our guide to [visiting the UAE during Ramadan](/stories/visiting-uae-during-ramadan/) explains what changes.

## Building it into your trip

An Emirati breakfast fits neatly into the start of a heritage day. Eat first, then walk a souk or a historic quarter while the morning is still cool. In Dubai, a cultural breakfast in Al Fahidi puts you a short walk from the old lanes, the creek and the souks, so the morning flows naturally from table to street. If you are pairing it with lunch and dinner elsewhere, keep it light: one shared plate of balaleet, a basket of khameer and a pot of coffee is plenty for two.

## Quick answers

**What is a traditional Emirati breakfast?** Breads such as khameer, the spiced pancake chebab and the sweet-and-savoury noodle dish balaleet, served with cheese, honey or date syrup and usually Arabic coffee.

**What is balaleet?** Vermicelli cooked with sugar, cardamom and saffron and topped with a thin layer of egg, so each forkful is both sweet and savoury.

**What does khameer mean?** The UAE government portal explains that khameer is the Arabic word for yeast. The bread is made with yeast, flour and powdered milk and sprinkled with sesame.

**Is an Emirati breakfast vegetarian?** Usually, but it relies heavily on eggs, wheat and dairy, so flag any allergies before you order.

**Where can visitors try an Emirati breakfast?** Options include the cultural breakfast at the Sheikh Mohammed bin Rashid Al Maktoum Centre for Cultural Understanding in Dubai (booking required), home hospitality experiences listed by Visit Abu Dhabi, and restaurants and cafés with Emirati menus.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Can you book a cultural breakfast or a home hospitality breakfast for our group, and how far ahead is booking needed?
- Which dishes are usually served, and can you check for nut, egg, dairy or gluten allergies in advance?
- Will we sit on the floor or at a table, and what should we wear?
- Can a breakfast be combined with a heritage walk or souk visit afterwards?
- If we travel during Ramadan, what are the best ways to try these dishes in the evening instead?
