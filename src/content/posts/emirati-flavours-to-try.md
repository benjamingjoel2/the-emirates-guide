Pick up a loomi in a souk and it feels like nothing: a dark, hollow, papery ball, lighter than a ping-pong ball. Crack it open and the smell hits you, sharp, sour and faintly smoky, nothing like the fresh lime it once was. Drop it into a pot of rice and meat, and it changes the whole dish. Of all the Emirati flavours to try, it is the one that surprises people most.

That is the quiet trick of Emirati cooking. It does not rely on chilli heat or long lists of ingredients. It relies on a handful of strong, specific flavours used with confidence: saffron, cardamom, dried lime, date syrup, and in the desert, camel milk.

If you want to taste the UAE rather than just eat well in it, these are the Emirati flavours to try, what each tastes like, the dishes they show up in, where to find them, and how to buy them well.

## A pantry shaped by trade

Emirati food was built from little. The UAE government portal explains that [agricultural produce was limited in the olden days](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/culture/food), and that foreign trade brought spices and later rice. It names cinnamon, saffron and turmeric, along with nuts, limes and dried fruit, as the flavours that lift Emirati dishes.

Visit Abu Dhabi puts it more vividly: in every Emirati household, [the pantry tells a story](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/local-cuisine) of ingredients brought by trade routes, including saffron, cardamom and cinnamon. Knowing a few of them by name makes menus, markets and home kitchens far easier to read.

The same government page notes that some Emirati dishes carry [a hint of Arabian, Indian, Iranian, Mediterranean and Turkish influences](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/culture/food). It also explains how geography shaped the table: along the coasts fish was plentiful, and Emiratis learnt to dry it so it could reach people in the deserts and oases, while inland, communities relied on camel milk and meat, with sheep and goat as staples. The arrival of rice and spice made dishes such as khuzi, machboos and biryani possible.

Visit Abu Dhabi adds that hospitality and generosity are [two key ingredients](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/local-cuisine) of cooking in the Gulf, with dining long seen as a communal celebration. That matters for visitors: many of these flavours are at their best in shared dishes, eaten slowly, rather than as a quick bite on the go.

## Saffron: the golden thread

Saffron is everywhere once you start looking. It colours rice gold, scents breakfast and slips into coffee. The UAE portal describes machboos as [lamb or chicken layered with rice seasoned with spices and some saffron](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/culture/food), and madrooba, a salted fish dish, as sprinkled with saffron, nuts and spices.

At breakfast, Visit Ras Al Khaimah describes balaleet as [vermicelli cooked with sugar, cardamom and saffron](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/blog/emirati-flavors-traditional-food-uae/) and topped with egg, and chabab as thin pancakes flavoured with saffron and cardamom.

**What it tastes like:** floral, slightly honeyed and a touch bitter. It is more aroma than flavour, so notice it with your nose first.

**What to look for when buying:** whole threads rather than powder, a deep red colour, and a strong scent when the pack is opened. Powdered saffron is harder to judge. A seller confident in their stock will happily let you see and smell it before you buy.

## Cardamom: the scent of welcome

If one smell defines Emirati hospitality, it is cardamom. Visit Abu Dhabi says Arabic coffee comes [blended with cardamom and saffron](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/coffee-and-dates), served in tiny handleless cups. The UAE government's Ramadan page describes alqurs, a popular Ramadan sweet, as [a bread-like crumble with dates and cardamom](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/public-holidays-and-religious-affairs/ramadan).

<photo-set layout="inset-left" keys="emirati-flavours-to-try-2" alts="Green cardamom pods in a stone mortar seen from above" caption="Whole green cardamom pods, the spice behind Arabic coffee."></photo-set>

**What it tastes like:** warm, resinous and slightly citrus. In coffee, it is often stronger than the coffee itself. Our guide to [Arabic coffee and dates](/stories/arabic-coffee-etiquette/) explains how to accept a cup like a local.

**What to look for when buying:** green pods that are plump and evenly coloured. Whole pods keep their aroma far longer than ground cardamom, so crush them just before use at home.

## Cinnamon and turmeric: the quieter spices

Two more spices on the government's list deserve a mention, even if they are less showy. Cinnamon adds warmth to rice and meat dishes, and turmeric gives a gentle colour and earthiness. The UAE portal explains that spices were used to prepare fish dishes such as [machboos, saloona (stew), mhammar and al madrooba](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/culture/food), so a fish dish is a good place to notice them working together.

**How to try them:** order a saloona or a fish machboos and see how many layers you can pick out. If you are unsure, ask your server which spices are in the dish; most will be glad to explain.

## Loomi: the dried lime

Loomi is the flavour visitors least expect. Visit Ras Al Khaimah lists [loomi (dried lime), cinnamon and saffron](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/blog/emirati-flavors-traditional-food-uae/) as the spices that give machboos, often referred to as the national dish, its flavour. The UAE portal's list of core flavours includes limes and dried fruit.

**What it tastes like:** sour and earthy, with a faint bitterness and smokiness. It cuts through rich meat and rice the way a squeeze of fresh lemon might, but deeper.

<photo-set layout="pair" keys="emirati-flavours-to-try-3,emirati-flavours-to-try-4" alts="PAIR1|PAIR2" caption="PAIR"></photo-set>

**How to try it:** order machboos and look for the darkened lime among the rice. If you buy whole loomi to cook with at home, pierce or crack it before adding it to a pot so the flavour can escape.

## Dates and date syrup: the sweetener

Before refined sugar, there were dates. The UAE portal describes dibs, or date syrup, as [a dip to relish both savoury and sweet dishes](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/culture/food), and notes that chabab is served with it. Visit Ras Al Khaimah describes luqaimat, golden fried dumplings, [drizzled with date syrup and sprinkled with sesame seeds](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/blog/emirati-flavors-traditional-food-uae/).

**What it tastes like:** dark, rich and caramel-like, less sharply sweet than honey. Try it on warm bread with a little salty cheese for the classic contrast. Our guide to [dates in the UAE](/stories/uae-dates-guide/) covers varieties and markets.

Dates once did more than sweeten. The UAE portal records that in the olden days [dates were fermented in the sun](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/culture/food) to act as a raising agent for bread, a reminder of how central the fruit was to the whole kitchen.

## Sweet and savoury together

One of the most distinctive things about Emirati food is how happily it mixes sweet and savoury. The UAE portal describes mhammar, whole spiced fish, as [often served with sweet caramelised onions and sugary yellow rice](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/culture/food), a blend of savoury and sweet. Balaleet, with its sweet noodles and salty egg, works the same way.

If that sounds odd, trust it for one meal. Our guide to an [Emirati breakfast](/stories/emirati-breakfast-guide/) is the easiest place to start.

## Camel milk: the desert staple

In the desert and mountains, the UAE portal explains, Emiratis [largely relied on camel milk and meat](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/culture/food). Today it is easy to try. Visit Ras Al Khaimah describes camel milk as [slightly saltier than cow's milk](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/blog/emirati-flavors-traditional-food-uae/), used in desserts such as camel milk ice cream or enjoyed on its own, and available in groceries and supermarkets.

**What it tastes like:** lighter than you might expect, with a faint saltiness. A small carton from a supermarket is a low-stakes first try.

### Camel milk chocolate

The friendliest introduction is chocolate. Visit Abu Dhabi describes camel milk chocolate as made by [blending camel milk with vanilla, honey and cocoa beans](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/what-to-buy-and-souvenirs/dates-chocolates-and-sweets), sold in bars, pralines and camel-shaped figurines in souvenir shops and supermarkets. Its souvenir guide adds that it is [slightly saltier than cow's milk chocolate](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/what-to-buy-and-souvenirs), sometimes infused with dates, nuts or local spices. The same guide lists camel milk products such as soaps and chocolates among popular souvenirs, and says camel milk chocolate is sold in supermarkets, duty-free shops and souvenir stores.

## Where to taste and buy Emirati flavours

### In souks and markets

Souks are where you smell these flavours before you taste them. Visit Abu Dhabi encourages visitors to [explore Abu Dhabi's traditional souks](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/local-cuisine) to discover the ingredients that shape local cooking. In Dubai, the spice souk on the Deira side of the creek is the classic place to browse sacks of saffron, cardamom and dried limes; our guide to [Dubai's gold and spice souks](/stories/dubai-gold-and-spice-souks/) explains how to browse and bargain politely.

In the Heart of Sharjah, Visit Sharjah notes that Souq Al Arsah has [a popular halwa shop](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/shopping/souq-al-arsah/) for sweets and a café serving Arabic coffee.

In Abu Dhabi, Visit Abu Dhabi points food lovers to [the Mina Market and the Mina Fruit and Vegetable Souk](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/local-cuisine), and encourages shoppers buying seafood to choose fish wisely to support sustainable fishing. A market morning there gives you a sense of the raw ingredients behind the dishes, before you taste them cooked.

### In traditional dishes

The best way to taste these flavours is in the dishes they were made for. In Dubai's Al Fahidi Historical Neighbourhood, the Sheikh Mohammed bin Rashid Al Maktoum Centre for Cultural Understanding runs [cultural meals](https://cultures.ae/program/cultural-meals) where traditional food is served with conversation about Emirati culture. Booking is required. For dishes by name, see [Emirati food: what to eat and where](/stories/emirati-food-what-to-eat-where/).

## A day of tasting: how it might unfold

You do not need a food tour to taste all of these in a day, although a guide helps. A simple pattern works in Dubai, Abu Dhabi or Sharjah:

1. **Morning: breakfast and a souk.** Start with balaleet or chebab, where saffron, cardamom and date syrup appear together, then walk a spice souk while it is still cool. Smell the sacks, ask questions and buy little.
2. **Midday: a rice dish.** Order machboos and look for the loomi. If there is a fish version, try that too, and compare.
3. **Afternoon: coffee and dates.** A small cup of gahwa and a few dates is the classic pause, and the best way to taste cardamom on its own.
4. **Later: something sweet.** Finish with luqaimat and date syrup, or a square of camel milk chocolate.

Keep portions small through the day. These are rich, generous dishes, and you will taste more if you share.

## Souk etiquette when you are tasting and buying

Spice and sweet sellers are used to visitors, and most enjoy explaining their stock. A few habits make the exchange easier. Ask before you dip a hand into an open sack, and use your right hand to take any sample you are offered. Be clear early on if you are only browsing, and take your time: comparing two or three stalls is normal. If you want to photograph a stall, ask first, and remember that a photograph of the goods is not the same as a photograph of the seller. Our guide to [Dubai's gold and spice souks](/stories/dubai-gold-and-spice-souks/) has more on polite bargaining.

## Tasting with children

Children often enjoy this kind of food more than parents expect. Date syrup, luqaimat, chebab and camel milk chocolate are easy wins, and the smells of a spice souk can be a highlight in themselves if you turn it into a guessing game. Saffron rice and mild machboos are usually fine for younger eaters, but keep an eye on nuts in garnishes and sweets, and check the ingredients of anything new if your child has allergies.

## What catches people out

- **Expecting heat.** Emirati food is fragrant rather than fiery. If you want chilli, ask, but try it as it comes first.
- **Biting into loomi.** The dried lime is there to flavour the dish. Push it aside rather than eating it whole.
- **Too much saffron at home.** It is powerful. A small pinch, soaked in warm water first, goes a long way.
- **Nuts and dairy in unexpected places.** Nuts appear in garnishes and sweets, and milk in breads and desserts. If you have an allergy, say so clearly every time you order.
- **Buying the biggest bag.** Spices lose their aroma over time. Buy small amounts you will use, and buy whole rather than ground where you can.

### Buying to take home

- **Saffron:** buy from a reputable seller, ideally sealed, and ask to see the threads.
- **Loomi:** light and easy to pack; whole limes keep well.
- **Date syrup and dates:** sealed jars and boxes travel best.
- **Camel milk chocolate:** widely sold in supermarkets and souvenir shops.

## Quick answers

**What is loomi?** A dried lime used in Emirati cooking. Visit Ras Al Khaimah lists it among the spices that flavour machboos.

**What spices are used in Emirati food?** The UAE government portal names cinnamon, saffron and turmeric, along with nuts, limes and dried fruit; cardamom and saffron also flavour Arabic coffee.

**What does camel milk taste like?** Visit Ras Al Khaimah describes it as slightly saltier than cow's milk. It is sold in groceries and supermarkets.

**What is camel milk chocolate made from?** Visit Abu Dhabi says it blends camel milk with vanilla, honey and cocoa beans.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Can you arrange a guided souk walk focused on spices, dates and sweets, with tastings along the way?
- Can we join a cultural meal where dishes like machboos and balaleet are served?
- Where is the best place to buy good-quality saffron and loomi to take home?
- Can you check for nut, dairy or other allergies, given how often nuts and milk appear in these dishes?
- Is there somewhere we can try camel milk products as part of a desert or heritage day?
