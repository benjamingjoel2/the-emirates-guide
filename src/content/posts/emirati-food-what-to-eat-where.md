You can eat extremely well in the UAE without ever tasting Emirati food. The big cities are a catalogue of the world's kitchens, from Indian and Pakistani to Iranian, Filipino and Japanese, and most visitors fill their days with them happily. The local cooking is quieter. It lives in family kitchens, in heritage quarters, at Ramadan tables and in a small number of places that set out to serve it.

Find it and you get a very different picture of the country. Rice perfumed with saffron and dried lime. A wheat-and-meat porridge stirred until it is almost silky. Hot dough balls glossed with date syrup. And always, at the start, a tiny cup of coffee scented with cardamom and a plate of dates, offered before anyone asks what you would like.

This guide walks through the dishes worth knowing by name, where to look for them without relying on anyone's restaurant list, and the table manners that will make you a welcome guest.

## Where the flavours come from

Emirati food grew out of scarcity and trade. The UAE government portal explains that [agricultural produce was limited in the olden days](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/culture/food), so people relied on animal products: fish along the coasts, and camel milk and meat in the desert and mountains, with sheep and goat as staples. Trade brought spices and later rice, and with them the influences you can still taste: the same page notes hints of Arabian, Indian, Iranian, Mediterranean and Turkish cooking.

The result is food built on a handful of ingredients used with confidence. Cinnamon, saffron and turmeric, nuts, limes and dried fruit, according to [the portal](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/culture/food). Visit Ras Al Khaimah adds loomi, the [dried lime that gives machboos its sour depth](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/blog/emirati-flavors-traditional-food-uae/).

## Coffee and dates come first

Before any dish, there is gahwa. The UAE portal describes how [guests are traditionally welcomed with coffee and dates](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/culture/food), the coffee blended with cardamom and saffron and served in small handleless cups called finjans. It comes in a few small mouthfuls at a time.

UNESCO lists Arabic coffee as intangible cultural heritage and describes the [etiquette around it](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/arabic-coffee-a-symbol-of-generosity-02111): older people and guests are served first, the coffee is poured with the right hand, and the drinker shakes the cup to show they have finished. [Visit Abu Dhabi](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/coffee-and-dates) notes that each cup is poured just a quarter full, with guests enjoying one to three cups as a sign of respect.

Dates are the other half of the welcome. According to [the UAE portal](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/culture/food), more than 40 varieties are grown in the country, each with its own colour and flavour, and date syrup (dibs) is used as a dip for both sweet and savoury food. UNESCO's entry on the date palm, which the UAE coordinated, calls it [a key source of nutrition](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/date-palm-knowledge-skills-traditions-and-practices-01902) that has been tied to the region's crafts and traditions for centuries. Try a few varieties side by side and you will notice how much they differ, from fudge-soft to almost chewy.

## Breakfast dishes

Emirati breakfast is one of the easiest ways in, because the dishes are distinctive and gentle on a jet-lagged stomach.

- **Khameer:** a soft bread whose name, the [UAE portal](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/culture/food) explains, is the Arabic word for yeast. It is made with yeast, flour and powdered milk, sprinkled with sesame and served with cheese or honey.
- **Chabab (often spelt chebab):** the Emirati pancake, made with flour, eggs, sugar and spices such as cardamom and saffron, and [served with date syrup](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/culture/food).
- **Balaleet:** vermicelli cooked with sugar, cardamom and saffron and topped with a thin layer of egg. [Visit Ras Al Khaimah](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/blog/emirati-flavors-traditional-food-uae/) calls the mix of sweet and salty "comforting", and it is the dish that surprises first-timers most.

## The main dishes

### Machboos

If you only try one savoury dish, make it this. Visit Ras Al Khaimah describes machboos as [often referred to as the national dish](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/blog/emirati-flavors-traditional-food-uae/), a spiced rice dish with chicken, lamb or fish. The UAE portal describes it as [lamb or chicken layered with rice seasoned with spices and some saffron](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/culture/food), and lists a fish version among the coast's traditional dishes. Expect warmth rather than heat: it is fragrant, not fiery.

### Harees

Harees is the dish that tells you most about Emirati hospitality. [Visit Ras Al Khaimah](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/blog/emirati-flavors-traditional-food-uae/) describes it as wheat, meat (usually chicken or lamb) and a pinch of salt, slow-cooked for hours until it becomes smooth and porridge-like, often finished with ghee, and prepared for weddings, religious occasions and family gatherings. It is mild and deeply savoury, the kind of dish that tells you a meal is an occasion.

### Thareed, madrooba and the fish dishes

- **Thareed:** crisp rigag flatbread combined with a spiced meat or vegetable stew that soaks into it. Visit Ras Al Khaimah calls it [a staple during Ramadan](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/blog/emirati-flavors-traditional-food-uae/).
- **Madrooba:** salted fish cooked with flour and spices into a thick, smooth dish. The [UAE portal](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/culture/food) describes it as sprinkled with saffron, nuts and spices and served with rice.
- **Mhammar and saloona:** the portal also lists mhammar, [whole spiced fish often served with sweet caramelised onions and sugary yellow rice](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/culture/food), and saloona, a stew.
- **Khuzi:** for celebrations, the [same page](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/culture/food) describes stuffed whole roast lamb or goat on a bed of spiced rice.

## Something sweet: luqaimat

Luqaimat are the sweet you will remember. [Visit Ras Al Khaimah](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/blog/emirati-flavors-traditional-food-uae/) describes them as deep-fried dumplings, crisp outside and fluffy inside, drizzled with date syrup and sprinkled with sesame seeds. They arrive hot and sticky and disappear fast. The same guide also points visitors towards camel milk and camel milk desserts, which you will find in ordinary groceries and supermarkets.

## Where to find Emirati food

We don't name restaurants, because menus and quality change. Instead, here are the kinds of places where Emirati cooking reliably turns up.

### Cultural centres and heritage quarters

The most rewarding introduction is a meal with conversation. In Dubai's [Al Fahidi Historical Neighbourhood](/destinations/dubai/), the Sheikh Mohammed bin Rashid Al Maktoum Centre for Cultural Understanding runs [cultural meals](https://cultures.ae/program/cultural-meals): traditional food served while Emirati presenters talk about culture, the influence of Islam on local traditions and daily life, and take questions. Its page lists breakfast, brunch, lunch, dinner and an Emirati tea time, with iftar replacing dinner during Ramadan, and says booking is required.

Heritage areas are good hunting grounds in general. The UAE portal notes that [every emirate has at least one heritage village](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/culture/arab-and-islamic-heritage), and it names annual heritage festivals such as Sharjah Heritage Days and the Sheikh Zayed Heritage Festival that bring that heritage alive. They are worth checking for traditional food, but confirm current dates before you plan around one.

### Home-style restaurants and cafés

Across the cities you will find restaurants and cafés serving Emirati recipes, some in heritage-style settings, and several offer an Emirati breakfast. Look for menus that list the dishes above by name. A menu built around balaleet, chabab, khameer, machboos and luqaimat is a good sign.

### Home hospitality experiences

[Visit Abu Dhabi](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/article-hub/traditional-emirati-experiences) lists experiences where locals serve a traditional Emirati meal, and coffee and dates, in their own homes. This is as close as most visitors get to a family table, and it is worth asking a local operator about.

### Souks

Souks are where you shop for the flavours rather than eat the dishes. Dubai's spice souk, on the Deira side of the creek, is the place to smell saffron, cardamom and dried limes in sacks, and markets across the country sell dates by variety, so you can compare a few. A box of good dates also makes a welcome gift to take home.

### Ramadan iftars

If your trip falls during Ramadan, the iftar meal at sunset is the time Emirati dishes come out in force. The UAE portal notes that [fasts are still broken with dates](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/culture/food), and Visit Ras Al Khaimah notes that [madrooba is frequently served for iftar](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/blog/emirati-flavors-traditional-food-uae/) and thareed is a Ramadan staple. Remember that during the day the [FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) advises that eating, drinking or chewing gum in public may cause offence, and that restaurant opening hours change, so check ahead. Our guide to [visiting during Ramadan](/stories/visiting-uae-during-ramadan/) covers the month in detail.

## Eating as a guest

If you are invited to share a meal, especially in a majlis, a few habits go a long way.

- **Expect to sit on the floor.** UNESCO describes the majlis as typically [a large space with carpets on the floor and cushions against the wall](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/majlis-a-cultural-and-social-space-01076). Wear something loose and longer.
- **Use your right hand.** Visit Ras Al Khaimah's etiquette guide advises that you [take plates, cups and food with your right hand](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/blog/all-social-customs-and-etiquette-every-female-traveler-should-know/), as using the left is considered disrespectful.
- **Accept what is offered.** The same guide notes that refusing food and drink can cause offence, so if you have a medical or dietary reason, explain it and your host will understand. Always say thank you for the meal.
- **Shake the cup when you are done with coffee.** Otherwise it will keep coming.

For dress codes and the wider etiquette questions, see [what to wear in the UAE](/stories/what-to-wear-uae-etiquette/).

## Questions to ask your operator

- Can you arrange a cultural meal or a home hospitality experience, and how far ahead does it need booking?
- Which Emirati dishes are likely to be served, and can you handle allergies, vegetarian diets or other needs?
- Will we be sitting on the floor in a majlis, and is there anything we should know before we arrive?
- Are there heritage festivals or food events on during our dates where we might try traditional food?
- If our trip falls in Ramadan, can you help us join an iftar and plan around daytime opening hours?
- Can your guide take us through a souk and explain the dates and spices we see?
