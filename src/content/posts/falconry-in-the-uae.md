The falconer slips off the small leather hood, and for a second the bird simply looks. Then it lifts from the glove, drops low over the sand and climbs again, and everyone watching forgets about their phone for a moment.

Falconry in the UAE is not a show put on for tourists. It is a tradition that families still practise, that shaped how people once fed themselves in the desert, and that the country works hard to keep alive. The good news for visitors is that you can see it up close without any special connections. A desert display, a guided visit to a falcon hospital, or a reserve trip with a falconer who talks you through each bird will all give you a real feel for it.

Here is what falconry means in the Emirates, where you can watch it, and how to enjoy it well.

## Why falconry in the UAE matters so much

Spend a few days in the country and you will notice the falcon everywhere, in heritage displays, artwork and logos. The bird runs through the culture.

Visit Abu Dhabi calls falconry [one of the UAE's most treasured traditions](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/falconry), practised for over 2,000 years and passed down through generations. It explains that falcons played a vital role in Bedouin life, hunting bustard, curlews and other animals for food. In a landscape where food was hard won, a well-trained bird could make the difference.

The tourism board also notes that the late Sheikh Zayed bin Sultan Al Nahyan, the UAE's founding father, wrote about the practice in *Falconry as a Sport, Our Arab Heritage*, describing how early hunters caught and trained their birds using simple, time-honoured techniques. That a founding leader wrote a book about it tells you a great deal about its place in national identity.

### A heritage recognised by UNESCO

Falconry is also recognised internationally. The UAE is one of the submitting states for [falconry on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/falconry-a-living-human-heritage-01708), in an inscription dated 2021 that brings together many countries. UNESCO describes falconry as the traditional art of training and flying falcons, and sometimes eagles, hawks and other birds of prey, and says it has been practised for over 4,000 years worldwide.

The same entry makes a point that is easy to miss: the modern practice focuses on safeguarding falcons, quarry and habitats, as well as the practice itself. UNESCO notes that skills pass from generation to generation through mentoring, within families or in training clubs, and that the wider falconry community includes falcon hospitals, breeding centres, conservation agencies and traditional equipment makers.

## The bond between falconer and bird

What surprises most first-time watchers is how calm everything is. There is no shouting. A falconer moves slowly, speaks quietly, and the bird responds.

Visit Abu Dhabi describes falconers training their birds [with patience and care](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/falconry), creating a deep bond between human and falcon. UNESCO lists the shared elements that falconers across the world have in common: [the methods of breeding, training and caring for birds, the equipment used and the bonds between the falconer and the bird](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/falconry-a-living-human-heritage-01708).

Watch for a few details during any display:

- **The hood.** A small leather cap covers the bird's eyes and keeps it calm until it is time to fly.
- **The glove.** The falconer carries the bird on a thick glove, which protects the hand from talons.
- **The lure.** A weighted lure swung on a line gives the bird something to chase in the air, so you see its speed and turns without any live quarry.
- **The quiet.** Notice how little the falconer needs to say. Much of the communication is in movement and routine.

If the falconer invites questions, ask about the bird's name, its age and how long it took to train. The answers are often the best part of the visit.

## Where you can watch falconry

You do not need to know a falconer to see one at work. These are the most straightforward ways in.

### On a desert trip

Visit Abu Dhabi says [most desert safari experiences offer a chance to discover falconry](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/falconry), and that falcons also appear at cultural shows in several destinations. Quality varies, so ask before you book whether the falconry is a proper display with time to talk, or a quick photo stop. Our guide to [whether a desert safari is worth it](/stories/is-a-desert-safari-worth-it/) explains how different safari styles compare.

### In a conservation reserve

The Dubai Desert Conservation Reserve lists [falconry among its activities](https://www.ddcr.org/what-to-do/), describing a display of the birds' speed and natural instincts while an expert falconer discusses the breed and character of each bird. It adds that you get a chance to see and photograph the falcons up close. The reserve also runs an approved tours scheme, with guides certified by the reserve and tours expected to consider sustainability and waste. Our [Dubai Desert Conservation Reserve guide](/stories/dubai-desert-conservation-reserve-guide/) covers the reserve in more detail.

### At a falcon hospital

For a different angle, Abu Dhabi has a hospital just for falcons. Visit Abu Dhabi says the [Abu Dhabi Falcon Hospital](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/falconry) is located near Zayed International Airport, features a falcon display museum and offers guided tours. Seeing the medical side of falconry, and how carefully the birds are looked after, adds depth that a desert display alone cannot. Check current tour arrangements and booking directly before you go.

### Further into the desert

If your trip takes you deep into the dunes, perhaps on a drive out to Liwa, ask whether your camp or guide can arrange a falconry display. Our [Liwa and Empty Quarter guide](/stories/liwa-empty-quarter-guide/) helps you plan the journey.

## Photos, distance and good manners

A falcon on a glove is one of the most photogenic sights in the country, and most falconers expect cameras. Still, a few habits make the experience better for everyone, including the bird.

- **Ask before photographing people.** The UK Foreign Office advises visitors in the UAE [not to photograph people without their permission](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security). A falconer at a public display will usually be happy to pose, but ask first, and take extra care around families or anyone not part of the show.
- **Let the falconer lead.** Only hold a bird or step closer when invited, and follow instructions about how to hold your arm.
- **Keep still and quiet.** Sudden movements, waving and loud voices can unsettle the bird.
- **No flash.** Turn off your flash, especially at evening displays.
- **Keep children close.** Talons and beaks are sharp, and the bird is not a pet.

If you are offered a falcon on your own gloved arm, it is usually a brief, calm moment. Hold the arm steady and let the falconer do the rest.

## Quick answers

**Can tourists see falconry in the UAE?**
Yes. Visit Abu Dhabi says most desert safari experiences offer a chance to discover falconry, and the Dubai Desert Conservation Reserve lists falconry displays among its activities.

**Why are falcons important in the UAE?**
Falcons once helped Bedouin communities hunt for food, and falconry has been practised in the region for over 2,000 years, according to Visit Abu Dhabi. It is now recognised by UNESCO as intangible cultural heritage, with the UAE among the submitting states.

**Is there a falcon hospital visitors can tour?**
Visit Abu Dhabi says the Abu Dhabi Falcon Hospital, near Zayed International Airport, has a falcon display museum and offers guided tours. Check booking arrangements before you go.

**Can I take photos at a falconry display?**
Usually, yes, but the UK Foreign Office advises not photographing people without their permission, so ask the falconer and other guests first.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Is the falconry a full display with a falconer who explains the birds, or a short photo stop?
- Where does the display take place, and is it inside a conservation reserve or managed area?
- How many guests usually attend, and will there be time to ask the falconer questions?
- Will we be able to hold a falcon, and what safety guidance do you give, especially for children?
- How are the birds rested and cared for between displays?
- Can you arrange a guided visit to a falcon hospital, and how far ahead do we need to book?
