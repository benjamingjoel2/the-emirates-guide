The falconer slips off the small leather hood, and for a second the bird simply looks. Then it lifts from the glove, drops low over the sand and climbs again, and everyone watching forgets about their phone for a moment.

Falconry in the UAE is not a show put on for tourists. It is a tradition that families still practise, that shaped how people once fed themselves in the desert, and that the country works hard to keep alive. The good news for visitors is that you can see it up close without any special connections. A desert display, a guided visit to a falcon hospital, or a reserve trip with a falconer who talks you through each bird will all give you a real feel for it.

Here is what falconry means in the Emirates, where you can watch it, what a visit actually involves, and how to enjoy it well.

## Why falconry in the UAE matters so much

Spend a few days in the country and you will notice the falcon everywhere, in heritage displays, artwork and logos. The bird runs through the culture.

<photo-set layout="inset-left" keys="falconry-in-the-uae-1" alts="Close portrait of a dark-hooded falcon with a speckled chest and pale blue beak against a warm sandy background" caption="A falcon photographed at a falconers' club in Abu Dhabi."></photo-set>

Visit Abu Dhabi calls falconry [one of the UAE's most treasured traditions](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/falconry), practised for over 2,000 years and passed down through generations. It explains that falcons played a vital role in Bedouin life, hunting bustard, curlews and other animals for food. In a landscape where food was hard won, a well-trained bird could make the difference. The same page describes falcons as a cultural symbol of the UAE, valued for their skill and their individual personalities.

The tourism board also notes that the late Sheikh Zayed bin Sultan Al Nahyan, the UAE's founding father, wrote about the practice in *Falconry as a Sport, Our Arab Heritage*, describing how early hunters caught and trained their birds using simple, time-honoured techniques. That a founding leader wrote a book about it tells you a great deal about its place in national identity.

### A heritage recognised by UNESCO

Falconry is also recognised internationally. The UAE is one of the submitting states for [falconry on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/falconry-a-living-human-heritage-01708), in an inscription dated 2021 that brings together many countries. UNESCO describes falconry as the traditional art of training and flying falcons, and sometimes eagles, hawks and other birds of prey, and says it has been practised for over 4,000 years worldwide.

The same entry makes a point that is easy to miss: the modern practice focuses on safeguarding falcons, quarry and habitats, as well as the practice itself. UNESCO notes that skills pass from generation to generation through mentoring, within families or in training clubs, and that the wider falconry community includes falcon hospitals, breeding centres, conservation agencies and traditional equipment makers.

### Falcons and conservation

That conservation thread is visible in the UAE. The Abu Dhabi Falcon Hospital says the [Sheikh Zayed Falcon Release Program](https://www.falconhospital.com/services/falcon-services/falcon-release-programme/) was introduced in 1995 by Sheikh Zayed, with the idea of returning wild falcons to nature to help preserve the natural population. The hospital handles the medical side of the programme, rehabilitating wild peregrine and saker falcons before release.

According to the hospital, more than 2,274 falcons have been released so far, at locations such as Pakistan, Iran and Kazakhstan that sit on the natural migration routes of wild falcons. The birds are taken from the UAE each spring, release areas are chosen in unpopulated regions with food and water nearby, and several falcons carry satellite transmitters so researchers can follow their flight paths and learn how many survive. It is a useful thing to know before you watch a display: the people who keep these birds are often closely involved in protecting their wild cousins too.

## The bond between falconer and bird

What surprises most first-time watchers is how calm everything is. There is no shouting. A falconer moves slowly, speaks quietly, and the bird responds.

Visit Abu Dhabi describes falconers training their birds [with patience and care](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/falconry), creating a deep bond between human and falcon. UNESCO lists the shared elements that falconers across the world have in common: [the methods of breeding, training and caring for birds, the equipment used and the bonds between the falconer and the bird](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/falconry-a-living-human-heritage-01708).

Watch for a few details during any display:

- **The hood.** A small leather cap covers the bird's eyes and keeps it calm until it is time to fly. Many are finely stitched and decorated, so look closely when the bird is resting.
- **The glove.** The falconer carries the bird on a thick glove, which protects the hand from talons.
- **The lure.** A weighted lure swung on a line gives the bird something to chase in the air, so you see its speed and turns without any live quarry.
- **The perch.** Between flights, birds rest on a low perch or block. A bird that is fluffing its feathers and settling is usually relaxed.
- **The quiet.** Notice how little the falconer needs to say. Much of the communication is in movement and routine.

If the falconer invites questions, ask about the bird's name, its age and how long it took to train. The answers are often the best part of the visit.

## How a falconry display usually unfolds

Formats differ between operators and venues, but most good displays follow a similar shape, and knowing it helps you get more from the time.

First comes an introduction while the bird sits hooded on the glove. This is when the falconer explains the species, the equipment and something of the bird's character, and when the group gathers round. The Dubai Desert Conservation Reserve describes this part well: an expert falconer [discusses the breed and character of each bird](https://www.ddcr.org/what-to-do/) while you watch.

Then the hood comes off and the bird flies. Usually it circles, stoops at the lure and is called back, sometimes several times. Stand where the falconer asks you to, since the flight path is planned around the group. Keep your eyes on the bird rather than your screen for at least one pass; the speed is easier to appreciate in person than on a replay.

Finally there is time for questions and, where offered, photographs with the bird. This last part is often the most relaxed, and it is the right moment to ask about training, diet or how the bird spends the hotter months.

## Where you can watch falconry

You do not need to know a falconer to see one at work. These are the most straightforward ways in.

### On a desert trip

Visit Abu Dhabi says [most desert safari experiences offer a chance to discover falconry](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/falconry), and that falcons also appear at cultural shows in several destinations. Quality varies, so ask before you book whether the falconry is a proper display with time to talk, or a quick photo stop. Our guide to [whether a desert safari is worth it](/stories/is-a-desert-safari-worth-it/) explains how different safari styles compare.

### In a conservation reserve

The Dubai Desert Conservation Reserve, which describes itself as [the UAE's first national park](https://www.ddcr.org/what-to-do/), lists falconry among its activities, describing a display of the birds' speed and natural instincts and a chance to see and photograph the falcons up close. The reserve also runs an approved tours scheme, with guides certified by the reserve and tours expected to consider sustainability and waste. Its list of activities even includes a hot air balloon ride that takes in a falcon show from the air; our [hot air balloon guide](/stories/hot-air-balloon-uae-desert/) explains how those mornings work. Our [Dubai Desert Conservation Reserve guide](/stories/dubai-desert-conservation-reserve-guide/) covers the reserve in more detail.

### At a falcon hospital

For a different angle, Abu Dhabi has a hospital just for falcons. Visit Abu Dhabi says the [Abu Dhabi Falcon Hospital](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/falconry) is located near Zayed International Airport, features a falcon display museum and offers guided tours. The hospital itself says it [opened on 3 October 1999](https://www.falconhospital.com/) as the first public institution in the UAE to provide comprehensive veterinary care exclusively for falcons, and that it receives around 11,200 falcons a year for examination and treatment.

Seeing the medical side of falconry, and how carefully the birds are looked after, adds depth that a desert display alone cannot.

### Further into the desert

If your trip takes you deep into the dunes, perhaps on a drive out to Liwa, ask whether your camp or guide can arrange a falconry display. Our [Liwa and Empty Quarter guide](/stories/liwa-empty-quarter-guide/) helps you plan the journey.

## What a falcon hospital tour involves

A hospital tour is the best choice if you want to understand falconry rather than simply watch it, and it works well on a day when the desert is too hot or you have no car.

The hospital's own tour page lists what is included. You visit a [falconry museum with displays of traditional tools and equipment](https://www.falconhospital.com/services/adfh-tourism/abu-dhabi-falcon-hospital-tour/), learn how falcons are registered and about their falcon passports, and go into the examination room, where you may see birds having treatments such as feather repair or what the hospital calls a pedicure. The tour continues to a large free-flight aviary where falcons fly freely inside, and ends with the chance to be photographed with a falcon on your arm. The page also mentions an optional lunch buffet.

Two practical points stand out. All tours have to be booked in advance, and the hospital asks for around a week's notice for groups and two days for individual visitors. The home page also shows different tour packages, so check which one you are booking and what it includes before you travel.

Because the hospital is a working medical facility, follow your guide's lead closely. Birds are there for treatment, and staff will decide how close you can get.

## Photos, distance and good manners

A falcon on a glove is one of the most photogenic sights in the country, and most falconers expect cameras. Still, a few habits make the experience better for everyone, including the bird.

- **Ask before photographing people.** The UK Foreign Office advises visitors in the UAE [not to photograph people without their permission](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security). A falconer at a public display will usually be happy to pose, but ask first, and take extra care around families or anyone not part of the show.
- **Let the falconer lead.** Only hold a bird or step closer when invited, and follow instructions about how to hold your arm.
- **Keep still and quiet.** Sudden movements, waving and loud voices can unsettle the bird.
- **No flash.** Turn off your flash, especially at evening displays.
- **Keep children close.** Talons and beaks are sharp, and the bird is not a pet.

If you are offered a falcon on your own gloved arm, it is usually a brief, calm moment. Hold the arm steady, keep your elbow close to your body and let the falconer do the rest.

### Falcons on the street

In busy tourist areas you may come across a handler with a falcon offering photographs. These encounters are not the same as a display and come with no explanation of the bird or its care. If you want a picture, a reserve display or a hospital tour gives you a better setting and a better sense of the tradition behind it.

## Planning tips: timing, families and access

A few practical points help a falconry outing go smoothly.

- **Pick the cooler part of the day.** Outdoor displays are more comfortable early or late in the day, and in the cooler months of the year. Ask your operator when theirs takes place and how much shade there is.
- **Bring sun protection.** Hats, sunglasses and water make even a short outdoor display easier, especially for children.
- **Think about children's ages.** Older children usually love holding a bird, while toddlers may find the waiting hard. A hospital tour, with an indoor museum and aviary, can suit younger families better than a long desert evening.
- **Ask about access.** Desert displays take place on sand, which is hard going for wheelchairs and pushchairs. If anyone in your group has limited mobility, ask whether the display can be watched from a firm surface or a vehicle, and ask the hospital in advance about routes through the building.
- **Combine it sensibly.** A reserve display fits naturally into a desert afternoon or an overnight camp. A hospital tour pairs easily with a day of sightseeing in Abu Dhabi.

## Quick answers

**Can tourists see falconry in the UAE?**
Yes. Visit Abu Dhabi says most desert safari experiences offer a chance to discover falconry, and the Dubai Desert Conservation Reserve lists falconry displays among its activities.

**Why are falcons important in the UAE?**
Falcons once helped Bedouin communities hunt for food, and falconry has been practised in the region for over 2,000 years, according to Visit Abu Dhabi. It is now recognised by UNESCO as intangible cultural heritage, with the UAE among the submitting states.

**Is there a falcon hospital visitors can tour?**
Yes. The Abu Dhabi Falcon Hospital, near Zayed International Airport, offers guided tours with a falconry museum, the examination room and a free-flight aviary. Tours must be booked in advance.

**Can I take photos at a falconry display?**
Usually, yes, but the UK Foreign Office advises not photographing people without their permission, so ask the falconer and other guests first.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Is the falconry a full display with a falconer who explains the birds, or a short photo stop?
- Where does the display take place, and is it inside a conservation reserve or managed area?
- What time of day is the display, and is there shade or seating?
- How many guests usually attend, and will there be time to ask the falconer questions?
- Will we be able to hold a falcon, and what safety guidance do you give, especially for children?
- How are the birds rested and cared for between displays?
- Can you arrange a guided visit to a falcon hospital, and how far ahead do we need to book?
