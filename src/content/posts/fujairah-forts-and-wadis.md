Stand on the slope below Fujairah Fort in the early morning and the town seems to fall away beneath you. Palm tops, flat roofs, a minaret, then the line of the sea. Turn around and the view is all rock: the Hajar Mountains, rising in folds, with dry valleys cut deep between them.

Those two views explain Fujairah. People built forts to watch the coast and the mountain passes, and the passes themselves are wadis, the stony valleys that carry water down to the sea when it rains. Fujairah's forts and wadis are best seen together, on a slow day of short drives, short walks and long looks. Go in the cooler months, start early, and treat rain in the mountains as the one thing that can change your plans entirely.

## Why Fujairah built so many forts

The east coast was always a meeting point of sea routes and mountain roads, and it needed watching. The Fujairah Tourism and Antiquities Authority describes an emirate [rich in archaeological sites](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-MuseumAndForts.html), including castles, forts, towers and mosques, and explains that the forts were used for defence, to repel invaders and to protect the land and its identity.

The [UAE government portal](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/the-seven-emirates/fujairah) dates Fujairah's modern history to 1808, when the leader of the Al Sharqi tribe built his castle and the old city around it. Walk through the forts below with that in mind: each was placed to see something, whether a stretch of coast, a valley floor or a road through the mountains. The tourism authority says some of the emirate's archaeological treasures date back about five centuries.

The landscape explains the rest. The authority's [facts page](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-FactsAndStatistics.html) describes Fujairah as the only emirate that is almost totally mountainous, and the one with the highest annual rainfall in the UAE. Water from those mountains fed farms and palm groves in the valleys, and the routes along the valleys linked the coast to the interior. Whoever controlled the passes controlled both.

## Fujairah Fort: the one to see first

### A fort shaped by its rock

PHOTOSET1

Fujairah Fort, one of the largest in the emirate, stands on a high hill in the old Fujairah area, overlooking the city. The authority puts it at [about 20 metres above sea level and three kilometres from the coast](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-MuseumAndForts.html). According to the [tourism authority](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-MuseumAndForts.html), its design differs completely from the usual layout of forts elsewhere in the country. It has three round towers and a fourth square tower, plus a tall building with a tower-like section, all linked by walls around a central hall. The authority puts the irregular layout down to the irregular rocks it was built on.

Look closely at the walls and you will see what the builders had to hand. The authority lists local stone, gravel, clay, hay and sarooj, a gypsum-based mortar. The result is a building that looks as though it grew out of the hill.

Give yourself time to walk around it rather than straight up to it. From the lower slopes you see how each tower is set on a different rock, and from the top you understand why it was built here: the view runs over the old town and palms to the sea.

### Pair it with the museum

The Fujairah Museum, nearby, is the best place to put the forts in context. The authority describes halls on traditional occupations such as farming, fishing, trade, weaving and pottery, a heritage hall with old weapons, costumes and household utensils, and antiquities found in excavations around the emirate. Check opening arrangements locally before you go.

## Forts in the valleys

Fujairah's other forts lie inland, along the routes that cross the mountains. They are smaller and quieter, and you may have them entirely to yourself.

### Al Bithna: guarding Wadi Ham

Al Bithna fort stands near the village of the same name, overlooking Wadi Ham. The authority notes it is [close to the main road](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-MuseumAndForts.html) that links Fujairah to the rest of the UAE, which makes it an easy stop on the way in or out of the emirate. It [dates the fort to 1735](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-MuseumAndForts.html) and describes a large, solid building with two watchtowers on its western side, surrounded by palm groves and mountains.

Stand by the walls and look along the valley, and you see at once why someone wanted a fort here. The authority places Al Bithna [only 13 kilometres from Fujairah city](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-MuseumAndForts.html), overlooking Wadi Ham where it runs across the mountains towards Wadi Siji.

### Al Hail: a hill above the wadi

Southwest of Fujairah city, Al Hail sits on a mountain above the west bank of the valley of the same name. The authority describes it as occupying an important strategic position at a meeting point of routes, with a two-storey building whose roof was used for watching and defence through openings and platforms. The surrounding Al Hail wadi is, in the authority's words, easily accessible and very close to the city.

The history here is layered. The authority says the original fortress stood on a mountain about [40 metres above the valley floor](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-MuseumAndForts.html), in a village about 13 kilometres southwest of Fujairah city, and that carbon-14 analysis dates it to 1930. After that fortress was demolished, a square tower was built on a hill about 15 metres above the ground, using gravel, burnt clay, palm leaves and strong wood for the ceilings. The authority notes that it clearly overlooks the area in all four directions.

### Awhala: high in rough country

Further into the mountains, the fort at Awhala sits in rugged, jagged country that the authority says is now reached by paved roads. Its round watchtower let guards [watch for anyone approaching](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-MuseumAndForts.html) from the sea or along the mountain roads. The authority also mentions rooms in the backyard, a majlis with decorated walls and a deep well. Of all the forts, this one gives the strongest sense of how remote the interior once felt.

The details are worth knowing before you arrive. The authority places Awhala [30 kilometres from Fujairah city](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-MuseumAndForts.html) and describes a gate about 1 metre wide and 2.5 metres high, a rectangular lobby with ladders to the upper floor, and a round tower 11 metres high and 9 metres across where guards and archers were stationed. It also suggests that the design recalls the walls of ancient Assyrian cities, a sign of how far this region's links once reached.

## Reading a fort: what to look for

You do not need to be a historian to enjoy these buildings, but a few things make them easier to read.

- **Where it stands.** Ask what the fort was watching: the sea, a valley floor, a junction of roads. The answer usually tells you why it is there.
- **The towers.** Round towers and square towers were built for watching and defence. At Fujairah Fort you see both in one complex.
- **The walls.** Look for local stone, gravel and clay, and for the gypsum mortar called sarooj. The authority lists these materials for Fujairah Fort.
- **Small openings.** High, narrow openings let in light and air and gave defenders a view out. At Al Hail, the authority describes openings and platforms on the roof used for surveillance and defence.
- **Water.** A well, as at Awhala, meant a fort could hold out. In a valley, the palms and farms around it tell you where the water was.

## Fujairah's wadis: what they are and where to go

Wadis are dry riverbeds for most of the year. Gravel, boulders, oleander and the odd palm; sometimes a pool reflecting the cliffs. Then, after rain, they can become rivers within minutes.

### The Hajar Mountains

The authority's [natural sites page](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-NaturalSites.html) describes the Hajar as the highest mountain range in the eastern Arabian Peninsula, running through both the UAE and Oman, with hiking and tours on offer to the west of Fujairah. It notes that the highest named point in the UAE, Jabal Yibir, at [1,527 metres](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-NaturalSites.html), lies in the same range. The [UAE government portal](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/the-seven-emirates/fujairah) adds that Fujairah's land is irrigated by rainwater from these mountains, and that the emirate has several hot, cold and mineral springs.

### Wadi Ham: the long valley

The same page calls Wadi Ham the [largest and longest wadi in the UAE](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-NaturalSites.html), running for 30 km from Masafi to the dam, with much of it alongside a main road, so access is easy. It notes the riverbed remains dry for most of the year, but when it rains it turns into a rushing wadi. Combine it with Al Bithna fort, which overlooks it.

### Wadi Al Wurayah: protected and precious

North of Fujairah city, Wadi Al Wurayah is a boulder-strewn valley that the authority says is protected because of its [unique flora and fauna](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-NaturalSites.html). The UAE government portal names the [Wadi Al Wurayah waterfalls](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/the-seven-emirates/fujairah) among the emirate's major attractions. The authority places it [42 km north of Fujairah city](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-NaturalSites.html), spanning 192 square kilometres, and says it is protected by EWS-WWF. Because it is protected, check what access is allowed before you go, keep to permitted paths, and leave nothing behind.

### Wadi Siji and the dams

The authority also describes Wadi Siji, in a mountainous farming area, as having [the oldest dam in the UAE](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-NaturalSites.html), built in the seventies. It gives the dam's size as 10 metres high and 500 metres wide, able to store up to 1,200,000 cubic metres of water, in a valley 49 km north of Fujairah city. Dams across several of Fujairah's wadis hold back floodwater after rain, and their lakes can be a peaceful stop.

### Wadi Tawian and Al Hail

Two smaller valleys round out the list. The authority describes Wadi Tawian as 5 km long, about 45 km from Fujairah and reached only by a rough mountain road, with its own dam; this is one to do with a 4x4 and a guide. Al Hail wadi, by contrast, runs for about 15 km and is very close to the city, which makes it the easiest to pair with a fort visit.

## Staying safe in the wadis

This part matters more than any viewpoint.

### Rain changes everything

The UK Foreign Office warns that [heavy rain in the UAE can cause flooding](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), and advises checking forecasts from the [National Centre of Meteorology](https://www.ncm.gov.ae/) and following local authorities. Rain falling high in the mountains can send water down a valley where the sky is still blue.

- Do not drive, walk or camp in a wadi bed if rain is forecast anywhere nearby.
- Never try to drive through flowing water, however shallow it looks.
- If water starts to rise or turns muddy, climb to higher ground straight away.
- Tell someone where you are going and when you expect to be back.

### Heat and terrain

Fujairah's tourism authority describes summer, from [May to September](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-Overview.html), as hot and sometimes humid, and October to April as very pleasant. Even in winter, the rocks hold heat by midday. Start early, carry more water than you think you need, and wear shoes with grip for loose stones. The authority also warns that [climbs in the Hajar can be treacherous](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-SportsAndCruises.html) and that climbers should be well prepared. Our guide to [staying safe in the UAE heat](/stories/uae-heat-safety-guide/) has more.

### Families and accessibility

Forts on hills mean steps, loose stones and uneven ground. Children usually enjoy the towers and the sense of adventure, but hold hands on the steeper paths and keep them away from unguarded edges. Visitors with limited mobility can still enjoy Al Bithna and Fujairah Fort from the roadside and lower paths, and the drive along Wadi Ham, much of it beside the main road, is a good way to see a wadi without walking far.

### Respecting villages and farms

Many wadis pass through villages and farms. Drive slowly, park considerately, and keep out of private gardens and irrigation channels. The Foreign Office also reminds visitors [not to photograph people without their permission](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security).

## A simple day of Fujairah's forts and wadis

Start at Fujairah Fort in the early light, then visit the museum. Drive inland to Al Bithna and walk a short way along Wadi Ham, and finish with a slow drive back towards the coast for a late lunch. On a second day, head north for [Al Bidya Mosque](/stories/al-bidya-mosque-guide/) and the coast road.

### If you have two or three days

- **Day one, the south.** Fujairah Fort and museum in the morning, Al Hail fort and its wadi in the afternoon, and the Corniche at sunset.
- **Day two, the mountains.** Al Bithna and Wadi Ham early, then on to Awhala in the rougher country inland. Take a guide if you are unsure of the roads.
- **Day three, the north.** Wadi Siji or Wadi Al Wurayah, depending on access, then Al Bidya Mosque and the beaches on the way back.

The tourism authority's [sports and cruises page](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-SportsAndCruises.html) also mentions walking in the wadis and mountains and cycling on the emirate's winding roads, if you would like to see the valleys more slowly.

## What catches people out

- **Thinking a dry wadi is safe.** It is dry until it is not. Rain upstream can arrive without warning.
- **Starting too late.** By late morning the rock is hot and the light flat. Forts and wadis are best at the start of the day.
- **Assuming every fort is open inside.** Some are best seen from the outside, and access can change. Check locally.
- **Rough roads.** Some valleys, such as Wadi Tawian, are reached only by rough mountain tracks. A normal hire car is not the right tool.
- **Rubbish and graffiti.** Protected valleys and old buildings are fragile. Take everything home with you and leave walls untouched.

## Quick answers

**What is the main fort in Fujairah?** Fujairah Fort, on a hill above the old town, is one of the largest forts in the emirate, with three round towers and one square tower, according to the Fujairah Tourism and Antiquities Authority.

**Which is the longest wadi in the UAE?** The tourism authority describes Wadi Ham as the largest and longest wadi in the UAE, running for 30 km from Masafi to the dam.

**Which is the oldest fort to visit?** Of the forts in this guide, the tourism authority gives the earliest date to Al Bithna, built in 1735.

**Is it safe to visit wadis after rain?** Not straight away. The UK Foreign Office warns that heavy rain can cause flooding, so check National Centre of Meteorology forecasts and stay out of wadi beds when rain is possible.

**When is the best time to explore Fujairah's wadis?** The cooler months from October to April, which the tourism authority describes as very pleasant.

- Which forts are open to visitors on our dates, and can we go inside or only see them from outside?
- How will you check rain forecasts and wadi conditions on the day, and what is the backup plan?
- Do any of the roads to Awhala or the wadis need a 4x4?

- How long and how steep are the walks you suggest, and is there any shade?
- Can a guide explain the history of the forts and how they worked as a defensive network?
