The road climbs out of the flat sand, threads between bare brown peaks and then, quite suddenly, tips you towards the sea. Not the Gulf you left behind in Dubai, but a second coast, facing east, where fishing boats sit on the water and the mountains come almost down to the beach.

That is the first surprise of Fujairah. The second is how unhurried it feels. This Fujairah travel guide is for anyone wondering whether the UAE's east coast is worth the drive. In short: yes, if you want mountains, wadis, old forts, the country's oldest mosque and clear water for snorkelling, and you are happy to trade glamour for space. A night or two lets you see the highlights without rushing; a long day trip gives you a taste.

## Why Fujairah feels different from the rest of the UAE

Fujairah sits on its own side of the country. The [UAE government portal](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/the-seven-emirates/fujairah) describes it as the only emirate located completely on the east coast, along the Gulf of Oman, with a shoreline running from Fujairah city in the south to Dibba in the north. Its neighbours are Sharjah and Ras Al Khaimah to the west, and Oman on its northern border. The same page notes that its position gives the UAE its only access to the Indian Ocean, and that the emirate's economy is based on fishing and agriculture, with land irrigated by rainwater from the mountains.

The landscape is the draw. The portal lists rugged mountains, valleys, waterfalls, oases and wide sandy beaches, plus hot, cold and mineral springs. It also notes that, because of its easterly position, Fujairah's climate is more moderate than that of Abu Dhabi and Dubai, which is why weekend visitors from the cities come here to slow down.

The tourism authority's own [facts page](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-FactsAndStatistics.html) adds two points that shape any visit: Fujairah is the only emirate that is almost totally mountainous, and it receives the highest annual rainfall in the UAE. That rain is what fills the wadis and waters the date gardens, and it is also why flash floods are the one hazard you must plan around. The authority's [natural sites page](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-NaturalSites.html) notes that the highest named point in the UAE, Jabal Yibir, lies in the same Hajar range.

## When to visit Fujairah

The [Fujairah Tourism and Antiquities Authority](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-Overview.html) describes summer, from May to September, as hot and sometimes humid, and the months from October to April as very pleasant. Those cooler months are when walking in the wadis, climbing to forts and lying on the beach all feel comfortable.

Summer still has its fans, mostly for the sea, but plan anything on land for early morning. Our [month-by-month guide](/stories/best-time-to-visit-uae-month-by-month/) compares the whole country through the year.

## Fujairah city: a fort, a museum and a heritage village

Start in the old part of the city, where a fort rises from a rocky hill above the palms.

### Fujairah Fort

The authority's [museums and forts page](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-MuseumAndForts.html) calls Fujairah Fort one of the important and largest forts in the emirate. It stands on a high hill in the old Fujairah area, about 20 metres above sea level and three kilometres from the coast, looking over the whole city. Its design is unusual: three round towers and a fourth square tower, linked by walls, with a layout shaped by the irregular rocks it was built on. The builders used local stone, gravel, clay, hay and sarooj, a traditional gypsum mortar.

Walk around the outside first to see how the towers grip the rock, then look back towards the mountains. The ground around old forts is uneven, so anyone with limited mobility may prefer to enjoy the walls from the lower paths.

### Fujairah Museum

Nearby, the museum helps you make sense of what you will see on the road. According to the same page, one hall covers traditional work such as farming, fishing and trade, along with weaving and pottery; another shows old weapons, costumes, household utensils and a model of a palm-leaf house; and the antiquities halls hold finds from excavations around the emirate, including steatite pots and brass objects. Among the weapons, the authority mentions [an old rifle made in 1916](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-MuseumAndForts.html). Check visiting arrangements locally before you go.

### Heritage Village, Ain Al Madhab and the Corniche

The authority's [recreation places page](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-RecreationPlaces.html) describes the Heritage Village in the Madhab district, established in 1996, with models of traditional homes and tools used by Fujairah's people in the past, all inside a high wall topped by watchtowers. Ain Al Madhab Park sits nearby, close to the mountains. Down by the water, the Corniche is where residents walk, exercise and socialise, and the page notes it is usually busy during the cooler months.

The same page mentions bull butting, a local family event held on Fridays in an open field. If you go, keep a respectful distance and ask before photographing people.

### A newer landmark

Fujairah city also has a very modern place of worship. The authority's [places of worship page](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-PlacesOfWorship.html) describes the Sheikh Zayed Mosque in Fujairah as modelled on its namesake in Abu Dhabi and calls it the second largest mosque in the UAE. Seeing it on the same trip as the tiny mosque at Al Bidya is a good way to understand how much the country has changed.

## North along the coast: Al Bidya and Dibba

Drive north and the coast road runs between the sea and the mountains.

### Al Bidya Mosque

The highlight for many visitors is a small mosque that is easy to drive past. The same places of worship page describes Al Bidya Mosque as the oldest mosque in the UAE, built from local stone and burnt clay, with four domes held up by a single central column and no timber in the roof. It is still a place of worship, so dress modestly and visit quietly. Our full guide to [visiting Al Bidya Mosque](/stories/al-bidya-mosque-guide/) explains what to look for inside.

### Dibba, where three territories meet

At the northern end of the coast, Dibba is a town shared three ways. [Visit Sharjah](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/regions/east-coast/dibba-al-hisn/) explains that Dibba Al Hisn belongs to Sharjah, Dibba Al Baya to the north is part of Oman, and Dibba Muhallab to the south belongs to Fujairah. Expect a harbour, a fort, markets and boats heading out for snorkelling and fishing. Do not cross into Oman without checking the entry rules first.

### Khorfakkan on the way

Between the two, you will pass through Khorfakkan, which belongs to Sharjah rather than Fujairah. Visit Sharjah places it [about halfway between Dibba and Fujairah](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/beaches/khorfakkan-beach/), on a crescent-shaped beach with water sports and boat trips. It is worth a stop for lunch and a walk along the seafront.

## Into the Hajar: Fujairah's wadis

Turn inland and the mountains close in fast. The natural sites page describes the Hajar Mountains as the highest range in the eastern Arabian Peninsula, running through both the UAE and Oman.

Wadis, the dry valleys that cut through them, are the reason many people come. The page describes Wadi Al Wurayah as a boulder-strewn valley protected for its unique flora and fauna, and Wadi Ham as a riverbed that stays dry for most of the year but turns into a rushing stream when it rains. It gives [Wadi Ham a length of 30 km](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-NaturalSites.html), from Masafi to the dam, with much of it alongside a main road. The u.ae portal names the Wadi Al Wurayah waterfalls among the emirate's major attractions.

### Respect the rain

That rushing water is the thing to plan around. The UK Foreign Office warns that [heavy rain in the UAE can cause flooding](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) and advises checking forecasts from the [National Centre of Meteorology](https://www.ncm.gov.ae/) and following local authorities. Never drive, walk or camp in a wadi bed when rain is possible, even if the sky above you is clear. Our guide to [Fujairah's forts and wadis](/stories/fujairah-forts-and-wadis/) goes into more detail.

### Inland forts along the way

If you drive in through Wadi Ham, the authority describes [Al Bithna fort, dating from 1735](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-MuseumAndForts.html), close to the main road that links Fujairah with the rest of the country. Al Hail fort stands on a mountain above its own wadi, southwest of the city, and the fort at Awhala sits further out in rough, jagged country that is now reached by paved roads. Each makes a short stop that breaks up a drive and shows why these valleys needed watching.

## On and under the water

Fujairah's sea is a big part of its appeal. The authority's [sports and cruises page](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-SportsAndCruises.html) lists swimming, diving, snorkelling, fishing, kayaking and parasailing, and notes that most hotels can arrange the water sport of your choice. The facts page describes diving as one of the best sports in the emirate, with diverse coral reefs. The sports page says the Hajar attract climbers too, but warns that climbs can be treacherous and you should be well prepared.

For swimming, the Foreign Office advises that [rip currents can occur at any beach](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), that red flags mean swimming is dangerous, and that you should only swim from approved beaches with lifeguards. On boats, make sure life jackets are available for everyone. Our guide to [snorkelling and diving on the east coast](/stories/snorkelling-diving-uae-east-coast/) covers the underwater side.

## How a day in Fujairah actually unfolds

The rhythm here follows the sun. Mornings are the time for anything on foot: the fort, a wadi walk, the climb to the watchtowers at Al Bidya. By late morning the rock is already warm, so most people drift towards the coast, the museum or a long lunch of grilled fish.

The middle of the day is for shade, a swim from a beach with lifeguards, or a nap. In the cooler months, the light softens from mid-afternoon and the Corniche fills up as families come out to walk. Evenings are relaxed rather than lively; this is not a place for nightlife, and that is much of its charm.

If you are coming from Dubai or Sharjah for the day, leave early so that you reach the mountains before the heat, and plan to drive back before dark if you are not used to mountain roads. Two nights let you spend one day in the south around Fujairah city and Wadi Ham, and one in the north around Al Bidya, Dibba and the beaches. Our guide to [where to stay on the east coast](/stories/where-to-stay-uae-east-coast/) compares the main bases.

### South to Kalba

If you have an extra half day, head south. [Visit Sharjah](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/regions/east-coast/kalba/) places Kalba around 12 km south of Fujairah city and describes the mangroves at Khor Kalba as the oldest in the UAE, with kayaking, wildlife spotting and a bird of prey centre. It also mentions a museum in a house built in 1901 that once served as a royal residence. Kalba belongs to Sharjah, so the rules on alcohol change as you cross into it, as they do in Khorfakkan and Dibba Al Hisn.

## Food, crafts and local life

Fujairah's tourism authority describes the region's food on its [customs and traditions page](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-CustomsAndTraditions.html) as built around lamb, fish and rice, with biryani among the most popular dishes and local fish such as hammour on many menus. On the coast, the simplest meal is often the best: whatever the boats brought in, grilled, with rice and salad.

Look out too for the traditional crafts that shaped life here. The authority's [occupations and crafts page](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-OccupationsAndCrafts.html) describes pottery made in mountain areas from red, green and yellow clay, and the shasha, a fishing boat made from palm fronds that are dried, soaked in sea water and bound with rope. It also describes rope twisted from palm fibre and fish traps woven from palm leaves. You may see some of these at the museum or the Heritage Village, and they make the old fishing harbours easier to read.

## Getting to Fujairah and getting around

The authority's [getting there page](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-GettingThere.html) describes road links, including Sheikh Khalifa Street, that connect Fujairah with Sharjah and Dubai, an international airport offering seasonal flights and daily domestic flights, and a port that receives cruise ships in the tourism season.

Once here, a car makes life far easier, because the sights are spread along the coast and up the valleys. The Foreign Office notes that driving standards are not always as disciplined as in the UK and that [speeding is common](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), so take mountain roads steadily. If you would rather not drive, a guide or driver for a day is a good compromise. Our [east coast road trip](/stories/uae-east-coast-road-trip/) links Fujairah, Khorfakkan and Dibba into one loop.

## What catches people out

- **Underestimating the drive.** The mountain roads are good, but the sights are spread out. Pick a southern day and a northern day rather than trying to see everything at once.
- **Wadi weather.** Rain in the mountains can send water down a valley under a clear sky. Check the forecast every morning you plan to go inland.
- **Two emirates at once.** Khorfakkan, Kalba and Dibba Al Hisn belong to Sharjah. The Foreign Office notes that drinking or possessing alcohol is [illegal in the Emirate of Sharjah](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security).
- **The Oman border.** Dibba is split, and the northern part is Omani. Make sure you know where the border lies before you follow a road or a boat north.
- **Prayer times.** Mosques, including Al Bidya, are busy at prayer times. Plan visits around them, and step outside if a prayer begins.

## Being a good guest on the east coast

Life here is more traditional than in Dubai's tourist districts. The Foreign Office asks visitors to [dress modestly in public areas](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), with women covering shoulders and knees, and to wear swimwear only on beaches or at pools. It also warns not to photograph people without their permission.

In practice, that means a cover-up for the walk from beach to café, a scarf in your bag for mosque visits, and a quick question before you point a camera at fishermen at the harbour.

### Families and emergencies

Fujairah suits families well: short drives, beaches, forts to scramble around (with a hand held on the steeper paths) and early nights. Bring hats, plenty of water and reef-friendly habits for the snorkelling. For emergencies, the Foreign Office lists the numbers as [999 for police, 998 for an ambulance and 997 for fire](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/getting-help). ## Quick answers

**Is Fujairah worth visiting?** Yes, if you want mountains, wadis, forts and a quieter coast. The UAE government portal describes rugged mountains, valleys, waterfalls, oases and wide sandy beaches, and a climate more moderate than Abu Dhabi and Dubai.

**What is the best time to visit Fujairah?** The Fujairah Tourism and Antiquities Authority describes the months from October to April as very pleasant, and May to September as hot and sometimes humid.

**What is Fujairah known for?** Its east coast setting on the Gulf of Oman, the Hajar Mountains and wadis, Fujairah Fort, and Al Bidya Mosque, which the tourism authority describes as the oldest mosque in the UAE.

**Can you swim in Fujairah?** Yes, but swim only from approved beaches with lifeguards and flags, and stay out when red flags are flying, as the UK Foreign Office advises.

**Is Khorfakkan part of Fujairah?** No. Khorfakkan, like Kalba and Dibba Al Hisn, belongs to Sharjah, though it sits on the same coast.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Can you plan our days so that forts and wadi walks fall in the cooler early morning?
- How will you check rain forecasts and wadi conditions before we head inland?
- Which beaches near our base have lifeguards and flags, and which boat trips carry life jackets for every passenger?
- Can a guide explain the history at Fujairah Fort and Al Bidya Mosque on site?
- Is the Fujairah Museum open on the day we plan to visit?
- If we go as far as Dibba, how do we make sure we stay on the UAE side of the border?
