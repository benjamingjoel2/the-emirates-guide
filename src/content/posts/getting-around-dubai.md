The train glides out of the station with nobody at the front. No driver, no cab, just a wide window and the city sliding past below: towers, highways, then a flash of the Creek. For many visitors, the Dubai Metro is the first surprise of getting around Dubai.

Getting around Dubai is easier than its size suggests. The short answer: use the metro and tram for the long runs along the city's length, licensed taxis for door-to-door trips and late nights, and the abras for crossing the Creek. Buy a nol card early, because you will use it on almost everything. This guide takes each option in turn, with the rules that catch visitors out, notes on accessibility and travelling with children, and an example of how a day's journeys fit together.

## Getting around Dubai at a glance

- **Metro:** fast and air-conditioned, best for long distances along the city's spine.
- **Tram:** links the metro with the Marina, JBR and the Palm Monorail.
- **Taxis:** licensed taxis are everywhere and can be hailed or booked.
- **Buses:** useful for gaps in the rail network, and for intercity trips.
- **Abras, ferries and water taxis:** the Creek crossing is a short pleasure; other boats are scenic extras.
- **Walking:** good within neighbourhoods, less so between them, especially in the heat.

## The Dubai Metro: your fastest way across the city

RTA describes Dubai Metro as running on [the Red and Green lines](https://www.rta.ae/wps/portal/rta/ae/public-transport/metro/about-metro), with a Blue Line approved and scheduled for completion in 2029. The UAE government portal calls it a [driverless and fully automated metro rail network](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/transportation/Modes-of-transport/public-transport).

RTA describes the planned Blue Line as a [30 km route with 14 stations](https://www.rta.ae/wps/portal/rta/ae/public-transport/metro/about-metro), interchanging with the Green Line at Creek Station and the Red Line at Centrepoint Station. For now, almost every visitor trip uses the Red Line, which runs the length of the city, or the Green Line, which serves the older districts around the Creek.

### Choosing your carriage

Each train has different types of cabin. According to RTA, [Gold Class cabins are for gold class ticket holders only](https://www.rta.ae/wps/portal/rta/ae/public-transport/metro/about-metro), with wider seats, and there are cabins reserved for women and children. Everyone else uses the general cabins. Look for the signs on the platform and on the floor so you board at the right door.

### Luggage and good manners

RTA allows [two suitcases per passenger](https://www.rta.ae/wps/portal/rta/ae/public-transport/metro/about-metro), one large and one small, kept in the luggage area of each cabin. It gives maximum sizes of 81cm by 58cm by 30cm for the large case and 55cm by 38cm by 20cm for the small one, so a big holiday suitcase may be over the limit. The same page lists fines for eating or drinking where it is prohibited, smoking, carrying alcohol and putting your feet on the seats, and says animals are not allowed except guide dogs. The etiquette is simple: let people off before you board, move into the cabin and do not push.

### Children travelling alone

RTA's rules say [children under 8 cannot use public transport without an adult](https://www.rta.ae/wps/portal/rta/ae/public-transport/metro/about-metro), children aged 8 to 11 need a permit from their parents, and those 12 and over can travel alone.

### Step-free travel

The metro is one of the easier systems to use with limited mobility. RTA lists [dedicated spaces for wheelchair users on all trains and lift access to all metro station levels](https://www.rta.ae/wps/portal/rta/ae/public-transport/metro/about-metro), as well as tactile guidance paths for people with difficulty seeing, audible beeps and flashing lights when doors open and close, and information on screens throughout trains and stations. The tram has [wheelchair spaces and emergency call boxes](https://www.rta.ae/wps/portal/rta/ae/public-transport/tram/about-tram) too.

## nol cards: one card for almost everything

Before your first ride, sort out how you will pay. Dubai uses the nol system, and RTA lists [four main options](https://www.rta.ae/wps/portal/rta/ae/public-transport/Nol-Home):

- **nol Red Ticket:** a paper ticket for the occasional traveller, sold at ticket vending machines and loadable with up to 10 single trips.
- **nol Silver Card:** described as "the perfect starter card", a smart card with an e-purse available at any ticket office.
- **nol Gold Card:** like the Silver Card, plus access to Gold Class cabins at a premium fare.
- **nol Blue Card:** a personalised smart card.

<photo-set layout="inset-left" keys="getting-around-dubai-2" alts="A nol card reader mounted on a pole inside a Dubai public bus" caption="Tap your nol card on readers like this one when you board and leave a bus."></photo-set>

For most short trips, a Silver Card is the simplest choice. RTA also lists online services for topping up and a journey planner. The government portal notes that you need a nol card or ticket for [water taxis, ferries and abras](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/transportation/Modes-of-transport/waterways) as well as the [tram](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/transportation/Modes-of-transport/public-transport). If you are travelling as a family, each person needs their own card or ticket, so buy them together on your first visit to a ticket office rather than queuing again at every station. Keep your card handy: RTA lists fines for not presenting a card when asked by an inspector.

## The tram: Marina, JBR and the Palm

Along the coast in the Marina, the tram fills the gap the metro leaves. RTA says it [links Dubai Metro and the Palm Monorail and runs along Al Sufouh Road and Jumeirah Beach Road](https://www.rta.ae/wps/portal/rta/ae/public-transport/tram/about-tram). It also has Gold Class and women and children cabins, and the same luggage allowance as the metro. The government portal describes it as [the first tram service outside Europe powered by a ground-based electric supply system](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/transportation/Modes-of-transport/public-transport).

Take care around the tracks. RTA lists fines for crossing tracks outside designated areas and for entering prohibited zones, so use the marked crossings. Trams are quiet, and on a busy promenade they can arrive sooner than you expect.

## Buses: filling in the gaps

Buses reach the neighbourhoods the rail lines miss. RTA's bus pages include [timetables by route number, a journey planner and a link to book intercity tickets](https://www.rta.ae/wps/portal/rta/ae/public-transport/bus/about-bus). The journey planner is the easiest way to see whether a bus beats a taxi for your trip, and the same page points to the S'hail app for planning journeys on your phone. Pay with your nol card, tapping in and out on the readers.

Buses are most useful for short links between a metro station and a beach, a park or a residential area, and for trips to other emirates. They are slower than the metro, so for long distances inside the city the train usually wins.

## Taxis and ride-hailing: door to door

A taxi is often the easiest way home after dinner, or from the metro to a beach hotel. The UK Foreign Office advises using [only licensed taxis or other recognised forms of public transport](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), including recognised ride-hailing apps. It says authorised Dubai taxis are painted cream with different coloured roofs, and that taxis with pink roofs have female drivers. There are also special taxis for women and children.

The UAE government portal says taxis [can be hailed from the roadside or booked in advance](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/transportation/Modes-of-transport/public-transport), including through RTA, and that booking charges apply and differ by emirate, type of taxi and time of day. In Dubai it lists booking by phone with RTA or through ride-hailing apps, and it notes special taxis for families and for people of determination as well as for women and children. The Foreign Office also advises women in particular to take care when travelling alone or at night, and not to accept lifts from strangers.

Have your destination ready on a map or with a nearby landmark, as addresses can be hard to describe. If you need a child seat, book ahead rather than hailing on the street.

## Abras, ferries and water taxis

### Crossing the Creek

Nothing beats the old way across Dubai Creek. RTA runs heritage abras on [four Creek routes](https://www.rta.ae/wps/portal/rta/ae/public-transport/marine/about-marine): Dubai Old Souq to Baniyas, Al Fahidi to Al Sabkha, Al Fahidi to Deira Old Souq, and Al Seef to Baniyas. The motorised traditional abra between Bur Dubai and Deira Old Souq accepts cash. You step down into a low wooden boat, sit on the central bench and are across in minutes. Our [beginner's guide to the abra](/stories/dubai-creek-abra-guide/) walks through the crossing step by step.

### Ferries and water taxis

RTA's Dubai Ferry is air-conditioned, [stroller-friendly and has restrooms](https://www.rta.ae/wps/portal/rta/ae/public-transport/marine/about-marine). It covers six main routes, including one intercity route, and seats up to 98 people, including two wheelchairs. In the Marina, the Dubai Water Taxi runs hop-on, hop-off between four stops and has a wheelchair area. There is also an air-conditioned abra between Al Jaddaf and Dubai Festival City, and heritage abras on the Dubai Water Canal, where RTA describes a round trip of about 45 minutes. These are as much a sightseeing trip as a way to get somewhere.

### Children on the water

The boats have stricter rules for children than the trains. RTA says [unaccompanied children under 12 are not allowed on its marine transport](https://www.rta.ae/wps/portal/rta/ae/public-transport/marine/about-marine), that children under 12 travelling with an adult must wear a life jacket on board, and that a valid ID for the child must be shown at the ticketing office at marine stations. Bring a copy of your child's passport if you plan a ferry or water taxi trip.

## Walking: shorter than it looks, hotter than you think

Within Al Fahidi, the Marina or Downtown, walking is a pleasure. Between them, it is rarely practical. Two things to know:

- The Foreign Office warns that it is [illegal to cross the road anywhere other than designated pedestrian crossings](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), and that you may face a fine or even imprisonment. It also says vehicles often do not stop at zebra crossings, so take care.
- Heat changes everything. The NHS advises [avoiding the sun between 11am and 3pm and drinking more cold drinks](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/heat-exhaustion-heatstroke/). Plan longer walks for the morning or evening, and use the air-conditioned metro for the middle of the day.

## Driving yourself

Most visitors to Dubai city do not need a car, but one helps for trips to the mountains or other emirates. The government portal suggests a [1.5 hour drive to Hatta](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/visiting-and-exploring-the-uae/Discover-the-UAE/explore-the-uae-by-emirate-) for its natural water pools, which is the kind of trip where a car earns its keep.

The Foreign Office says you can use a [UK photocard driving licence](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) to drive in the UAE, and warns that it is illegal to drink and drive, no matter how small the amount. It notes that driving standards are not always disciplined and speeding is common, that flashing headlights can mean a driver is coming through rather than giving way, and that offensive gestures and bad language towards other drivers can lead to fines, a jail sentence and possibly deportation. If you are in a crash in Dubai, it says to move your vehicle only if it is causing an obstruction, and to call the police in all cases.

Desert driving is a different skill. The Foreign Office says excursions to the desert can be dangerous unless you are in a properly equipped four-wheel drive, and advises travelling with other cars, carrying water and a phone, and leaving a copy of your plans with someone. Most visitors are better off with a guided trip. Our guide to [driving in the UAE as a visitor](/stories/driving-in-the-uae-as-a-visitor/) has more.

## How a day of journeys fits together

To see how the pieces connect, picture a typical first full day. You take the Green Line in the morning to the stations near the Creek, walk through Al Fahidi, and cross to Deira by abra for the souks. Around midday you take the metro back towards your hotel to rest out of the heat. In the late afternoon you ride the Red Line along the city, change to the tram for the Marina, and walk the promenade at sunset. After dinner you take a licensed taxi back, because the door-to-door ride is simpler at the end of a long day.

That pattern, rail for distance, boats for the Creek, feet for neighbourhoods and taxis for the last leg, works for most trips. Our [three days in Dubai](/stories/three-days-in-dubai/) itinerary is built around it.

## Ramadan, late nights and other timing tips

Transport timetables and taxi availability can shift during Ramadan, when evenings get busy around iftar. The Foreign Office notes that [driving may be erratic, particularly when people are trying to get home at dusk](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), and that eating and drinking in public in the daytime can cause offence during the holy month, so keep snacks out of sight on public transport. Our guide to [visiting the UAE during Ramadan](/stories/visiting-uae-during-ramadan/) explains what changes.

Where you stay makes a big difference too: see [where to stay in Dubai](/stories/where-to-stay-in-dubai/) for which neighbourhoods sit closest to the metro and tram.

### Emergencies on the move

Save the local emergency numbers before you set out. The Foreign Office lists [999 for police, 998 for an ambulance and 997 for fire](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/getting-help). If something goes wrong on the tram, RTA says [emergency call boxes are available in each train and station](https://www.rta.ae/wps/portal/rta/ae/public-transport/tram/about-tram). ## Quick answers

**Is the Dubai Metro easy for tourists?** Yes. It runs on the Red and Green lines, is driverless and air-conditioned, and has Gold Class and women and children cabins. Pay with a nol card or Red Ticket.

**Which nol card should I buy?** For most short trips, the nol Silver Card, sold at ticket offices, is the simplest. The Red Ticket is a paper option loadable with up to 10 single trips.

**Can you eat or drink on the Dubai Metro?** No, not where it is prohibited. RTA lists fines for eating or drinking in prohibited areas, as well as for smoking and carrying alcohol.

**Is the Dubai Metro wheelchair accessible?** RTA lists dedicated wheelchair spaces on all trains and lift access to all station levels, and the Dubai Ferry has space for two wheelchairs.

**Are Dubai taxis safe?** The UK Foreign Office advises using only licensed taxis or recognised apps. Licensed Dubai taxis are cream with coloured roofs.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Which of our planned sights are easiest by metro or tram, and which need a taxi or transfer?
- Can you arrange a driver for day trips outside the city, and what is included?
- Is a nol card enough for all the transport on our itinerary, or will we need cash for anything?
- How do transfer times change at rush hour or during Ramadan?
- Do your vehicles have child seats if we are travelling with young children?
- Are your vehicles suitable for a wheelchair user, if we need one?
- If we want to drive ourselves, what licence and insurance checks should we make?
