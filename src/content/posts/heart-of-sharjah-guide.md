Push open a heavy wooden door off the Corniche and the noise of the traffic simply stops. Inside, lanterns hang over a cool, covered alley, someone is pouring coffee from a brass pot, and the air smells faintly of oud.

That is Souq Al Arsah, and it is the best way into the Heart of Sharjah, the restored old quarter at the centre of the city. The [UAE government portal](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/the-seven-emirates/sharjah) lists it among the emirate's main historical areas. Its own site calls it [the revival of a cultural soul](https://www.heartofsharjah.ae/): coral-stone walls, wind towers and old pathways, with artisans still at work and museums in the former family homes.

So what is it like? Small, walkable and quiet, especially in the morning. You can see the main souks and two or three houses in half a day without rushing, and it rewards slowing down more than ticking things off.

## Getting your bearings in the Heart of Sharjah

The district runs along the waterfront between the Corniche and the creek. Most of what you will want sits within a short walk: the covered souks, a handful of house museums, the fort, the calligraphy square and the art houses. The newer Central Souk is a short taxi ride or a longer walk away.

The Heart of Sharjah site describes a [Cultural Heritage Tour](https://www.heartofsharjah.ae/) through the old alleys and restored homes, lasting one and a half to two hours with museum entry included, and says prior booking is required. If you like context, a guide turns a pleasant stroll into a story; if you prefer to wander, the lanes are easy to explore alone.

Most visitors arrive by taxi from elsewhere in Sharjah or from Dubai. Ask to be dropped on the Corniche by the souks and start on foot from there. The UK Foreign Office advises using [only licensed taxis](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) or recognised ride-hailing apps, and reminds walkers that crossing the road away from a designated pedestrian crossing is illegal. The Corniche road is busy, so use the crossings.

## Souq Al Arsah: where traders once met

Start here, because it sets the mood. [Visit Sharjah](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/shopping/souq-al-arsah/) describes it as the oldest marketplace in the history of the UAE, once a meeting point for traders from Persia, India and the Bedouin tribes. Today it is a covered, air-conditioned souk of wooden doors, coral brick walls and hanging lanterns. It is also known as Souk Al Masqoof.

Browse for copper coffee pots, pearl chests, jewellery, abayas and kandooras, perfume bottles and incense. When your feet need a rest, the café serves Arabic coffee, and the halwa shop is hard to walk past.

## How to read the buildings

Before you go into the houses, it helps to know what you are looking at. The Sharjah Museums Authority says the fort was built from [coral stones collected from the sea](https://www.sharjahmuseums.ae/en-us/museums/sharjah-fort-%28al-hisn%29), covered with light brown plaster, with doors of teak and ceilings made of mangrove trunks and stripped palm branches. The same materials appear all over the district, so once you have seen them in one building you will spot them everywhere.

Look up for ventilation. Square wind towers, or barjeel, caught the breeze and pushed it down into the rooms. Some houses used a different trick: the authority says Bait Al Naboodah has [wind scoops built into the walls](https://www.sharjahmuseums.ae/en-us/museums/bait-al-naboodah) instead of towers. Look, too, at the carved plaster panels and teak columns, which show how far the pearl merchants' trade links reached.

## The houses behind the walls

Step out of the souk and the district's real character is in its houses. Many were homes of pearl merchants and are now small museums.

### Sharjah Heritage Museum

Right opposite the souk, the [Sharjah Heritage Museum](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/heritage/sharjah-heritage-museum/) occupies a restored pearl merchant's house. Its six galleries cover how people lived across the emirate, from celebrations to oral traditions, with labels in English and Arabic.

### Bait Al Naboodah

Next door, [Bait Al Naboodah](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/heritage/bait-al-naboodah-museum/) was built in 1845 around a courtyard, with coral walls, a grand entrance, carved decoration and wind catchers. Walk upstairs, feel the shade of the verandas and the draught through the rooms, and you understand how the house stayed livable without air conditioning. The museums authority says its owner was a pearl merchant with trading ties in India, Africa and France, and that the house grew in phases as his family did.

### Sharjah Fort (Al Hisn)

Behind Souq Al Shanasiyah stands the building the district grew around. The authority says Al Hisn was built in 1823 as the seat of government and the home of the ruling Al Qasimi family, with two main floors, three defensive towers and a large central yard. Inside are photographs, weapons and archaeological finds, an early jail, and a display on how date syrup was made. It is the best single stop for understanding Sharjah's modern history.

### Majlis Al Midfa and its round wind tower

Most wind towers are square. The one at [Majlis Al Midfa](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/heritage/majlis-al-midfa/) is round, and Visit Sharjah says it is the only one of its kind in the UAE. The square was a gathering place for intellectuals and merchants, named after Ibrahim Bin Mohammed Al Midfa, who started the country's first newspaper in 1927 and held a majlis here to discuss poetry, literature and politics.

### Calligraphy Square

The [Sharjah Calligraphy Museum](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/culture/sharjah-calligraphy-museum/) stands on Calligraphy Square, in the historic house of Hamad Al Midfaa. Visit Sharjah says it is the only museum in the Arab world devoted entirely to calligraphy, built around a covered, light-filled courtyard.

### Al Qasimia School

Sharjah's first formal school is now a museum of education. The authority says [Al Qasimia School](https://www.sharjahmuseums.ae/en-us/museums/al-qasimia-school) opened as a museum in January 2025, with restored classrooms, original school books and photographs across six galleries. It is a small, surprisingly moving visit.

If you plan to see several of these, the authority's [heritage joint ticket](https://www.sharjahmuseums.ae/en-US/Visit/Heritage-Joint-Ticket) covers Bait Al Naboodah, the Heritage Museum, the Calligraphy Museum, the fort and Al Qasimia School, bought at any of them.

### The art houses

A few lanes away, the old houses take on a new life. [Bait Al Serkal](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/arts/bait-al-serkal-and-bait-obaid-al-shamsi/) was once the headquarters of the British delegate in the Gulf and later a hospital, and was restored between 1993 and 1995. It now hosts Sharjah Art Foundation exhibitions and the Sharjah Biennial. Bait Obaid Al Shamsi, built around 1845, is a two-level house with wind towers. Exhibitions change, so look at what is on before you go.

### The Museum of Islamic Civilization next door

On the waterfront at the northern edge of the district, the Sharjah Museums Authority says the [Museum of Islamic Civilization](https://www.sharjahmuseums.ae/en-us/museums/sharjah-museum-of-islamic-civilization) began life as Souq Al Majarrah, opened in 1987, before reopening as a museum in 2008. Its collection of more than five thousand pieces makes it the natural place to spend the hottest part of the day after a morning in the lanes.

For a fuller picture of the city's museums, read our [guide to Sharjah's museums](/stories/sharjah-museums-guide/).

## The other old souks

Sharjah's souk trail does not end at Al Arsah, and each souk has its own personality.

### Souq Al Shanasiyah

At the junction of Corniche Road and Al Hisn Street, [Souq Al Shanasiyah](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/shopping/souq-al-shanasiyah/) is built on the foundations of one of the oldest souks in the region. It has been renovated and leans towards regional designers: furniture and homewares, prayer beads, Arabic perfumes and oils, bespoke kandoras and handcrafted abayas. Visit Sharjah also mentions a speciality coffee house and a place serving Emirati food. It is a good place for a gift that does not feel mass-produced.

### Souq Saqr, the market by the sea

On the eastern side of the district, running parallel to the creek, [Souq Saqr](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/shopping/souq-saqr/) was once a centre of the gold trade, when dhows loaded with goods left the waterfront for Iran, Pakistan and India. It is also known as Souk Al Bahar. Today its air-conditioned lanes sell spices and herbs, henna, perfumes and oils, and traditional clothing, with tailors specialising in national and beaded dress.

### The Central Souk

A little outside the old quarter, the [Central Souk](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/shopping/central-souk-blue-souk/) is the one with the blue-tiled facade that appears on the five-dirham note. Visit Sharjah says it has more than 600 shops over two levels. The upper floor is best for carpets, kilims and handwoven textiles; the lagoon side for gold and jewellery.

### Browsing well

- Greet the shopkeeper before you start looking. It makes the rest of the conversation easier.
- Bargain politely and with a smile in the traditional souks. If the price does not work, thank them and move on.
- Ask before taking a photo of anyone. The UK Foreign Office says not to [photograph people without their permission](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security).
- For gold, carpets or anything costly, ask what it is made of and where it comes from, and keep the receipt.

Our guide to [Dubai's gold and spice souks](/stories/dubai-gold-and-spice-souks/) has more on bargaining etiquette if you are comparing the two cities.

## A half-day walking route

This loop keeps the walking short and the shade frequent. Start at Souq Al Arsah, then cross to the Heritage Museum and Bait Al Naboodah. Walk on to Majlis Al Midfa and Calligraphy Square, then head towards the Corniche to the fort and Souq Al Shanasiyah. If you still have energy, follow the waterfront east to Souq Saqr, or take a taxi to the Central Souk.

Allow more time than the distances suggest. The point here is to stop: in a courtyard, at a carved door, in a café with a small cup of coffee. Our [Sharjah day trip guide](/stories/sharjah-day-trip-from-dubai/) shows how to add a larger museum and Al Noor Mosque at sunset.

## Visiting respectfully

This is a living district with people at work and at prayer, and Sharjah is more conservative than Dubai.

- **Dress modestly.** The Foreign Office advises that women [cover shoulders and knees](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) in public areas. Loose, light layers are the comfortable way to do it. Our guide to [what to wear](/stories/what-to-wear-uae-etiquette/) has more.
- **Leave alcohol behind.** The same advice says [drinking or possessing alcohol is illegal in the Emirate of Sharjah](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security).
- **Mind Ramadan.** During the holy month, the Foreign Office says eating, drinking or smoking in public in the daytime [may cause offence](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), and opening hours change. Our [Ramadan guide](/stories/visiting-uae-during-ramadan/) explains what to expect.
- **Keep your voice down.** The lanes are narrow and sound carries into homes, offices and prayer rooms.

## Accessibility and families

The lanes are mostly flat but the surfaces vary, and the older houses have thresholds, courtyards and stairs to upper floors. The authority lists a lift and a wheelchair entrance on the [fort's page](https://www.sharjahmuseums.ae/en-us/museums/sharjah-fort-%28al-hisn%29), so it is the easiest of the heritage museums for wheelchair users and pushchairs. Elsewhere, ask at the door or check with your operator before you go.

Children usually enjoy the Heritage Museum's folk tales and puzzles, the fort's towers and old weapons, and the drop-in family workshops that the authority says run at Bait Al Naboodah. Short visits work better than long ones; mix a house, a souk and a juice stop.

## When to go

Mornings are best: cooler, quieter and with soft light on the coral walls. The covered souks are pleasant later in the day too, since they are air-conditioned. The Heart of Sharjah site lists the district as [open daily from 10am to 10pm](https://www.heartofsharjah.ae/), but individual museums keep their own hours, so check them separately.

If you can, time a visit for Sharjah Heritage Days. [Visit Sharjah](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/events/sharjah-heritage-days/) says the event is held in the heritage area each year, usually in March, with storytelling, talks, performances, Emirati food and displays on traditional clothing; its 2026 listing ran in February, so check dates for your year. Expect it to be busier, and livelier, than usual. More ideas are on our [Sharjah page](/destinations/sharjah/).

## Quick answers

**What is the Heart of Sharjah?**
It is Sharjah's restored historic quarter, with coral-stone houses, wind towers, souks, a fort and small museums. The UAE government portal lists it among the emirate's main historical areas.

**Which is the oldest souk in Sharjah?**
Visit Sharjah describes Souq Al Arsah as the oldest marketplace in the history of the UAE, once used by traders from Persia, India and the Bedouin tribes.

**Can I drink alcohol in the Heart of Sharjah?**
No. The UK Foreign Office says drinking or possessing alcohol is illegal anywhere in the Emirate of Sharjah.

**When is Sharjah Heritage Days?**
Visit Sharjah says it is usually held in March, with its main venue in the Heart of Sharjah, though dates vary by year.

**Is there a joint ticket for the house museums?**
Yes. The Sharjah Museums Authority sells a heritage joint ticket for five museums in the district.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Can you arrange a guided walk through the Heart of Sharjah that covers the fort, the house museums and the souks?
- Which of the house museums and art houses are open on our day, and is anything on at the Sharjah Art Foundation?
- What is the best time of day to visit the souks to see them working but not crowded?
- Can you suggest where to find good-quality crafts or perfume rather than mass-produced souvenirs?
- If we visit during Ramadan or Sharjah Heritage Days, how will our day change?
