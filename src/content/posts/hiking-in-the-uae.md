The path turns a corner and the coast drops away below you: a curve of bay, a scatter of boats, and the Gulf of Oman fading into haze. The only sound is your breathing and the scrape of boots on loose stone. It is hard to believe that a few hours earlier you were in a city of glass towers.

Hiking in the UAE surprises almost everyone. Beyond the dunes, the Hajar Mountains run down the east of the country, and they are laced with marked trails, old watchtowers and wadis that run with water after rain. The short version: hike between October and April, start at first light, pick a marked route in Ras Al Khaimah, Sharjah's east coast, Hatta or Al Ain, and treat heat and flash floods with real respect.

## Where the mountains are

The UAE government portal says most of the UAE's mountains belong to [the Hajar range](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/environment-and-energy/Nature-and-wildlife/topography-and-ecosystems), spread across Fujairah, Ras Al Khaimah and Sharjah as well as the Hatta area of Dubai. It notes that the valleys are usually dry, but flourish with brooks and pools in the cooler months when it rains.

<photo-set layout="inset-right" keys="hiking-in-the-uae-1" alts="A narrow path running beside a concrete water channel between farm trees in Hatta" caption="In Hatta, some walks follow the water channels that feed the mountain farms."></photo-set>

That gives you four main hiking regions, each with its own character: high and airy in Ras Al Khaimah, coastal and historic around Khorfakkan, green and watery in Hatta, and stark and open around Jebel Hafit near Al Ain. Fujairah adds a fifth, wilder option for experienced walkers with a guide.

The rock itself shapes the experience. Much of the Hajar is steep, broken and loose underfoot, with little soil and almost no shade on the higher slopes. Paths that look short on a map can be slow going, and descents are often harder on the legs than climbs. Plan your time on the basis of terrain, not distance.

## When to hike: the season is everything

Visit Ras Al Khaimah says [October to April is the best time to visit Jebel Jais](https://www.visitrasalkhaimah.com/jebel-jais/), and that the summit area is usually 10°C cooler than other parts of the emirate. That same window works for hiking across the country. Outside it, even short walks on exposed rock become hard work.

Within the season, timing still matters. Start early, aim to be off open slopes before the strongest midday sun, and allow far more water than you think you need. Our [month-by-month guide](/stories/best-time-to-visit-uae-month-by-month/) and our guide to [staying safe in the UAE heat](/stories/uae-heat-safety-guide/) go into more detail.

## The best trails, by region

### Ras Al Khaimah: Jebel Jais

The country's highest peak is the obvious draw. Visit Ras Al Khaimah says Jebel Jais rises to [1,934 metres above sea level](https://www.visitrasalkhaimah.com/jebel-jais/), and its hiking page describes [six marked trails with a cumulative distance of 16km](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/discover/activities/hiking/), spread through the upper and lower parts of the mountain and suited to walkers of all experience levels. It adds that climbing experience is not needed, but a head for heights, reasonable fitness and good shoes are.

The same page has one rule larger groups need to know: if your group is [more than ten people, you must inform the Ras Al Khaimah Tourism Development Department](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/discover/activities/hiking/) before your hike, with details of the route, participants and timings. Our [Jebel Jais guide](/stories/jebel-jais-guide/) covers the drive up.

### Sharjah: watchtowers, wadis and fossils

Sharjah has some of the best-documented short hikes in the country:

- **Al Rabi Trail, Khorfakkan.** Visit Sharjah describes a [4 to 5km round trip of 2 to 3 hours](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/hiking-trails/al-rabi-hiking-trail/) up to a watchtower built in 1915, on a well-marked path that suits beginners and children. It warns that the small car park fills quickly and that the trail is busy at weekends. See our [Khorfakkan guide](/stories/khorfakkan-guide/).
- **Wadi Shees Nature Trail.** A [1.8km loop of 30 to 40 minutes](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/hiking-trails/wadi-shees-nature-trail/) beside a traditional falaj water channel, palm groves and farms. There is some shade but no major rest stops.
- **Al Rafisah Trail.** Visit Sharjah calls it [a rewarding 3-hour round trip](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/hiking-trails/al-rafisah-hiking-trail/) on an easy to moderate, well-marked trail with rocky terrain and wide views.
- **Fossil Rock, Mleiha.** A [6km trail with one short, steep rock section](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/hiking-trails/mleiha-fossil-rock-hiking-trail/) that some will find hard. There is no shade, so Visit Sharjah advises starting early in the morning or late in the afternoon.

A few extra details help with planning. Visit Sharjah puts the Al Rabi Tower at [379m elevation](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/hiking-trails/al-rabi-hiking-trail/), with rest stops and historical signage along the way, and says extra parking lower down the hill adds a little to the walk. At Wadi Shees, it says there is the [sound and sight of running water in the old falaj](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/hiking-trails/wadi-shees-nature-trail/), and the path joins an old village and farms, so bring food as well as water. The Al Rafisah route runs to the [Al Suhab Rest Houses and back](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/hiking-trails/al-rafisah-hiking-trail/), and less keen walkers can enjoy the views on the first part and turn back. At Fossil Rock, Visit Sharjah says many of the fossils are [said to be over 80 million years old](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/hiking-trails/mleiha-fossil-rock-hiking-trail/), and pitches the trail at intermediate hikers.

Visit Sharjah also notes that hiking in the emirate is rarely dangerous, with information boards along most walks and help and phone signal usually close at hand. That makes Sharjah's trails a good first step before anything more remote.

### Hatta: Dubai's mountain corner

Dubai Municipality says the Hatta Mountain Reserve has [some of the best hiking routes in the UAE](https://www.dm.gov.ae/dubai-protected-areas/wildlife/), and Visit Dubai lists [hiking, kayaking, mountain biking and camping](https://www.visitdubai.com/en/beyond-dubai/nature-adventures-in-hatta) there. The landscape is greener than most, with dams, farms and wadis, and the reserve's flagship animal is the Arabian tahr. Our [Hatta guide](/stories/hatta-guide/) covers how to plan a day.

### Al Ain: Jebel Hafit

At the base of Abu Dhabi's highest mountain, Visit Abu Dhabi says Jebel Hafit Desert Park offers [hiking and mountain biking](https://www.visitabudhabi.ae/en/things-to-do/nature-and-wildlife/parks/jebel-hafit-desert-park) past ancient tombs, is open daily from 8am to 6pm, and allows accompanied dogs. The Environment Agency Abu Dhabi recommends [visiting during the day](https://www.ead.gov.ae/en/Experience-Green-Abu-Dhabi/Places-To-Go/Jabal-Hafit-National--Park) to take in the rocky terrain.

Visit Abu Dhabi says the park stretches along nine kilometres at the base of the mountain, that the area was recognised by UNESCO in 2011 as part of the Al Ain World Heritage Site, and that camping is allowed with your own equipment. The walks here are as much about history as views: the beehive shaped tombs on the lower slopes are around 5,000 years old, and some have been restored to show how they were built.

### Fujairah: wadis and wild climbs

Fujairah's tourism authority describes [Wadi Ham as the largest and longest wadi in the UAE](https://www.fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-NaturalSites.html), dry for most of the year but transformed into a rushing stream after rain. It also warns that [climbs in the Hajar Mountains can be treacherous](https://www.fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-SportsAndCruises.html) and that climbers should be well prepared. Off the marked trails, this is guide territory.

## How a hiking day usually unfolds

On a typical winter hike, the day starts in the dark. You drive to the trailhead so that you are walking at first light, while the rock is still cool and the low sun throws the ridges into relief. The first hour is the most comfortable, so use it for the climb. By mid morning the heat is building on exposed slopes, and a sensible plan has you at your high point, resting in whatever shade you can find, before you start down.

Most marked routes in the UAE are out and back or short loops, so you can turn round at any point. Agree a turnaround time before you set off and stick to it, whoever feels strong. Many walkers finish before lunch and then fold the hike into a wider day: a heritage village in Hatta, the old town and beach in Khorfakkan, or the oasis in Al Ain.

## What to wear and carry

Beyond the essentials in the checklist below, a few items earn their place on almost every UAE hike:

- **A wide brimmed hat and sunglasses.** Reflected light off pale rock is intense.
- **Long, loose, breathable clothing.** It protects from sun and scrapes better than shorts, and is more respectful when trails pass farms and villages.
- **Electrolytes as well as water.** You lose salt quickly in dry heat.
- **A head torch.** Useful for pre dawn starts and in case a walk runs late.
- **A charged phone with offline maps.** Signal can drop in deep wadis.

## Navigation and trail markings

The marked trails on Jebel Jais and in Sharjah are generally easy to follow, with painted waymarks, signposts or information boards at key points. Off those routes, paths fade quickly into goat tracks and dry stream beds that all look alike, and it is easy to drift into the wrong side valley. Download the route before you leave, check your position regularly rather than only when you feel lost, and note landmarks on the way out so the return looks familiar. If the path disappears and you are unsure, retrace your steps to the last marker instead of pushing on across steep ground.

## Trail etiquette

Many trails pass through places where people live and farm. Keep to the path through plantations, do not pick fruit, and never wash, swim or step in a falaj channel, which carries water for crops and homes. Dress modestly when walking near villages, keep noise down, and ask before photographing anyone. Leave gates as you find them, carry out every scrap of rubbish, and do not build cairns or move rocks on marked routes, since they can confuse other walkers.

## Hiking with children

Children can enjoy UAE hiking if you pick the right trail and the right time. Short, well marked routes with a clear goal, such as a watchtower, a pool or a fossil hunt, work best. Visit Sharjah describes the Al Rabi Trail as good for people with little experience and children, and the Wadi Shees loop as suitable for all ages. Start early, plan frequent shade breaks, and turn back while everyone is still enjoying it. Avoid steep scrambles and any route with drops unless your children are experienced and closely supervised.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting late.** A trail that is pleasant at seven in the morning can be punishing by eleven.
- **Underestimating descents.** Loose scree and steep rock make the way down slow.
- **Relying on one water bottle.** Carry more than you expect to need, plus a reserve in the car.
- **Ignoring distant rain.** A storm in the hills can send water down a wadi under clear skies.
- **Wearing trainers with worn soles.** Grip matters on smooth limestone and loose gravel.

## Staying safe hiking in the UAE

### Before you set off

Visit Ras Al Khaimah publishes a [sensible pre-hike checklist](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/discover/activities/hiking/) that applies anywhere in the country:

- Choose a route that suits the least experienced person in your group.
- Check the weather forecast before you go.
- Never hike alone, and tell someone your route and expected return time.
- Carry a map, plenty of water and snacks.
- Wear proper hiking boots with good grip, never sandals or flip-flops, and layer your clothes.

Visit Sharjah adds that a [small medical kit with plasters and insect bite ointment](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/hiking-trails/al-rabi-hiking-trail/) is useful, and asks hikers to carry out all their rubbish.

### Rain and flash floods

Rain is rare, but it changes everything in the mountains. The UK Foreign Office warns that [heavy rain in the UAE can cause flooding](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), and advises checking forecasts from the National Centre of Meteorology and following local authorities. In practice: do not enter a wadi if rain is forecast, even far away in the hills, and never try to cross running water.

### Heat and emergencies

Heat is the commonest problem, even on cooler days. Stop, find shade and drink if anyone feels dizzy, sick or confused, and turn back early rather than late. The Foreign Office says to [dial 998 for an ambulance](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/health). Check, too, that your travel insurance covers hiking. ## Quick answers

**When is the best time to hike in the UAE?** October to April, which Visit Ras Al Khaimah names as the best time for Jebel Jais. Start early in the day.

**What is the easiest hike in the UAE for beginners?** Sharjah's Al Rabi Trail in Khorfakkan is described as ideal for beginners, and the Wadi Shees loop takes 30 to 40 minutes.

**How many trails are there on Jebel Jais?** Visit Ras Al Khaimah lists six marked trails with a cumulative distance of 16km.

**Is hiking in Sharjah safe for beginners?** Visit Sharjah says it is rarely dangerous, with information boards on most walks, but you still need water, proper shoes and an early start.

**Do groups need to register a hike in Ras Al Khaimah?** Visit Ras Al Khaimah says groups of more than ten must inform the Ras Al Khaimah Tourism Development Department in advance.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Which trail suits our group's fitness and experience, and how long will it really take us?
- What time do we start, and how do you decide to turn back in heat?
- How do you check for rain and flash flood risk on the day, and what is the plan if it changes?
- Is the guide trained in first aid, and what do they carry for emergencies?
- Do you handle any group notifications the trail requires, such as in Ras Al Khaimah?
- Is transport to and from the trailhead included, and where do we park if the car park is full?
