The room is full, the food is out, and nobody is eating. Glasses of water stand untouched beside small plates of dates. Conversation is quiet. Then, somewhere outside, the call to prayer begins, and the whole table moves at once.

That moment, when a day of fasting ends at sunset, is iftar. During Ramadan it happens every evening across the UAE, in homes, mosques, tents and restaurants, and it is one of the warmest experiences open to visitors. You do not need to be Muslim, and you do not need to be fasting. You need to know where to go, what to wear and how the meal unfolds.

The quick version: the easiest ways to join an iftar as a visitor are a hosted cultural iftar, such as the programme run in Dubai's Al Fahidi neighbourhood, a home hospitality experience, or an iftar at a restaurant or hotel. Below we explain each, plus the etiquette that makes you a welcome guest.

## What iftar is, and why it matters

Ramadan is the ninth month of the Islamic calendar. The UAE government portal explains that during it, [capable Muslims abstain from eating and drinking from dawn to dusk](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/public-holidays-and-religious-affairs/ramadan). There are two main meals: suhoor, eaten before dawn, and iftar, which breaks the fast. The same page says that, following the example of the Prophet Muhammad, the fast is broken with dates and laban (buttermilk).

It is also a social occasion. The portal describes how, on the first evening of Ramadan, families gather at the home of the head of the family for their first iftar, and how providing free iftar meals is a common practice across the country, at mosques, malls and other public places.

Non-Muslims are [not required to fast](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/public-holidays-and-religious-affairs/ramadan), but the government notes that visitors can enjoy the spiritual and social atmosphere, as cities become especially lively after sunset.

## Ways visitors can join an iftar

### A hosted cultural iftar

For a first iftar, a hosted programme is the easiest way in, because explaining things is the point. In Dubai, the Sheikh Mohammed bin Rashid Al Maktoum Centre for Cultural Understanding, in the Al Fahidi Historical Neighbourhood, runs a [Ramadan iftar programme](https://cultures.ae/program/ramadan-iftar-program/) described as an evening in a heritage setting, with a guided mosque visit, cultural conversation and interactive activities about Ramadan traditions. The centre also offers private group iftars.

Practical points from its page: same-day bookings are not available online, and the start time shifts slightly through the month as the time of Maghrib prayer changes. Dates for each Ramadan are set by the centre, so check the page nearer the time. Outside Ramadan, the same centre runs [cultural meals](https://cultures.ae/program/cultural-meals) where, during Ramadan, dinner is replaced by iftar.

If you want to combine it with a wander, our [Al Fahidi Historical Neighbourhood guide](/stories/al-fahidi-historical-neighbourhood/) covers the lanes around it.

### An iftar in someone's home

In Abu Dhabi, the tourism board lists experiences where you can be [a guest in a local home](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/article-hub/traditional-emirati-experiences), with Arabic coffee and dates, a meal personalised for your group, and an introduction to cultural rituals, including Ramadan celebrations. Ask your operator whether an iftar version is available during your dates.

### Restaurants and hotels

This is the option most visitors end up choosing, often more than once. The UAE government portal notes that visitors can enjoy [a wide range of iftar and suhoor experiences at many restaurants](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/public-holidays-and-religious-affairs/ramadan), and that some restaurants that adjust their hours during the day open fully at iftar. Many hotels set up dedicated Ramadan dining. Book ahead for popular places, as tables fill quickly at sunset.

### Ramadan tents and free iftars

You will see large tents near mosques and in open spaces. The government portal explains that charitable organisations [set up tents to offer free iftar meals](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/public-holidays-and-religious-affairs/ramadan), reflecting a spirit of giving and sharing among people of different nationalities and backgrounds. These exist for people who are fasting and people in need. If you are curious, be respectful, do not treat them as an attraction, and do not take a place or a meal intended for someone else. A donation through a registered charity is a more fitting way to take part.

### A mosque at iftar time

In Abu Dhabi, the Sheikh Zayed Grand Mosque publishes Ramadan arrangements. Its page says worshippers may enter directly, while [visitors should pre-book and obtain an online access pass](https://szgmc.gov.ae/en/worshippers/ramadan-kareem). Check the current arrangements on the official site before you go. Our [Sheikh Zayed Grand Mosque visitor guide](/stories/sheikh-zayed-grand-mosque-visitor-guide/) covers the visit in more detail.

## What happens at an iftar, step by step

1. **Arrive before sunset.** Aim to be seated in good time. Roads are busy in the run-up, and the government portal warns that [taxis can be hard to find in the evenings](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/public-holidays-and-religious-affairs/ramadan), so book in advance.
2. **Wait for the signal.** The meal begins with the call to prayer for Maghrib. In many places, the portal says, an [iftar cannon is fired](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/public-holidays-and-religious-affairs/ramadan) to mark the moment.
3. **Dates and a drink first.** Expect dates with water or laban to open the meal.
4. **A pause for prayer.** Many guests step away to pray before the main meal. Wait patiently; this is normal.
5. **Then the feast.** Dishes vary, but Visit Ras Al Khaimah notes that thareed is [a staple during Ramadan](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/blog/emirati-flavors-traditional-food-uae/) and that madrooba is frequently served for iftar. The government portal also mentions harees and alqurs, a sweet crumble of bread with dates and cardamom.

Our guide to [Emirati food: what to eat and where](/stories/emirati-food-what-to-eat-where/) explains these dishes.

## Etiquette for guests

### Before iftar

The day matters as much as the meal. The FCDO advises that during Ramadan it may cause offence to [eat, drink, smoke or chew gum in public in the daytime](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), including in your car. Do not snack on the way to iftar, even if you are hungry.

### What to wear

The FCDO notes that [clothing that does not meet local dress codes may cause more offence during Ramadan](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security). For an iftar, choose loose clothes that cover shoulders and knees, and bring a scarf if a mosque visit is included. Our guide to [what to wear in the UAE](/stories/what-to-wear-uae-etiquette/) has more detail.

### At the table

- **Use your right hand.** Visit Ras Al Khaimah advises you to [take plates, cups and food with your right hand](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/blog/all-social-customs-and-etiquette-every-female-traveler-should-know/).
- **Accept what is offered,** and explain any medical or dietary reason kindly.
- **Greetings.** The same guide notes that a man who is fasting may not be able to shake a woman's hand. Follow your host's lead.
- **Say the greeting.** People wish each other ["Ramadan Kareem" or "Ramadan Mubarak"](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/public-holidays-and-religious-affairs/ramadan), and visitors are welcome to do the same.
- **Keep the volume down.** The FCDO lists [playing loud music or dancing](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) among things that may cause offence during the month.

## Planning your evening

Iftar times move earlier or later through the month with sunset, and Ramadan itself [arrives about 11 days earlier](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/public-holidays-and-religious-affairs/ramadan) each year in the Gregorian calendar. ## Quick answers

**Can non-Muslims join an iftar in the UAE?** Yes. The UAE government says non-Muslims are not required to fast but can enjoy the atmosphere and iftar experiences at many restaurants, and hosted cultural iftars welcome visitors.

**How is the fast broken?** With dates and laban (buttermilk), following the example of the Prophet Muhammad, according to the UAE government portal.

**Where can a visitor join a cultural iftar in Dubai?** The Sheikh Mohammed bin Rashid Al Maktoum Centre for Cultural Understanding in Al Fahidi runs a Ramadan iftar programme with a guided mosque visit. Same-day bookings are not available online.

**What should I wear to an iftar?** Modest, loose clothing covering shoulders and knees. The FCDO notes that clothing that does not meet local dress codes may cause more offence during Ramadan.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Can you book a hosted cultural iftar or a private group iftar for us, and when does booking open?
- Is a home hospitality iftar available during our dates?
- How will you handle transfers around sunset, when roads are busy and taxis are scarce?
- Is a mosque visit included, and what should each of us wear?
- Can you cater for allergies or dietary needs at the iftar?
- If our trip overlaps with the start or end of Ramadan, how will you confirm timings once the dates are announced?
