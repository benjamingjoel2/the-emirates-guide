The driver pulls over on a sandy verge, steps out and lets the air hiss from each tyre in turn. Inside the car nobody says much, because ahead of you the first dune is rising like a wave about to break, and everyone has just realised the vehicle is going over it.

That pause, half nerves and half anticipation, is the evening desert safari in a nutshell. It is one of the best-known excursions in the UAE and an easy way for a first-time visitor to get out of the city and see the desert in its best light. It is also the trip that divides people most. Some come back talking about the silence and the colour of the sand at sunset. Others remember a lurching ride, a crowded camp and a buffet queue.

Both can be true on the same evening. Whether it is worth it depends on which kind you book and how you feel about being thrown about in a 4x4. Here is what happens, and how to choose.

## An evening on the sand, hour by hour

Most evening trips follow the same shape. Timings shift through the year because the evening is built around sunset, so check yours when you book.

### The pickup and the tyre stop

A 4x4 collects you in the afternoon, usually shared with a few other guests unless you book a private vehicle. The towers thin out faster than you expect, the sand on the verge turns from beige to warm orange, and the highway gives way to a sand track.

Here the driver usually stops to lower the tyre pressure for better grip on soft sand. This is your moment: if anyone gets motion sick or has a bad back, say so now, not halfway up a dune.

### The first minutes on the dunes

Then comes the part often sold as "dune bashing". The driver takes the car up, along and down the faces of the dunes, sometimes sideways across a slope, sometimes over a crest with a drop that feels far steeper than it is. The engine roars, the car tilts, your stomach lifts and sand hisses against the windows.

Thrilling for some, too much for others. A few habits help:

- Keep your seatbelt on the whole time.
- Sit back in your seat rather than leaning forward to look.
- Fix your eyes on the horizon if you start to feel queasy.
- Ask the driver to go gentler if you need to. A good driver will adjust without fuss.

On trips run inside the Dubai Desert Conservation Reserve, dune driving follows [predefined routes](https://www.ddcr.org/what-to-do/) rather than free-roaming across the sand.

### Sunset from the top of a dune

Most trips pause on a high dune before the sun goes down, and for many people this is the best part of the evening. The engine cuts out and the quiet arrives all at once. The light turns the ridges gold, then rust, shadows pour into the hollows, and the warmth starts to leave the sand under your feet.

Walk a little way from the cars if you can; the wind ripples and the sheer emptiness are what stay with you. Many trips offer short camel rides or sandboarding around here or at the camp.

### Lanterns, grills and the camp

After dark you arrive at a desert camp: low seating, carpets, lanterns, the smell of charcoal from the grill and usually some form of evening entertainment. The DDCR's own description of a [dune dinner](https://www.ddcr.org/what-to-do/) gives a fair picture of the format in the reserve: Bedu-style tents, grilled meats, salads, Arabic sweets and drinks, and a belly dance performance under the stars.

Outside protected areas, camps range from small and calm to large and busy, with many groups sharing the same space.

## Why two safaris can feel like different trips

The gap between a good safari and a disappointing one usually comes down to three things: how many people share the camp, how the driving is done, and where in the desert you go.

The Dubai Desert Conservation Reserve shows what the protected end of the scale looks like. It describes itself as [the UAE's first national park](https://www.ddcr.org/), set up to protect Dubai's inland desert and its native plants and animals. According to the reserve, it is divided into [four zones](https://www.ddcr.org/about/), from a wilderness area open only to researchers on foot to a zone where approved safari operators with qualified, registered guides can run four-wheel excursions and small dune dinner camps on pre-planned routes. The reserve says it limits access to selected operators, trains guides in wildlife and conservation, recycles waste at the camps and restricts activities with an environmental impact.

It also runs an [approved tours scheme](https://www.ddcr.org/what-to-do/) with a seal you can look out for: approved tours focus on nature or culture, guides are certified by the reserve, and the tour is expected to consider waste and environmental impact.

That does not make every trip outside a reserve bad, but do ask where you are going and how many people will be at the camp.

## Other ways into the desert

Not keen on the classic format? The desert has quieter doors.

### Wildlife drives in the reserve

Trips in protected areas tend to be calmer. The DDCR lists [wildlife drives](https://www.ddcr.org/what-to-do/) where the guide explains the desert ecology and looks out for free-roaming herds, as well as nature walks, horse riding, falconry and hot air balloon flights. The reserve is home to [Arabian oryx and gazelles](https://www.ddcr.org/), and spotting them in the early light is a world away from a high-speed dune session.

### Morning trips

Morning safaris usually swap the camp dinner for an earlier, shorter outing with dune driving, sandboarding or camel riding. They suit families with young children and anyone who would rather be back for lunch. Check whether the trip includes any shade or breakfast.

### A night under the stars

An overnight stay shows you what most visitors miss: the desert after the day-trippers leave, a sky thick with stars, and sunrise creeping over the dunes. The DDCR lists [camping](https://www.ddcr.org/what-to-do/) with professional safari guides among its activities. Ask about bedding, toilets and how cold it gets at night in the month you are travelling.

### Liwa, for sheer scale

For the full scale of the sand sea, head to Liwa in Abu Dhabi's western Al Dhafra region. Visit Abu Dhabi describes Al Dhafra as stretching from the Liwa Desert to the west coast, with Tel Moreeb and the Liwa dunes among its main draws, and says the drive from Abu Dhabi city typically takes [around two to three hours](https://www.visitabudhabi.ae/en/where-to-go/regions/al-dhafra). It suggests a car is the most convenient way to get around and the cooler months are best for desert experiences. The same page notes the [Liwa International Festival](https://www.visitabudhabi.ae/en/where-to-go/regions/al-dhafra) each December, with desert motorsports and cultural events, so expect it to be busier then.

### Hatta, for rock instead of sand

If you want the outdoors without dune driving, Hatta, in the mountains of Dubai emirate, trades sand for rock. Visit Dubai lists [hiking trails, kayaking, mountain biking and camping](https://www.visitdubai.com/en/beyond-dubai/nature-adventures-in-hatta) there. Our guide to [the quieter emirates](/stories/beyond-dubai-quieter-emirates/) has more ideas outside the big cities.

## Before you book: the safety checks that matter

The desert is beautiful, and it does not forgive poor preparation. The UK Foreign Office warns that [desert excursions can be dangerous](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) unless you are in a properly equipped 4-wheel-drive vehicle, and advises that if you join a tour for desert driving or quad biking you go with a reputable company with adequate health and safety procedures. It also says to check that these activities are covered by your insurance. Some policies exclude or limit "adventure" activities, so read yours.

### Seatbelts and where you sit

Wear a seatbelt for the whole off-road section, including in the back. If you get motion sick, ask for a front seat and a gentler drive, and keep lunch light on the day.

### Backs, necks and other conditions

Dune driving means sudden jolts and steep angles. If you have back or neck problems, a heart condition, or any recent surgery, speak to your doctor before you go and tell the operator when you book. Ask whether you can skip the dune-driving section or travel in a vehicle that takes a gentler route to camp.

### Pregnancy

The same applies if you are pregnant: get medical advice first and tell the operator. The Foreign Office stresses that having [appropriate travel insurance](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/health) for local treatment is particularly important if you are pregnant or have a health condition. A camel ride, a wildlife drive or an evening at camp without the dune section may be a better fit.

### Heat

Even in the cooler months, the sun on open sand is strong. Outside them, it can be dangerous. The NHS advises [drinking more cold fluids, wearing light, loose clothing and avoiding the sun between 11am and 3pm](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/heat-exhaustion-heatstroke/), and lists signs of heat exhaustion including tiredness, dizziness, headache, nausea, heavy sweating and strong thirst. It describes heatstroke as a medical emergency. In the UAE, the Foreign Office says to [dial 998 for an ambulance](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/health).

Small things help: keep the middle of the day slow before an afternoon pickup, carry your own water, and bring sunglasses and a hat. Our [month-by-month guide](/stories/best-time-to-visit-uae-month-by-month/) will help you pick a comfortable time to go.

## What to bring, and what to leave at the hotel

A desert evening needs less kit than people expect, but the right few things make a real difference.

- **A warm layer.** In the cooler months the sand loses its heat quickly after sunset, and a camp that felt warm at dusk can feel chilly by dinner.
- **Closed shoes or sandals you can empty.** Sand gets everywhere. Many people end up barefoot on the dunes, so carry your shoes rather than wearing socks.
- **Water, a hat and sunglasses,** even on an evening trip, for the hour before sunset.
- **A small bag for your phone and camera.** Fine sand is hard on lenses and charging ports, so keep them zipped away during the driving and at camp when the wind picks up.
- **Cash or a card for extras,** if you think you may want photos, henna or a second camel ride at camp. Ask in advance what is included.

Leave valuables and anything bulky at the hotel. You will be climbing in and out of a 4x4 and walking on soft sand in the dark.

## Taking photos, and taking care

The desert at sunset is one of the most photographed scenes in the country, and it is easy to come home with good pictures. Shoot the ridges in side light, keep people small in the frame against the dunes, and turn around: the colour behind you is often better than the sunset itself.

Be careful with people, though. The UK Foreign Office advises [not to photograph people without their permission](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), and that applies at camp as much as in the city. Ask before photographing staff, performers or other guests. The same advice notes that you may need a licence for some technical equipment, including powerful cameras or binoculars, so check before bringing specialist kit. Drones are best left at home unless your operator confirms they are allowed.

## Thinking of driving into the desert yourself?

We would not recommend it on a first visit. The Foreign Office advises that if you do go into the desert independently you should always travel with other cars, take a supply of water and a mobile phone, and leave a copy of your travel plans with someone where you are staying. Soft sand, sudden slopes and the speed at which daylight goes are all easy to underestimate. An experienced guide removes most of that risk and knows where the best viewpoints are.

## Leaving the dunes as you found them

Off-road driving compacts sand, crushes plants and disturbs wildlife, and large camps produce a lot of waste. The DDCR's approach, with [zoning, fixed routes and waste recycling at camps](https://www.ddcr.org/about/), shows what lower-impact desert tourism looks like in practice.

Help by choosing an operator who can explain where they drive and what happens to the rubbish, leaving nothing behind, and keeping your distance from wildlife.

## So, is it worth it? Who each style suits

For most first-time visitors, yes, as long as you book the right kind.

- **The classic evening safari suits** groups of friends, older children and teenagers, and anyone who wants a lively, sociable evening with a bit of adrenaline and a sunset photo.
- **A conservation-reserve or wildlife trip suits** couples, photographers, nature lovers and anyone who wants the desert to feel like the desert rather than a show.
- **An overnight camp suits** people who want silence, stars and sunrise, and who do not mind simple facilities.
- **Liwa suits** travellers with a car and a couple of days, who want the biggest dunes and a sense of real distance.
- **Hatta suits** active travellers who would rather walk, paddle or cycle than ride in a 4x4.
- **Skip the dune-driving section if** you are pregnant, have back or neck problems, or simply hate fairground rides. You can still enjoy the sunset, the camels and the camp.

## Quick answers

**Is a desert safari worth it?** For most first-time visitors, yes, as long as you choose the style that suits your group.

**Is dune driving safe?** Go with a reputable company with proper safety procedures, wear a seatbelt throughout and check your insurance covers it.

**Are there calmer options?** Yes. Wildlife drives, morning trips, overnight camps, Liwa and Hatta all offer the desert or mountains without a long dune-driving session.

**What should I wear?** Light, loose clothing, a hat for the sun and a warm layer for the evening.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Where exactly do you drive and camp, and is it inside a conservation reserve or another managed area?
- How long is the dune-driving section, and can it be made gentler or skipped for anyone with back, neck or pregnancy concerns?
- How many guests will be at the camp on our evening, and how many people share each vehicle?
- Are your drivers trained and licensed for desert driving, and is there a first aid kit and a plan for medical emergencies on board?
- What do you do with waste from the camp, and how do you keep vehicles away from wildlife and plants?
- What time is pickup and return in the month we are travelling, and what happens if the weather turns?
