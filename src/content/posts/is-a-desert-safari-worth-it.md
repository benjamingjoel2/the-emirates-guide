The city falls away faster than you expect. One moment the car is sliding past glass towers and roundabouts, the next the road is lined with low scrub and the sand on the verge has turned from beige to a warm orange. Somewhere along the highway the driver pulls over, lets air out of the tyres, and the whole group goes quiet as the first dune rises ahead like a wave.

The evening desert safari is one of the best-known excursions in the UAE, and for good reason: it is an easy way for a first-time visitor to get out of the city and see the desert at its best light. It is also the trip that divides people most. Some come back talking about the silence and the colour of the sand at sunset. Others remember a lurching ride, a crowded camp and a buffet queue.

Both can be true on the same evening. Whether a safari is worth it for you depends on which kind you book, what you want from the desert, and a few honest questions about your health and your tolerance for being thrown about in a 4x4. Here is what actually happens, and how to choose.

## What happens on a typical evening safari

Most evening trips follow the same shape, whichever emirate you start from. Timings vary through the year because they are built around sunset, so check yours when you book.

### The pickup

A 4x4 collects you from your hotel in the afternoon, usually shared with a few other guests unless you book a private vehicle. The drive out to the desert takes you along a highway and then onto a sand track, where the driver often stops to lower the tyre pressure for better grip on soft sand. This is the moment to tell the driver about motion sickness or back trouble.

### Dune driving

Then comes the part that is often sold as "dune bashing": the driver takes the car up, along and down the faces of the dunes, sometimes sideways along a slope, sometimes over a crest with a drop that feels steeper than it is. It can be thrilling. It can also be a lot: the car tilts, your stomach lifts and sand sprays past the windows.

Wear your seatbelt the whole time, sit back in your seat rather than leaning forward, and do not be shy about asking the driver to go gentler. A good driver will adjust. On trips run inside the Dubai Desert Conservation Reserve, dune driving follows [predefined routes](https://www.ddcr.org/what-to-do/) rather than free-roaming across the sand.

### The stop for sunset

Most trips pause on a high dune before the sun goes down. This is often the best part of the evening. The light turns the ridges gold and then rust, shadows stretch into the hollows, and the temperature starts to drop. Many trips offer short camel rides or sandboarding around here or at the camp.

### The camp

After dark you arrive at a desert camp: low seating, carpets, lanterns, a grill and usually some form of evening entertainment. The DDCR's own description of a [dune dinner](https://www.ddcr.org/what-to-do/) gives a fair picture of the format in the reserve: Bedu-style tents, grilled meats, salads, Arabic sweets and drinks, and a belly dance performance under the stars. Outside protected areas, camps range from small and calm to large and busy, with many groups sharing the same space.

## Why the experience varies so much

The difference between a good safari and a disappointing one usually comes down to three things: how many people share the camp, how the driving is done, and where in the desert you go.

The Dubai Desert Conservation Reserve is a useful example of the protected end of the scale. It describes itself as [the UAE's first national park](https://www.ddcr.org/), set up to protect Dubai's inland desert and its native plants and animals. According to the reserve, it is divided into [four zones](https://www.ddcr.org/about/), from a wilderness area open only to researchers on foot to a zone where approved safari operators with qualified, registered guides can run four-wheel excursions and small dune dinner camps on pre-planned routes. The reserve says it limits access to selected operators, trains guides in wildlife and conservation, recycles waste at the camps and restricts activities with an environmental impact.

The reserve also runs an [approved tours scheme](https://www.ddcr.org/what-to-do/) with a seal you can look out for: approved tours focus on nature or culture, guides are certified by the reserve, and the tour is expected to consider waste and environmental impact.

That does not make every trip outside a reserve bad, but do ask where you are going and how many people will be at the camp.

## The alternatives

If the classic evening format does not appeal, there are other ways to see the desert.

### Conservation-reserve and wildlife trips

Trips in protected areas tend to be calmer. The DDCR lists [wildlife drives](https://www.ddcr.org/what-to-do/) where the guide explains the desert ecology and looks out for free-roaming herds, as well as nature walks, horse riding, falconry and hot air balloon flights. The reserve is home to [Arabian oryx and gazelles](https://www.ddcr.org/), and spotting them in the early light is a very different experience from a high-speed dune session.

### Morning trips

Morning safaris usually swap the camp dinner for an earlier, shorter outing with dune driving, sandboarding or camel riding. They suit families with young children and anyone who would rather be back for lunch. Check whether the trip includes any shade or breakfast.

### Overnight camping

An overnight stay lets you see what most visitors miss: the desert after the day-trippers leave, the stars, and sunrise over the dunes. The DDCR lists [camping](https://www.ddcr.org/what-to-do/) with professional safari guides among its activities. Ask about bedding, toilets and how cold it gets at night in the month you are travelling.

### Liwa

For the full scale of the sand sea, head to Liwa in Abu Dhabi's western Al Dhafra region. Visit Abu Dhabi describes Al Dhafra as stretching from the Liwa Desert to the west coast, with Tel Moreeb and the Liwa dunes among its main draws, and says the drive from Abu Dhabi city typically takes [around two to three hours](https://www.visitabudhabi.ae/en/where-to-go/regions/al-dhafra). It suggests a car is the most convenient way to get around and the cooler months are best for desert experiences. The same page notes the [Liwa International Festival](https://www.visitabudhabi.ae/en/where-to-go/regions/al-dhafra) each December, with desert motorsports and cultural events, so expect it to be busier then.

### Hatta

If you want the outdoors without dune driving, Hatta, in the mountains of Dubai emirate, swaps sand for rock. Visit Dubai lists [hiking trails, kayaking, mountain biking and camping](https://www.visitdubai.com/en/beyond-dubai/nature-adventures-in-hatta) there. ## Safety: what to think about before you book

The desert is beautiful, and it does not forgive poor preparation. The UK Foreign Office warns that [desert excursions can be dangerous](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) unless you are in a properly equipped 4-wheel-drive vehicle, and advises that if you join a tour for desert driving or quad biking you go with a reputable company with adequate health and safety procedures. It also says to check that these activities are covered by your insurance. Some policies exclude or limit "adventure" activities, so read yours.

### Seatbelts and seating

Wear a seatbelt for the whole off-road section, including in the back. If you get motion sick, ask for a front seat and a gentler drive, and avoid a heavy meal just before.

### Back, neck and other conditions

Dune driving involves sudden jolts and steep angles. If you have back or neck problems, a heart condition, or any recent surgery, speak to your doctor before you go and tell the operator when you book. Ask whether you can skip the dune-driving section or travel in a vehicle that takes a gentler route to camp.

### Pregnancy

The same applies if you are pregnant: get medical advice first and tell the operator. The Foreign Office stresses that having [appropriate travel insurance](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/health) for local treatment is particularly important if you are pregnant or have a health condition. A camel ride, a wildlife drive or an evening at camp without the dune section may be a better choice.

### Heat

Even in the cooler months, the sun on open sand is strong. Outside them, it can be dangerous. The NHS advises [drinking more cold fluids, wearing light, loose clothing and avoiding the sun between 11am and 3pm](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/heat-exhaustion-heatstroke/), and lists signs of heat exhaustion including tiredness, dizziness, headache, nausea, heavy sweating and strong thirst. It describes heatstroke as a medical emergency. In the UAE, the Foreign Office says to [dial 998 for an ambulance](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/health). ## The environmental side

Off-road driving compacts sand, crushes plants and disturbs wildlife, and large camps produce a lot of waste. The DDCR's approach, with [zoning, fixed routes and waste recycling at camps](https://www.ddcr.org/about/), shows what lower-impact desert tourism looks like in practice.

You can help by choosing an operator who can explain where they drive and what happens to the camp's rubbish, leaving nothing behind and keeping a respectful distance from wildlife.

## The verdict: who it suits

A desert safari is worth it for most first-time visitors, as long as you book the right kind.

- **The classic evening safari suits** groups of friends, older children and teenagers, and anyone who wants a lively, sociable evening with a bit of adrenaline and a sunset photo.
- **A conservation-reserve or wildlife trip suits** couples, photographers, nature lovers and anyone who wants the desert to feel like the desert rather than a show.
- **An overnight camp suits** people who want silence, stars and sunrise, and who do not mind simple facilities.
- **Liwa suits** travellers with a car and a couple of days, who want the biggest dunes and a sense of real distance.
- **Hatta suits** active travellers who would rather walk, paddle or cycle than ride in a 4x4.
- **Skip the dune-driving section if** you are pregnant, have back or neck problems, or simply hate fairground rides. You can still enjoy the sunset, the camels and the camp.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Where exactly do you drive and camp, and is it inside a conservation reserve or another managed area?
- How long is the dune-driving section, and can it be made gentler or skipped for anyone with back, neck or pregnancy concerns?
- How many guests will be at the camp on our evening, and how many people share each vehicle?
- Are your drivers trained and licensed for desert driving, and is there a first aid kit and a plan for medical emergencies?
- What do you do with waste from the camp, and how do you keep vehicles away from wildlife and plants?
- What time is pickup and return in the month we are travelling, and what happens if the weather turns?
