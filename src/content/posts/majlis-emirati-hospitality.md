You slip off your shoes at the door. Inside, the room is long and quiet, lined with cushions along the walls, and before you have properly sat down someone is already pouring a thin stream of pale gold coffee into a tiny cup.

That is the majlis, and it is the clearest window a visitor can get into Emirati hospitality. It is a room, a gathering and a set of manners all at once. You may meet one in a heritage house, at a hosted cultural meal, or, if you are lucky, as a guest in someone's home. Knowing a little about how it works turns a slightly nervous moment into a warm one.

In short: a majlis is a sitting place where guests are welcomed with coffee and conversation. Greet warmly, follow your host's lead, take your coffee with your right hand, and give a small shake of the cup when you have had enough. The rest of this guide explains why.

## What a majlis actually is

The word itself tells you a lot. Visit Abu Dhabi explains that in Arabic, majlis means ["sitting place" or "council"](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/majlis). Traditionally it was a tented meeting spot where guests were welcomed with coffee and conversation before important discussions, and today many homes still have a majlis to host visitors.

UNESCO, which lists the majlis as intangible cultural heritage, describes these [sitting places](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/majlis-a-cultural-and-social-space-01076) as spaces where community members gather to discuss local events, exchange news, receive guests, socialise and be entertained. It is also where communities resolve problems, pay condolences and hold wedding receptions. The typical setting will feel familiar once you have seen one: a large space with carpets on the floor, cushions against the wall, and usually a stove or fire to prepare coffee and other hot drinks.

Abu Dhabi Culture notes that the UAE, Oman, Qatar and Saudi Arabia applied together to inscribe the majlis on the UNESCO list, and that [it was listed in 2015](https://abudhabiculture.ae/en/Cultural-Heritage/InTangible/Heritage-Register/Social-Practices/Majlis).

## A room with many jobs

Hearing that a majlis is "a living room for guests" undersells it. Historically it was closer to a town hall, a law court and a story circle rolled into one.

### Council, court and marketplace

Abu Dhabi Culture describes the majlis as [an essential part of social and political life](https://abudhabiculture.ae/en/Cultural-Heritage/InTangible/Heritage-Register/Social-Practices/Majlis) in the UAE, a forum where the community could raise matters with elders and sheikhs. It sets out traditional types:

- **The barza**, a majlis run by elders, chieftains and sheikhs, where the host hears complaints and requests and seeks to resolve them.
- **The Majlis of the Merchants**, open to all but especially important to pearl divers, who used it to raise funds for their voyages and make sure their families had basic food while they were at sea. It also hosted storytelling and poetry.
- **The Majlis of Justice**, which dealt with judgments and religious education.

Majalis (the plural) were held in tents, in the open air around fires, or in simple shaded structures along the coast, with coffee always served. Abu Dhabi Culture adds that coastal majalis were often [built of plaster and mud, arbour and bower, or simple canvas](https://abudhabiculture.ae/en/Cultural-Heritage/InTangible/Heritage-Register/Social-Practices/Majlis) to give shade, while in the mountains they were held in special guesthouses or other gathering places.

The merchants' majlis had its own rhythm. According to the same source, it was busiest during the pearling season, especially near the end, when pearl prices were negotiated, and it doubled as a place to share stories of incidents at sea and to settle disputes between divers and traders. If you visit a pearling exhibit in a museum, picture those conversations happening over coffee a few streets away.

### Who pays for the welcome

One detail explains a lot about the generosity you may meet. Abu Dhabi Culture notes that the owner of a guesthouse or majlis is [responsible for all hospitality expenses](https://abudhabiculture.ae/en/Cultural-Heritage/InTangible/Heritage-Register/Social-Practices/Majlis), although in some cases tribe members contribute too. Hospitality is not an optional extra; it is part of the host's standing. That is worth remembering if you are ever offered more coffee, dates or food than you expected. Accepting graciously is the polite response.

### Where children learn manners

UNESCO highlights something that explains why the tradition survives: the majlis is [open to all age groups](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/majlis-a-cultural-and-social-space-01076), so knowledge passes informally as children accompany adults. By watching elders, young people learn the manners and ethics of their community, dialogue and listening skills, and respect for the opinion of others. The majlis also carries oral heritage, including folk stories, songs and Nabati poetry.

UNESCO also notes that women have their own majlis, while some prominent women attend others, particularly academic or literary gatherings.

## The majlis today

The majlis has not stayed in the past. Abu Dhabi Culture points out that the term has been [adopted by modern governments](https://abudhabiculture.ae/en/Cultural-Heritage/InTangible/Heritage-Register/Social-Practices/Majlis) to describe both elected and unelected advisory and decision-making bodies, including legislatures and councils of ministers. When you see the word on a government building or in the news, it carries the same idea of people sitting together to talk things through.

At the other end of the scale, the family majlis is still very much alive. Many homes keep a separate sitting room near the entrance for receiving guests, so visitors can be welcomed without entering the private part of the house. Some majlis gatherings are held on particular evenings each week, others spring up around a wedding, a bereavement or a religious holiday. For visitors, the practical point is that a majlis is a real social space with its own rhythm, not a performance staged for outsiders.

## What to look for in the room

Even an empty majlis in a museum can tell you a great deal if you know what to look at.

<photo-set layout="wide" keys="majlis-emirati-hospitality-9" alts="A woven Sadu tent decoration in red, orange, black and white geometric bands with wool tassels along the bottom, displayed in a museum case" caption="Sadu weaving in narrow geometric bands, made to decorate a tent."></photo-set>

Start with the textiles. Many majlis cushions, rugs and wall hangings use or echo Al Sadu, the traditional weaving of Bedouin women. Visit Abu Dhabi explains that [Sadu is woven on a floor loom](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/al-sadu) using a warp-faced plain weave, in traditional colours of black, white, brown, beige and red, with narrow bands of geometric patterns that reflect social identity and the surrounding environment. The wool came from goats and camels, and the yarn was dyed with local plant extracts such as henna or saffron.

The same page notes that Sadu was used to make the Bedouin tent itself, the bait al-shaar, with patterned dividers inside, and that camels were decorated with woven saddle trappings. Women traditionally gathered in small groups to spin and weave, exchanging family news and sometimes reciting poetry, which makes Sadu a social practice as much as a craft. In 2011 it was added to UNESCO's list of intangible heritage in need of urgent safeguarding.

Then look for the coffee corner. A hearth, a set of brass or copper coffee pots and a tray of small handleless cups show where the welcome began. Finally, notice the layout: seating runs around the edges so that everyone can see everyone else, with no obvious head of the table. That arrangement suits the conversation the majlis was built for.

## Coffee: the heart of Emirati hospitality

If the majlis is the stage, coffee is the opening line. Arabic coffee is light in colour, often spiced, and served in very small cups, usually alongside dates.

UNESCO lists Arabic coffee as intangible heritage too, describing it as [a symbol of generosity](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/arabic-coffee-a-symbol-of-generosity-02111) and part of daily life. Its entry spells out etiquette that is useful for any visitor:

- **Older people and guests are served first.** Do not be surprised if the cup comes to you early.
- **The coffee is poured with the right hand.** Take and drink with your right hand too.
- **Shake the cup to finish.** It is customary to shake your cup gently to signal that you are done. Otherwise, your host will keep refilling.

Accept at least one cup, even if you only sip. Turning down coffee outright can feel like turning down the welcome. For a fuller guide to the ritual, see our [Arabic coffee etiquette guide](/stories/arabic-coffee-etiquette/), and for the dates and dishes that often follow, read [Emirati food: what to eat and where](/stories/emirati-food-what-to-eat-where/).

## How a majlis visit usually unfolds

Every gathering is different, but hosted visits tend to follow a recognisable pattern, and knowing it removes most of the nerves.

You arrive, greet your hosts and remove your shoes if others have. You are shown where to sit, usually on floor cushions or low seating along the wall. Coffee comes early, often with dates, and may be followed by tea, sweets or a meal. Conversation moves at an easy pace: questions about where you are from, your family and how you are finding the country are common openers, and it is polite to ask after your hosts in return.

When it is time to go, there is no need for a dramatic exit. Thank your host for the welcome and the coffee, say goodbye to the group, and leave at the pace of the room. If you were invited as part of a hosted experience, your guide will usually signal when the session is ending.

### What to talk about

Good topics are the ones you would raise with any generous host: food, family, travel, the history of the place you are visiting, and the traditions you have seen on your trip. Hosted cultural sessions positively welcome questions about religion and daily life, but in a private setting let your hosts lead on anything sensitive. Avoid criticising the country, its leaders or religion, and keep your tone light and curious rather than argumentative.

## Greetings, shoes and small courtesies

First impressions in a majlis come down to a few simple habits.

### Greetings

Visit Abu Dhabi says greetings are [warm and respectful](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/majlis), often with handshakes or embraces among men or among women, while interaction between men and women is more reserved. A woman will offer her hand if she wishes to shake. The tourism board's advice when unsure is reassuringly simple: a friendly smile is always appreciated. A hand on the heart with a nod is a polite alternative to a handshake.

### Other small courtesies

- Take your shoes off if you see others have done so.
- Wait to be shown where to sit, and avoid pointing the soles of your feet at anyone.
- Stand when an elder enters, if others do.
- Let conversation unfold. A majlis is not rushed, and there is no need to fill every silence.
- Ask before taking photos of people or of the room.

### What to wear

There is no special outfit, but modest clothing shows respect. The UK Foreign Office advises visitors to [dress modestly in public areas](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), with women covering shoulders and knees, and notes that some venues and buildings have specific dress codes. Loose trousers or a long skirt and a top with sleeves will be comfortable sitting on cushions on the floor. Our [what to wear guide](/stories/what-to-wear-uae-etiquette/) has more detail.

## Where visitors can experience a majlis

Most visitors will not be invited into a private majlis, and that is fine. There are good ways to experience the spirit of one.

### A hosted cultural meal in Dubai

In the Al Fahidi Historical Neighbourhood, the Sheikh Mohammed bin Rashid Al Maktoum Centre for Cultural Understanding runs [cultural meals](https://cultures.ae/program/cultural-meals/) where guests share traditional Emirati food while Emirati presenters lead discussions on Emirati culture, the influence of Islam on local traditions, cultural values and daily life. Booking is required. It is a relaxed setting for asking the questions you might feel shy about elsewhere. Our [Al Fahidi Historical Neighbourhood guide](/stories/al-fahidi-historical-neighbourhood/) helps you plan the rest of the area.

### During Eid

Abu Dhabi Culture notes that during the Eid holidays [most majlises offer sweets, dates and other fruit, coffee and tea](https://abudhabiculture.ae/en/Cultural-Heritage/InTangible/Heritage-Register/Social-Practices/Celebrating-Eid) to visiting guests. If your trip falls over Eid, you may notice this generous, open-door mood. Our guide to [visiting the UAE during Eid](/stories/eid-in-the-uae-for-visitors/) explains what else changes.

### Heritage houses and museums

Many restored heritage houses and forts across the emirates include a majlis room. Even empty, the layout of cushions, carpets and coffee pots shows you how gatherings worked. Our [food and culture section](/food-culture/) has more ideas for heritage visits.

## Families, access and comfort

A majlis is usually a family-friendly space, and children are often present in local gatherings. If you bring yours, brief them beforehand: sit with you, accept a date or a drink politely, and avoid climbing on cushions or running about.

Sitting on the floor for a long time is not easy for everyone. If you have back, hip or knee problems, ask in advance whether chairs or higher seating are available; hosted experiences are generally used to the request. Sit cross-legged or with your legs tucked to one side rather than stretched out towards other guests, and change position quietly when you need to.

If you have dietary requirements, mention them when you book a hosted meal rather than at the table. In a private home, simply take a little of what you can eat and thank your host; nobody expects you to finish everything.

## Quick answers

**What is a majlis in Emirati culture?**
It is a "sitting place" or council where guests are welcomed with coffee and conversation, and where communities discuss news, resolve problems and hold gatherings. UNESCO recognised it as intangible cultural heritage in 2015.

**How do I drink Arabic coffee politely?**
Take the cup with your right hand and drink. When you have had enough, gently shake the cup to signal you are finished, as UNESCO's description of the custom explains.

**Should I shake hands in a majlis?**
Visit Abu Dhabi notes that interaction between men and women is more reserved, and a woman will offer her hand if she wishes to shake. When unsure, a friendly smile is always appreciated.

**Can tourists visit a majlis?**
Yes, through hosted experiences such as the cultural meals at the Sheikh Mohammed bin Rashid Al Maktoum Centre for Cultural Understanding in Dubai, which must be booked ahead.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Is this a hosted experience with an Emirati presenter, or a self-guided heritage visit?
- Will we sit on the floor, and are chairs available for anyone with mobility needs?
- What should we wear, and will we need to remove our shoes?
- Is it fine to ask questions about religion and daily life during the session?
- Are photos allowed inside, and of the hosts?
- Can the meal accommodate dietary requirements, and how far ahead must we book?
