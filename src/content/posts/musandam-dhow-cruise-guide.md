The engine drops to a low thud and the dhow turns into an inlet so narrow that the cliffs on either side seem to lean in. The water is glassy and green. Somebody on the cushions at the stern stops talking mid-sentence, because a fin has just broken the surface off the bow.

That quiet, slightly disbelieving moment is what most people remember about a Musandam dhow cruise: a wooden boat, a day on the water, swimming stops and mountains that fall straight into the sea. It is gentle rather than thrilling, and it suits almost everyone.

The short answer to "is it worth it?" is yes, if you want a slow, scenic day and you have checked the entry rules for your passport before you set off. You are crossing an international border, so this is not quite as simple as a trip to another emirate. Here is how the day works, what to sort out first and how to get the most from it.

## Where Musandam is, and why it feels so different

Musandam is a governorate of Oman, separated from the rest of the country by UAE territory. Oman's official tourism site describes it as a place where [steep mountains meet calm seas](https://experienceoman.om/destinations/musandam), looking out across the Sea of Oman, the Arabian Gulf and the Strait of Hormuz. It names four wilayats (districts): Khasab, Bukha, Dibba and Madha. It lists boat tours, mountain treks and diving as the main activities, and notes that Musandam can be reached by land, sea and air.

The same page gives each district its own character. Khasab is known for its forts, Bukha for sandy beaches, Dibba for its mountain and coastal scenery, and Madha for freshwater springs and valleys. Which one you end up in depends on where your boat leaves from, so ask your operator which side of the peninsula the trip uses before you compare itineraries.

For most visitors from the UAE, "Musandam" means a day on a dhow among the khors, the long fjord-like inlets that cut deep into the mountains. ## A Musandam dhow cruise, hour by hour

Most trips follow a similar rhythm, though routes and timings vary by operator, departure point and the state of the sea.

### Boarding and the first hour

Dhows are broad, wooden and slow, with a shaded deck of carpets and cushions. Shoes usually come off. The first stretch tends to hug the coast, past bare cliffs and small fishing villages tucked into coves. If you burn easily, claim a spot in the middle of the shade.

This is also the moment to look around the boat properly. Note where the life jackets are kept, how you get down to the water for swimming, and where the shade runs out. Ladders on the side of a dhow can be steep and wet, so it helps to know the route before you need it.

### Into the khors

The heart of the day is the slow run into the inlets. The water calms, the walls rise higher and the boat's wake becomes the only movement. Dolphins are often spotted in these waters, though no honest crew will promise them. If they do appear, the crew may slow the engine and let them come to the boat; resist the urge to lean far over the rail for a photo. Our [UAE marine life guide](/stories/uae-marine-life-guide/) explains what else lives in these waters.

Many itineraries also pass Telegraph Island, a small rocky islet with the ruins of an old British telegraph station on it. Some boats stop nearby for a swim; others simply circle it while the crew points out the stone steps and walls.

### Swimming and snorkelling stops

Most cruises anchor in a sheltered bay so you can swim or snorkel off the side. Ask in advance whether gear is provided and whether a crew member watches the water while people swim. The UK Foreign Office simply points travellers to [water safety guidance](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/oman/safety-and-security) for swimming in Oman, so the practical precautions are yours to take: swim in pairs, wear a life jacket if you are not confident, and keep children within arm's reach.

### Lunch and the run home

Lunch is usually served on board and eaten cross-legged on the carpets; flag allergies when you book, not on the boat. The return leg is the sleepiest part of the day, with the light softening on the cliffs.

## Choosing the right trip for you

Dhow days are sold in several shapes, and the right one depends on who is travelling.

- **Shared or private boat.** A shared boat is sociable and usually simpler to book; a private one lets you set the pace, linger at a swimming stop and skip the parts that do not interest you.
- **Half day or full day.** Longer trips reach deeper into the khors and allow more time in the water. Shorter trips suit young children and anyone prone to seasickness.
- **With or without border transfers.** Some trips start in Ras Al Khaimah and include the road crossing; others assume you will make your own way to the harbour. Be clear which you are buying.
- **Dhow or speedboat.** Speedboats cover more water quickly but are bumpier and offer less shade. The dhow is the calmer, more traditional choice.

Whatever you choose, ask what is included in writing: transfers, lunch, drinking water, snorkelling gear and any entry fees ashore.

## Crossing the border: the checks that matter

### Entry rules for Oman

Rules depend on your nationality, so check them for your own passport. For British citizens, the UK Foreign Office says you can [visit Oman for up to 14 days without a visa](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/oman/entry-requirements), and that your passport must be valid for at least six months after you arrive. It also says that at Omani border control you must be able to show:

- a ticket for return or onward travel
- proof of accommodation
- proof of your travel health insurance

Carry your passport itself, not a copy. On other passports, ask your operator which rules apply and confirm them with official sources. You will also pass through UAE immigration again on the way back, so check that your UAE status allows you to leave and re-enter.

### Medicines and other customs rules

Oman has its own customs rules. The Foreign Office notes that some UK prescription and over-the-counter medicines are [illegal in Oman](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/oman/entry-requirements), and advises carrying a copy of your prescription and checking with Oman's Ministry of Health well before you travel. It also says it is illegal to [import or use e-cigarettes or vape products](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/oman/safety-and-security) in Oman, so leave your vape at the hotel.

## Safety on the water

A dhow is a gentle way to see the sea, but it is still a boat in open water.

### Life jackets and boat standards

The Foreign Office warns that the safety of [tourist boats in Oman may not be up to UK standards](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/oman/safety-and-security) and advises making sure life jackets are available for all passengers. It gives the same advice for [tourist boats in the UAE](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security). Children's sizes matter too. Ask before you book, and look for them when you board.

### Regional tensions

Musandam faces the Strait of Hormuz, and that matters at the moment. The Foreign Office says the context in the Middle East is [deeply unpredictable](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/oman/warnings-and-insurance), with a high risk of sudden escalation and possible flight cancellations and airspace closures. It also notes that vessels in the [Gulf of Oman may be at increased risk of maritime attack](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/oman/safety-and-security). Read the latest advice for both countries before you book, check it again before you travel, and make sure your insurance covers the trip.

### Heat, sun and health

Even on a breezy deck, the sun is strong. The Foreign Office says extreme heat has [caused deaths in Oman due to dehydration and heat exhaustion](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/oman/health), so keep drinking, stay in the shade between swims and reapply sun cream every time you come out of the water.

The same page says British nationals must use the private healthcare system in Oman, and that visitors without travel insurance or the means to pay may be stopped from leaving until the debt is settled. That is one more reason to check that your policy covers Oman as well as the UAE. The emergency number in Oman is [9999 for ambulance, fire and police](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/oman/getting-help); save it before you cross.

## When to go

The cooler months are the most comfortable time to be on deck. Oman's tourism site gives the best time to visit [Khasab Fort](https://experienceoman.om/destinations/musandam/khasab-fort) as October to April, which is a fair guide for the region as a whole. The Foreign Office says tropical cyclones from the Indian Ocean [sometimes affect Oman in July and August](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/oman/safety-and-security), disrupting services, and that wadis are prone to flash flooding after heavy rain from October to March. If your day includes a mountain drive as well as the boat, check the forecast the night before. See our [guide to the best time to visit](/stories/best-time-to-visit-uae-month-by-month/) for how the seasons feel across the region.

If your trip falls during Ramadan, the Foreign Office advises [not eating, drinking or smoking in public in the daytime](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/oman/safety-and-security) in Oman, and not playing loud music. Ask your operator how lunch on board is handled during the holy month.

## Families, older travellers and accessibility

A dhow day is one of the easier excursions for mixed groups, with a few caveats.

- **Children.** Confirm that the boat carries life jackets in children's sizes, and bring snacks they like in case lunch does not appeal. Sheltered swimming stops suit confident young swimmers best.
- **Limited mobility.** Boarding usually means stepping across from a quay, and getting into the water means a ladder. Ask how the crew helps passengers on and off, and whether the boat can come alongside a proper jetty.
- **Seasickness.** The khors are usually calm, but the open stretches can roll. Sit low and near the middle of the boat, and look at the horizon.
- **Ashore.** Oman's tourism site lists both [Khasab Fort](https://experienceoman.om/destinations/musandam/khasab-fort) and the [Madha Museum](https://experienceoman.om/destinations/musandam/madha-museum) as wheelchair accessible, which makes either a good add-on for anyone who prefers a shorter time on the water.

## What catches people out

- **Forgetting the passport.** A copy will not get you across the border.
- **Assuming the visa rules are the same as the UAE's.** They are not, and they vary by passport.
- **Packing like a beach day.** You may stop in villages or at the fort, where beachwear is not appropriate.
- **Bringing a drone or a vape.** Both carry legal risk in Oman.
- **Leaving too little time.** Border queues add to the day, so do not plan a tight dinner booking for the evening.

## Respecting the rules on board and ashore

A few of Oman's rules apply directly to a boat day.

- **Dress.** The Foreign Office says swimwear should not be worn in public areas [except on tourist beaches or at swimming pools](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/oman/safety-and-security). Swim from the boat, then put a layer on before any stop ashore or in a village.
- **Photos.** It advises not to photograph people without their permission, and that photographing some government and military sites is illegal.
- **Drones.** Flying a drone without a valid licence is illegal, according to the same page. Leave it at home.
- **Alcohol.** The Foreign Office says it is illegal to drink alcohol in public or be drunk in public, and that border police may arrest people passing through seaports under the influence.

## Adding Khasab on land

If you have time before or after the boat, Khasab itself is worth an hour. Oman's tourism site describes [Khasab Fort](https://experienceoman.om/destinations/musandam/khasab-fort) as built at the beginning of the 17th century, square with four corner towers, overlooking the Strait of Hormuz. It gives opening hours of 8am to 6pm daily, with tickets at the reception desk. Hours can change, so check before you go.

The same page adds detail worth knowing before you walk in. The fort is built of mud, gravel and sarooj, a traditional mortar, and its oldest part is the round central tower. Over the centuries it has served as the governor's residence, housing for soldiers and later a court. Boats were once kept there for defence and transport, and old wooden boats still stand in the grounds today. Guided tours are available on request, and photography is permitted.

If you are heading towards Madha instead, Oman's tourism site says the [Madha Museum](https://experienceoman.om/destinations/musandam/madha-museum) holds archaeological and heritage pieces including rock inscriptions, fossils, coins, manuscripts, women's jewellery and pottery.

## What to bring on the day

- Passport, visa documents and proof of travel health insurance
- Swimwear, a towel and a cover-up for stops ashore
- Sun cream, sunglasses and a hat
- A light layer for the breeze on the way home
- Motion sickness tablets if you need them, cleared for entry to Oman
- A dry bag for your phone
- Plenty of drinking water, even if the boat provides some

## Quick answers

**Do I need a visa for a Musandam dhow cruise?**
It depends on your passport. The UK Foreign Office says British citizens can visit Oman for up to 14 days without a visa, but must show return travel, accommodation and travel health insurance at the border.

**Are life jackets provided on dhows?**
Do not assume so. The Foreign Office says tourist boat safety may not be up to UK standards and advises making sure life jackets are available for every passenger, so ask before you book and check when you board.

**Can I fly my drone in Musandam?**
No, not without a valid licence. The Foreign Office says flying drones without one, or in restricted airspace, is illegal in Oman.

**When is the best time to go?**
The cooler months are most comfortable. Oman's tourism site gives October to April as the best time to visit Khasab Fort.

**Is it safe right now?**
Check the latest Foreign Office advice for Oman and the UAE before booking and again before travel. It currently highlights regional tensions and an increased risk to vessels in the Gulf of Oman.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Which border crossing do we use, and what entry documents will each of our passports need on the way in and back?
- Are life jackets on board for every passenger, including children's sizes, and is there enough shade for everyone?
- Who watches the water during swimming stops, and is snorkelling gear included or should we bring our own?
- What happens if the sea is rough or the border is busy: do you change the route, delay or refund?
- What is served at lunch, and can you handle allergies or vegetarian, vegan or halal requirements?
- How do you keep up with travel advisories in the Strait of Hormuz area, and what is your plan if advice changes before our date?
