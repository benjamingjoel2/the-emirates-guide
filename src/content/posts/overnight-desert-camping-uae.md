The last car of day-trippers pulls away, its headlights bouncing over the dune, and the desert goes quiet in a way cities never do. The fire ticks. Somewhere out in the dark a fox barks. Above you, the sky fills with more stars than you knew were there.

Camping overnight in the UAE desert is how you see the sand after the show has ended. Most visitors do it with an organised camp, which handles the tents, the food and the driving, and that is the approach we recommend for a first night. You get the silence, the sky and the sunrise, without needing to know how to drive soft sand in the dark. Here is how it works, where to go and what to bring.

## What a night in the desert is really like

An organised overnight usually begins in the afternoon with a 4x4 transfer, a sunset stop on a high dune and arrival at camp as the light fades. Then comes dinner, often grilled meat, rice and salads, eaten on cushions around a fire or under a tent. After dinner, as other visitors leave, the camp settles.

PHOTO1

That is when the desert shows its best side. The temperature drops, the stars sharpen and conversation slows. Sleeping arrangements vary widely: from mattresses in shared tents to private tents with proper beds, or simply a bedroll under the open sky. Sunrise comes early and cold, then warms fast, and most camps serve a simple breakfast before the drive back.

It is not luxury, even when it is comfortable. Expect sand in everything, basic toilets at many camps, and limited phone signal. If those things sound like part of the charm, you will love it.

### How the evening unfolds

The rhythm of a camp night is worth knowing in advance, because it shapes how much you enjoy it. The first part of the evening is often the busiest: day visitors and overnight guests share the camp, there may be activities such as camel rides or sandboarding, and dinner is served while it is still lively. The quiet comes later, once the day groups have driven back to the city and the generators or lights are dimmed.

That second half is the reason to stay. Take a blanket a short way from the fire, let your eyes adjust to the dark for a few minutes, and the sky changes completely. If you can choose your dates, a night around the new moon gives the darkest skies; a full moon lights the dunes beautifully but washes out fainter stars. Either way, the hour before sunrise is worth setting an alarm for, as the sand turns from grey to gold and the air is at its coolest.

## Where to camp: three different deserts

### Dubai's inland desert and the conservation reserve

The easiest option from the city is a camp in Dubai's inland desert. The Dubai Desert Conservation Reserve lists [camping](https://www.ddcr.org/what-to-do/) among its activities, describing a drive with professional safari guides to a camp with a tent and a traditional Arabian dinner, as well as night safaris under a star-filled sky. The reserve says it recycles [waste at all the tour operators' camps](https://www.ddcr.org/about/) and that dune camps sit in a limited vehicle access zone, away from its most sensitive areas.

Camping in or near a protected area tends to be quieter, with fewer groups sharing the same patch of sand. Night is also when much of the reserve's wildlife is active, so a guided night safari before bed can add a lot. Our guide to the [Dubai Desert Conservation Reserve](/stories/dubai-desert-conservation-reserve-guide/) explains how its zones and approved tours work.

### Liwa and the edge of the Empty Quarter

For the full scale of the dunes, head to Liwa in Abu Dhabi's Al Dhafra region. Visit Abu Dhabi says the drive from Abu Dhabi city to Al Dhafra [typically takes around two to three hours](https://www.visitabudhabi.ae/en/where-to-go/regions/al-dhafra), that a car is the most convenient way to explore, and that the cooler months are ideal for desert experiences, while warmer months suit coastal stays and indoor attractions better. It points visitors to Tel Moreeb and the Liwa dunes, and notes the Liwa International Festival each December, with three weeks of desert motorsports and cultural events, so expect busier dunes then.

The UAE government describes desert as the dominant landscape, stretching from the Gulf coast to [the uninhabited sands of the Empty Quarter](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/environment-and-energy/Nature-and-wildlife/topography-and-ecosystems). A night out here feels genuinely remote, and the long drive means it works best as part of a two or three day trip rather than a single night out from Dubai. Our [Liwa and Empty Quarter guide](/stories/liwa-empty-quarter-guide/) covers the area in depth.

### Hatta, for mountains instead of dunes

If rock appeals more than sand, Hatta in Dubai's mountains is the alternative. Visit Dubai lists [camping alongside hiking, kayaking and mountain biking](https://www.visitdubai.com/en/beyond-dubai/nature-adventures-in-hatta) there. It is a different kind of night, with cooler air, rocky ridges and a lake nearby, and it pairs well with a morning on the water. See our [Hatta guide](/stories/hatta-guide/).

## Choosing the right camp for you

Camps range from simple to very comfortable, and the right one depends on who you are travelling with and what you want from the night. A few points separate them:

- **Shared or private.** Some camps put overnight guests in shared tents; others offer private tents or small cabins. Ask exactly what you get.
- **Beds or bedrolls.** Mattresses on the ground are common. If anyone in your group struggles to get up from floor level, ask for a raised bed.
- **Toilets and washing.** Facilities range from basic toilets to proper bathrooms. This matters more than most people expect, especially at 3am.
- **Group size.** A camp that hosts large evening crowds can still be quiet overnight, but only if most guests leave. Ask how many stay.
- **Power.** Charging points may be limited or only available while the generator runs.

If your group has mixed expectations, err towards more comfort. A bad night's sleep is the most common reason people say they would not camp again.

### Food and dietary needs

Dinner at most camps is a set menu, often a barbecue with rice, salads and bread. Vegetarian options are usually available, but other needs, such as allergies or a gluten-free diet, are much easier to meet if you mention them when you book. Bring a few snacks of your own; the gap between dinner and breakfast can feel long in cold air. Drink plenty of water through the evening too. Cool desert air hides how much you are losing, and a headache at midnight is often simple dehydration rather than anything more worrying.

## Desert camping on your own: be realistic

Some residents camp independently in the desert, but it is a different undertaking from joining an organised trip. You need a properly equipped 4x4, experience of driving on sand, recovery gear, and an up-to-date understanding of local rules, which are set at emirate level. Check with the relevant municipality or environment authority before planning a self-drive camp.

The UK Foreign Office is direct about the risks. It warns that [desert excursions can be dangerous](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) unless you are in a properly equipped 4-wheel-drive vehicle, and advises always travelling with other cars, taking a supply of water and a mobile phone, and leaving a copy of your travel plans with relatives, friends or where you are staying. For most visitors, that settles it: book a camp.

## Weather, cold nights and flash floods

The desert is not always hot. Nights in the cooler months can feel properly cold, especially after a warm afternoon, and wind can pick up sand in a hurry.

### Rain and wadis

The UAE government notes that the mountain valleys are usually dry, but [in cooler months when it rains they flourish with brooks and pools](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/environment-and-energy/Nature-and-wildlife/topography-and-ecosystems). That water comes fast. The Foreign Office warns that [heavy rain in the UAE can cause flooding](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) and advises checking forecasts from the [National Centre of Meteorology](https://www.ncm.gov.ae/) and following local advice. Never camp in a wadi bed, especially in the mountains around Hatta.

### Warnings on your phone

The UAE runs a National Early Warning System that [sends text messages to warn people of dangerous weather](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/justice-safety-and-the-law/Safety/handling-emergencies) and tells them what to do to stay safe, and the government says it covers visitors as well as residents. Read any alerts that arrive, and ask your camp what its plan is if one does.

The same government page lists the emergency numbers to save before you go: 999 for police, 998 for an ambulance, 997 for the fire department and 995 for search and rescue. It also notes that in Abu Dhabi you can use the police AD999 app to request help, which is worth installing before a trip to Liwa.

### Heat

On warmer days, heat is the bigger risk. The NHS advises [drinking more cold drinks, wearing light-coloured, loose clothing and avoiding the sun between 11am and 3pm](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/heat-exhaustion-heatstroke/), and lists heatstroke as a medical emergency. ## Camping with children

A desert night can be a highlight for children: the sand is a giant playground, the fire feels like an adventure, and the stars are unlike anything they see at home. A little planning keeps it that way. Choose a camp with proper toilets and private tents, bring a familiar blanket or pillow, and pack a head torch for each child so they can find their way without wandering into the dark. Keep children well away from the fire pit and from any vehicles moving around the camp, especially after dark when drivers may not see them.

Younger children may struggle with the late evening and the early start, so consider whether a sunset trip without the overnight suits them better for a first taste of the desert. Our guide to [Dubai with children](/stories/dubai-with-kids/) has other family ideas.

## What to pack for a night on the sand

Camps provide the essentials, but a small bag of your own makes the night much better.

- **Warm layers.** A fleece or light jacket for the evening and early morning.
- **A head torch** for walking to the toilets and around camp.
- **Closed shoes or sandals you can shake out,** plus socks for the night.
- **A scarf or buff** to keep sand off your face if the wind rises.
- **Water and snacks,** even if meals are included.
- **Sun protection** for the evening drive and the morning after: hat, sunglasses, sunscreen.
- **A power bank,** since charging may be limited.
- **Wet wipes and a small bag** to carry your rubbish back out.
- **Any medication** you need, kept with you rather than in the vehicle.

Dress modestly around camp, as you would elsewhere in the UAE. Keep your bag zipped and your shoes inside the tent or upside down at night; sand gets into everything left open.

## Camp etiquette

A shared camp works best when everyone remembers that some guests came for the quiet. Keep music low or off once the day visitors have gone, use a dim light rather than a bright phone torch near other tents, and ask before photographing staff or other guests. If the camp has a majlis area with coffee and dates, accept with your right hand. Our guide to [Arabic coffee etiquette](/stories/arabic-coffee-etiquette/) explains the customs.

## Leaving the desert as you found it

Desert plants grow slowly and tracks last a long time. Carry out everything you bring in, keep fires in the pits provided, never take plants or wood from the desert, and keep your distance from wildlife. Choosing a camp that can explain where its waste goes is the simplest way to help. If you are tempted to collect a little sand or a stone as a souvenir, take a photograph instead, and resist the urge to drive or walk on fragile vegetation for a better view.

## Quick answers

**Can tourists camp overnight in the UAE desert?** Yes. The easiest way is an organised camp, such as the guided camping trips listed by the Dubai Desert Conservation Reserve, which handle transport, tents and meals.

**Is desert camping safe?** With a reputable operator, it generally is. The Foreign Office warns that desert excursions can be dangerous without a properly equipped 4-wheel-drive vehicle and advises travelling with other cars, water and a phone.

**Where is the best place to camp?** Dubai's inland desert is closest to the city, Liwa offers the biggest dunes around two to three hours' drive from Abu Dhabi city, and Hatta suits those who prefer mountains.

**What if it rains?** Heavy rain can cause flooding. Check the National Centre of Meteorology forecast, watch for official text alerts, and never camp in a wadi bed.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Where exactly is the camp, and is it inside a conservation reserve or another managed area?
- What are the sleeping arrangements: shared or private tents, mattresses or beds, and is bedding provided?
- What toilet and washing facilities are there, and is there any power for charging?
- How cold does it get at night in the month we are travelling, and do you provide blankets?
- What is your plan if a weather warning is issued during the night?
- How many guests stay overnight, and how many leave after dinner?
