The light is perfect. The sun is dropping behind the mountains, the sky is turning apricot, and somewhere in your luggage is a drone that would turn this into the shot of the trip. Before you unpack it, stop.

Photography and drone rules in the UAE are two very different stories. With an ordinary camera or phone, you can photograph almost anything you see as a visitor, as long as you follow a few clear rules about people and sensitive sites. Drones are another matter. Under the current federal regulation, recreational drone flying is limited to registered UAE residents, which in practice means most tourists cannot fly one at all.

Here is how both work, based on official UAE and UK government sources. Rules change, especially for drones, so check the linked pages before you travel.

## Your camera: what is fine and what is not

Skylines, souks, desert dunes, museum courtyards, plates of food: point your camera at all of them. Visit Abu Dhabi says [normal tourist photography is acceptable](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/faqs). The rules that matter are about who and what is in the frame.

Bringing the kit itself is straightforward. The UAE government's customs page lists [video and digital cameras for personal use](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/visiting-and-exploring-the-uae/Tourist-services-and-information/Customs-clearance), along with portable computers and mobile phones, among the items travellers can bring in without paying duty. The same page lists transmission and wireless devices among restricted goods that need prior approval, which is worth bearing in mind if you travel with specialist radio equipment.

### Photographing people

This is the big one. The UK Foreign Office says plainly: [do not photograph people without their permission](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security). It adds that men have been arrested for photographing women on beaches.

Visit Abu Dhabi gives the same advice in gentler terms, saying it is polite to [ask permission before taking photos of people](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/faqs), particularly women. It also notes that cameras may be banned in public areas set aside for women and children only.

In practice:

- Ask before you photograph anyone, and accept a no gracefully.
- Take extra care at beaches, pools, parks and family areas.
- Frame wide shots of busy places so individuals are not the subject.
- Never photograph children who are not with you.
- Delete a photo if someone asks you to.

Families have an extra thing to watch. Photographing your own children on a public beach or in a mall is part of any holiday, but check the background before you press the shutter. If other families, and especially other people's children or women in swimwear, are clearly in the frame, move, wait or reframe. It takes a few seconds and avoids an uncomfortable conversation.

Privacy also matters after you press the shutter. The UAE government says its media rules include [respecting privacy rules and individuals' private lives](https://u.ae/en/media/media-in-the-uae/media-regulation), and the country's cybercrime law covers what you share online. Think twice before posting identifiable photos of strangers.

### Government, military and airport sites

The Foreign Office says it is [illegal to take photos of some government buildings and military installations](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), and that hobbies like bird watching and plane spotting may be misunderstood near military sites, government buildings and airports. Visit Abu Dhabi adds [ports and airports](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/faqs) to the list of places not to photograph.

That makes the airport itself a place to keep the camera low. A photo of your family at the gate is one thing; long lenses pointed at runways, security areas or aircraft movements are another. If you are a keen birder, our [birdwatching guide](/stories/birdwatching-in-the-uae/) suggests places where binoculars and big lenses are expected.

The current regional picture adds a further reason for care. The Foreign Office's latest warnings advise visitors to [stay away from areas around security or military facilities](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/warnings-and-insurance), and not to approach or touch any drone or missile fragments or unusual objects. Do not go looking for photographs of anything like that, and move away if you come across it.

### Mosques, palaces and museums

Landmarks that welcome visitors, such as palaces and mosques that run public tours, usually set out their own photography rules. Follow the signs and any instructions from staff. Visit Abu Dhabi notes that non-Muslims can learn about Islam through [complimentary guided tours of the Sheikh Zayed Grand Mosque](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/faqs), and that mosques have their own dress requirements, so check before you go. Remember that people at prayer are not part of the scenery: keep your distance and your voice down. Our guides to the [Sheikh Zayed Grand Mosque](/stories/sheikh-zayed-grand-mosque-visitor-guide/) and [Qasr Al Watan](/stories/qasr-al-watan-visit/) cover what to expect.

### People in national dress, guides and performers

Many Emiratis wear traditional dress in public. Visit Abu Dhabi describes the [kandura worn by men](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/faqs) with a white or red checked headdress, and the abaya and sheyla worn by women, some of whom also wear a face veil. It is natural to find the clothing striking, but the person wearing it is not part of the scenery. The same rule applies as for anyone else: ask first, and if the answer is no, put the camera down.

On tours, the people you meet are often happy to be photographed, but it is still their choice. Falconers, camel handlers, guides, dancers and craftspeople at heritage sites may welcome a photo, may prefer you to photograph the bird, the animal or the work rather than their face, or may decline altogether. A quick question to your guide before the stop saves any awkwardness, and showing someone the picture afterwards is a friendly habit that is usually appreciated.

## Posting your pictures online

The rules do not stop when you leave the country, because what you post can still fall under UAE law if it involves people or places there. The UAE government says its cybercrime law and [other laws protecting privacy and reputation](https://u.ae/en/media/media-in-the-uae/media-regulation), including defamation, apply while using social media. Among the categories of online content restricted under the country's internet access policy are invasion of privacy, insult, slander and defamation, and contempt of religion.

A few simple habits keep you on the right side of that:

- Crop or blur strangers who are clearly identifiable, especially children.
- Do not post photos that mock people, religious practice or national symbols.
- Avoid geotagging images of private homes or people's faces.
- Think about captions as well as pictures; an insulting comment can be the problem even when the photo is harmless.

## Big lenses, content creators and media permits

If you are travelling with serious kit, or planning to make content, read this part closely.

The Foreign Office warns that you [may need a licence to use equipment](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) like satellite phones, recording devices, radio transmitters, powerful cameras or binoculars, and suggests getting advice from the UAE embassy before you travel.

It also says you must apply for the appropriate permissions to carry out media activity, including the production and distribution of printed, digital, audio, video or visual information, and that failing to do so could lead to imprisonment and a substantial fine. The UAE government defines a media activity as the production, circulation, printing or publishing of media content, and says the activity [may be at a cost or free of charge](https://u.ae/en/media/media-in-the-uae/media-regulation). Its list of media activities includes imaging activities and digital and electronic media.

If you are a professional photographer, a filmmaker or an influencer working with brands, check what permits apply to you before you arrive. Holiday snaps for your own album are a different matter, but the line can blur once content is being made for an audience.

## Drones: why most visitors should leave theirs at home

Picture the scene from the start of this piece again. The answer to "can I fly my drone here?" is, for most tourists, no.

### What the current regulation says

The General Civil Aviation Authority's regulation for recreational drone flights, [Issue 04 dated 3 September 2025](https://www.gcaa.gov.ae/en/epublication/EPublications/Civil%20Aviation%20Regulations%20%28CARs%29/EMERGING%20TECHNOLOGIES/UNMANNED%20AIR%20SYSTEMS%20%28UAS%29/CAR-UAR%20-%20UNMANNED%20AIRCRAFT%20UA%20RECREATIONAL%20FLIGHTS%20-%20ISSUE%2004.pdf), sets out who may fly for fun and how. It defines a recreational flight as one for fun and enjoyment only, excluding work, business or hire. Under it, a recreational pilot must:

- Register on the UAE Drones platform via UAE Pass.
- Be at least 18 years old.
- Be a UAE resident.
- Hold a training certificate from an approved training organisation before flying.
- Register the drone, which must have a remote identification system.
- Obtain flight approval before each flight and use the UAE Drones app during it.

The same regulation sets operating limits: daylight only, within visual line of sight, at no more than 400 feet above ground, with a drone weighing no more than 5 kilograms, away from airports and helicopter landing pads, and over permitted areas only. Pilots must avoid flying over gatherings and respect people's privacy. It also prohibits fitting a recreational drone with a camera or any device that can film or record.

That last point is worth reading twice. Even for residents who qualify, the recreational category is not about aerial photography.

### The platform

The [UAE Drones platform](https://drones.gov.ae/) is where pilots sign up, request flight approvals and get live airspace updates. Operators log in with UAE Pass, the national digital identity.

### Older guidance you may find online

Search for UAE drone rules and you will find older pages describing a simpler system. The UAE government portal, for example, still lists rules such as [registering with the GCAA](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/justice-safety-and-the-law/Safety/aviation-safety), flying in green zones shown in an app, and staying more than 5 kilometres from airports. The GCAA's own registration page states that [all recreational drones, regardless of weight](https://www.gcaa.gov.ae/en/pages/UASRegistration.aspx/), must be registered, and lists penalties including imprisonment and fines for flying without authorisation or over prohibited areas. Where older pages and the latest regulation differ, the regulation's own header says to check the GCAA website for the current version.

### If something goes wrong with a drone

For those who do fly legally, the regulation says that any accident or safety incident must be reported straight away to the GCAA or to the local emirate police. It also prohibits modifying a drone in ways that change its approved specification, and carrying goods.

### Commercial and professional filming

Aerial filming for work is a separate category. The government portal says organisations need an [Unmanned Aircraft Operator Authorisation](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/justice-safety-and-the-law/Safety/aviation-safety) and must apply for security clearance if a flight involves a camera or other recording device, in any flying zone. If you want aerial footage for a wedding, a business or a campaign, the realistic route is a licensed local company rather than your own drone. Ask to see their authorisation before you book.

## Great shots without breaking a rule

None of this should put you off bringing a camera. The UAE is wonderfully photogenic, and the best images rarely need a drone.

- Go early or late. The soft light at sunrise and in the hour before sunset flatters dunes, mountains and old stone alike. Visit Abu Dhabi says that in winter the sun [rises between 6.30am and 7am](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/faqs) and sets between 6pm and 7pm, and that in summer it can rise as early as 5.30am. Our [Jebel Jais guide](/stories/jebel-jais-guide/) is a good start for mountain light.
- Find height legally. Viewing decks and observation points give you the aerial feel without a drone.
- Shoot the details: wind towers, carved doors, spice sacks and coffee pots.
- Ask, smile and show people the picture. A friendly exchange often leads to better portraits than a sneaky snap.

### Looking after your kit

Heat and dust are harder on cameras than rules are. Visit Abu Dhabi describes summer highs of [around 42C](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/faqs), and its advice that air conditioning indoors can be chilly hints at the other problem: stepping from a cold car or mall into humid outdoor air can fog a lens instantly. Give cameras a few minutes to adjust before shooting, keep spare batteries out of direct sun, and use a bag or a lens cloth to keep sand off sensors on desert trips.

## Quick answers

**Can I fly a drone in the UAE as a tourist?** In practice, generally not. The current GCAA regulation requires recreational pilots to be UAE residents aged 18 or over, registered on the UAE Drones platform.

**Can I take photos of people in the UAE?** Only with their permission. The Foreign Office warns that photographing people without consent can lead to arrest.

**Where is photography not allowed in the UAE?** Some government buildings, military installations, ports and airports should not be photographed, and cameras may be banned in areas reserved for women and children.

**Do I need a permit to film content in the UAE?** Media activity, including producing and distributing video or visual content, requires the appropriate permissions, according to the Foreign Office.

**Can I post photos of strangers online?** Be careful. UAE privacy, defamation and cybercrime laws apply to social media, so avoid posting identifiable strangers, especially children, without consent.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Are there stops on our tour where photography is restricted, or where we should ask before taking pictures?
- What time of day gives the best light at each place on our itinerary?
- If we want aerial footage, can you recommend a licensed provider rather than us flying our own drone?
- Is it acceptable to photograph the guides, falconers or performers we meet on tours?
- Do any venues on our itinerary charge or require permission for professional camera equipment?
