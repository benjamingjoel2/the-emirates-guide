Stand at the gate of Qasr Al Hosn and look up. Glass towers rise on every side, traffic hums along the avenues, and in the middle of it all sits a pale fort of coral and sea stone that was here long before any of them.

That contrast is the whole point of a visit. Qasr Al Hosn is, in the words of the site itself, [the oldest and most significant building in Abu Dhabi](https://www.qasralhosn.ae/en/discover), and it holds the city's first permanent structure: a watchtower built around the 1790s to watch over the coastal trade routes and protect the young settlement on the island. If you want to understand how Abu Dhabi grew from a pearling and fishing town into a capital, this is where to start. Plan on visiting between Saturday and Thursday from the morning, or on Friday afternoon, and give yourself time for the free spaces around the fort as well as the fort itself.

## From watchtower to fort: the story of Qasr Al Hosn

### Water, and a reason to stay

The fort's own [history pages](https://www.qasralhosn.ae/en/history) tell the story in chapters. It begins with the seasonal movements of the Bani Yas tribe, who found natural water on the island that would become Abu Dhabi. The site notes that this water was not a single well but shallow pools known as "scrapes". Sheikh Dhiyab bin Isa, Sheikh of the Bani Yas from the inland Liwa Oasis, ordered a watchtower built of coral and sea stone to overlook the coast and protect the community growing around it.

### A seat of power

According to the same history, it was in [1795 that Sheikh Shakhbut bin Dhiyab moved the seat of power](https://www.qasralhosn.ae/en/history) from Liwa to Abu Dhabi island. During his rule, which the site dates from 1793 to 1816, he ordered more towers, linked into a defensive enclosure that commanded the nearby shipping routes. The fort became his seat of government, his military headquarters and his family home. The site adds that it was first known as "Hosn Abu Dhabi", meaning simply Abu Dhabi Fort, and that from the start only those who controlled its walls could claim the right to rule.

### Pearls, then oil

The nineteenth century brought growth. The site says that in 1855 Sheikh Zayed bin Khalifa, known as Zayed the Great, united the tribes of the region, that his rule of more than half a century was the longest in Abu Dhabi's history, and that by the late 1800s the town had [over 400 pearling boats](https://www.qasralhosn.ae/en/history), the largest number in the Gulf. When the pearl trade collapsed, with global tensions and the invention of cultured pearls in Japan, the fort was again at the centre of change: in 1939, Sheikh Shakhbut bin Sultan Al Nahyan negotiated the country's first oil concessions, with Great Britain. Using some of the funds, he built a palace enclosing the original fort, tripling the size of the site, and it quickly became a symbol of the town's rising prosperity. The decorative arch outside the gates was added in the early 1950s.

### A home, then an archive

The fort was also a family home. The Sheikh Zayed Grand Mosque Centre notes that Sheikh Zayed bin Sultan Al Nahyan, the founder of the UAE, was [born at Qasr Al Hosn in 1918](https://www.szgmc.gov.ae/en/about/the-mosque-and-the-founder). The fort's history pages say that when he came to power it remained at the heart of his plans, serving as his family home and seat of government, and that he ordered a full restoration with the aim of turning it into the city's national archive. Over the decades, the site sums up, it has been home to the ruling family, the seat of government, a consultative council and a national archive.

### The building where a nation was discussed

Next to the fort stands the National Consultative Council building. The site says it was [founded by the late Sheikh Zayed bin Sultan Al Nahyan between 1968 and 1970](https://www.qasralhosn.ae/en/discover), and that it hosted federal and national debates until the late 1990s. The history pages add that meetings to negotiate the union of the United Arab Emirates in 1971 took place in the chamber. Its roots, the site notes, lie in the traditional ideas of the majlis and shura, consultation and council.

## Why the walls changed colour

Older photos often show Qasr Al Hosn bright white. The [history pages](https://www.qasralhosn.ae/en/history) explain why that changed: the white render covering the walls was found to be trapping corrosive moisture against the original coral stone. A major conservation project removed it so the structure could "breathe", revealing the original stonework. The conservation team also restored the palace's traditional barjeel, a wind-tower system used for natural cooling. Look up as you walk through and you will spot it.

## What to look for in the building

Even if you know nothing about architecture, a few details make the walk round more rewarding:

- **The stone.** The original walls are coral and sea stone, gathered from the coast. Where the render has gone, you can see the rough texture of the blocks.
- **The towers.** The round watchtower is the oldest part of the site; the later palace wraps around it. Try to work out where one ends and the other begins.
- **The windows and doors.** Openings are small and set deep in thick walls, which kept rooms cooler and safer.
- **The barjeel.** The restored wind tower caught breezes and pushed them down into the rooms below, a reminder of how people stayed cool before electricity.
- **The arch.** The decorative arch outside the gates dates from the early 1950s, when the oil era was beginning.

## What to see on your visit

### The inner fort and outer palace

The heart of the visit is the fort itself. The site's FAQ says you can [explore the inner fort and outer palace](https://www.qasralhosn.ae/en/faqs) and learn about the history of Abu Dhabi and the ruling Al Nahyan family. Take your time in the courtyards. The thick walls and small windows say a great deal about how people lived before air conditioning.

### House of Artisans

A short walk away, the House of Artisans is dedicated to the [traditional crafts of the UAE](https://qasralhosn.ae/en/house-of-artisans). Its page introduces Al-Sadu weaving by Bedouin women, using the wool of sheep, camels and goats; khoos, the weaving of date palm fronds into mats, fans and baskets; talli, decorative embroidery with gold and silver thread; and the sea crafts of boat building and fishing nets. It also offers training courses and workshops.

The details are worth knowing before you go in. The page explains that Al-Sadu was used to make the bait al-shaar, the traditional tent, and to decorate camel saddles and straps, and that it was inscribed in 2011 on UNESCO's list of intangible heritage in need of urgent safeguarding. Khoos, practised by both men and women, produced everyday objects such as round food mats, fans, food covers and baskets for carrying dates. Talli decorates the collars, sleeves and hems of the kandoora and the women's thawb, and is slow, painstaking work. Look for the boat-building displays too: the page notes that boats built for pearling and fishing carried trade across the Gulf and the Indian Ocean. If you are curious about everyday Emirati life, pair it with our guide to [Emirati food: what to eat and where](/stories/emirati-food-what-to-eat-where/), since several of the same traditions turn up on the table.

### Performances

The site runs a programme of [traditional performing arts](https://www.qasralhosn.ae/en/plan-your-visit), including Al Ayyala. UNESCO, which lists it as intangible cultural heritage, describes Al-Ayyala as a performance of [chanted poetry, drum music and dance that simulates a battle scene](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/al-ayyala-a-traditional-performing-art-of-the-sultanate-of-oman-and-the-united-arab-emirates-01012), with two rows of men carrying thin bamboo sticks. Check the calendar on the official site before you go if you want to catch one.

## Planning your visit to Qasr Al Hosn

### Hours and tickets

The official [plan your visit page](https://www.qasralhosn.ae/en/plan-your-visit) gives the hours as:

- **Saturday to Thursday:** 9:00 AM to 8:00 PM.
- **Friday:** 2:00 PM to 8:00 PM.

The same page says admission to the House of Artisans, the Cultural Foundation and the landscaped grounds is complimentary, while Qasr Al Hosn itself, special workshops and events are ticketed. Tickets can be booked online or bought on site, and are non-refundable. If you cannot visit in person, there is also a virtual guided tour.

### Guides and audio

The [FAQ](https://www.qasralhosn.ae/en/faqs) says you can book a private group tour through the website or join a guided tour, and that the Abu Dhabi Culture app offers audio and augmented reality experiences at the fort. Complimentary wifi is available across the site, so you can download it there if needed.

### Getting there

The fort sits in the centre of the city. The plan your visit page places it [between Zayed the First Street, Khaled Bin Waleed Street, Hamdan Bin Mohammed Street and Sheikh Rashid Bin Saeed Street](https://www.qasralhosn.ae/en/plan-your-visit), close to the World Trade Centre. The FAQ says the site has [underground parking](https://www.qasralhosn.ae/en/faqs) and that taxis drop off on Hamdan bin Mohammed Street and the surrounding streets. One thing to know if you are coming straight from the airport: there are no lockers, and you must carry your belongings with you at all times.

Visitors with disabilities are asked to speak to the visitor experience staff at the ticketing points. There is a prayer room near the restaurants on site.

### Groups and schools

The [plan your visit page](https://www.qasralhosn.ae/en/plan-your-visit) says group tours are welcome, with a booking form on the site, and that all-access admission for school groups is complimentary. It also notes that Al Hosn tickets, which cover Qasr Al Hosn, the Cultural Foundation, the House of Artisans and Bait Al Gahwa, are non-refundable, so settle your date before you buy.

## How a visit unfolds

Most visitors arrive by taxi or park underground, then walk across the open landscaped grounds towards the fort. It is worth pausing here first: this is where the contrast between the low pale walls and the towers around them is clearest, and the free grounds are pleasant in the cooler months. From the ticketing point you head into the outer palace, then through to the inner fort and the original watchtower. Displays along the way follow the history in roughly the same order as this guide, from the first water sources to the oil era and the founding of the UAE.

After the fort, cross to the House of Artisans for the crafts, and finish with a coffee or a meal on site. If you are interested in modern Abu Dhabi as well as the old, the fort's own discover page points to the way the city's urban fabric changed after the mid-twentieth century, when [roads reached out across the desert and new buildings rose around the fort](https://www.qasralhosn.ae/en/discover). Standing in the grounds, you can see that change for yourself.

### Timing and comfort

Much of the visit involves walking across open ground between buildings, and there is little shade in the middle of the day outside the winter months. A morning visit, or a late afternoon visit that runs into the evening when the walls are lit, is more comfortable. Bring water, a hat and comfortable shoes, and remember that you will be carrying any bags with you for the whole visit.

### With children

The fort works well for families if you turn it into a story: a watchtower, a well, a palace and a family that ruled from inside the walls. The House of Artisans gives children something hands-on to look at, and the open grounds give them space between buildings. Keep an eye on younger ones near steps and uneven floors in the older parts of the fort.

## Fitting it into your Abu Dhabi trip

Qasr Al Hosn is compact enough for a half day, which makes it easy to pair with other sights. Since Sheikh Zayed was born here, it makes a thoughtful pairing with the [Sheikh Zayed Grand Mosque](/stories/sheikh-zayed-grand-mosque-visitor-guide/), which bears his name. ## Quick answers

**What is Qasr Al Hosn?**
It is the oldest and most significant building in Abu Dhabi, a fort that grew from a watchtower built around the 1790s and served as the ruling family's home and seat of government.

**When is Qasr Al Hosn open?**
Saturday to Thursday from 9:00 AM to 8:00 PM, and Friday from 2:00 PM to 8:00 PM, according to the official site.

**Is Qasr Al Hosn free?**
The House of Artisans, the Cultural Foundation and the grounds are free; the fort itself needs a ticket.

**Can I leave luggage at Qasr Al Hosn?**
No. There are no lockers, so you must keep your belongings with you.
