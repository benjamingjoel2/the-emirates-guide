You cross from Dubai into Sharjah and nothing obvious changes. The traffic is the same, the towers keep going, and the only sign that you have entered a different emirate is a road sign. Yet the rules on what you can carry, drink and wear have shifted, and many visitors only find out after the fact.

That is the core of visiting Sharjah as a tourist: it is part of the same country, a short drive from Dubai, but it has its own approach to alcohol and public behaviour. The short answer to the question most people search for is simple. **Alcohol is not allowed in Sharjah at all**, not in hotels, not in restaurants and not in your bag, and the emirate expects modest dress and quiet public conduct. Follow a few easy habits and Sharjah is a relaxed, welcoming place to spend a day or more.

## Why Sharjah feels different

Walk into the Heart of Sharjah in the late afternoon and the mood is part of the answer. Families stroll between restored coral-stone houses and the call to prayer carries over the rooftops.

Sharjah has built its identity around culture and tradition. The [UAE government portal](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/the-seven-emirates/sharjah) notes that UNESCO named it the Cultural Capital of the Arab World in 1998, that it was named Capital of Islamic Culture in 2014, and that it is home to nearly a quarter of all the UAE's museums. The same page lists the Heart of Sharjah as one of its historical areas alongside modern waterfronts such as Al Qasba and Al Majaz, and describes Sharjah as the only emirate with coastline on both the Arabian Gulf and the Gulf of Oman.

Seen in that light, the rules are less about restriction and more about the kind of place the emirate wants to be: family-centred, conservative and proud of its heritage. Sharjah has long had a reputation as the most conservative of the seven emirates, and visitors who arrive expecting that tend to enjoy it most.

## The Sharjah alcohol law in plain terms

Start with the rule that catches people out. The UK Foreign Office states that [drinking or possessing alcohol is illegal in the Emirate of Sharjah](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security). That is a wider ban than you will find in Dubai or Abu Dhabi, where licensed venues serve drinks.

The same advice sets out how Sharjah fits into the national picture. The legal age for buying and drinking alcohol is [21 in Abu Dhabi, Dubai and the Northern Emirates except Sharjah](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), where drinking and having alcohol in your possession is illegal. It also notes that liquor licences are no longer required for residents in the other emirates, again excluding Sharjah, and that non-residents can drink in licensed venues such as hotels, restaurants and clubs in the emirates where alcohol is available.

### What that means on a real trip

A few situations come up again and again:

- **Staying in a Sharjah hotel.** Do not expect a bar or a drinks list. Do not bring a bottle from duty free or a Dubai shop to drink in your room, because possession itself is the offence.
- **Driving through with shopping in the boot.** The rule is about possession in the emirate. If you are carrying alcohol bought elsewhere, plan a route that does not take you through Sharjah, or do not carry it.
- **Coming back from a night out in Dubai.** Sharjah hotels are close to Dubai, and plenty of visitors stay there for that reason. Remember that it is also [illegal to drink or be under the influence of alcohol in a public place anywhere in the UAE](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security). The Foreign Office notes that British nationals have been arrested under this law, often after coming to police attention for something related such as disorderly behaviour.
- **Driving after any drink.** The same page is blunt: it is [illegal to drink and drive, no matter how small the amount](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), and your insurance is likely to be invalidated after an accident. Book a taxi or a transfer instead.

### Planning a trip that crosses emirates

The simplest approach is to treat Sharjah as dry from the moment you arrive and to keep any celebrating for a different emirate on a different night. If a group dinner in Dubai is part of the plan, decide who is getting everyone home before the evening starts, and book the ride rather than hoping to find one.

## Dressing for Sharjah's streets, souks and museums

There is no special tourist uniform, but there is a clear expectation of modesty. The Foreign Office's national advice is a good baseline for the whole country, and Sharjah is the place to follow it most carefully. It says to [dress modestly in public areas like shopping malls](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), that women should cover shoulders and knees, that underwear should not be visible, and that transparent clothing or clothes with images or phrases that might offend should be avoided. Swimwear, it says, should only be worn on beaches or at swimming pools. The same section notes that cross-dressing is illegal and that some venues, events or buildings have their own dress codes.

In practice, for a day in Sharjah:

- Choose loose trousers, midi or maxi skirts and tops with sleeves.
- Men should avoid very short shorts and sleeveless vests in town.
- Cover up with a shirt or kaftan when you leave the beach or pool.
- Bring a light scarf. It helps with sun, air conditioning and mosque visits.

### Visiting a mosque

If you want to see inside a mosque, the government portal's guidance for non-Muslims is clear: [clothes should fully cover the shoulders, arms and knees, and women would need to cover their heads](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/culture/arab-and-islamic-heritage). In Sharjah, Al Noor Mosque admits non-Muslim visitors, and the neighbouring emirate's tourism board describes it as the closest mosque to Ajman that does so, with [a free guided tour that teaches visitors about Emirati culture and religion](https://visit-ajman.ae/en/facts/culture-faq/). Check the current tour schedule before you go.

Visit Sharjah describes [Al Noor Mosque](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/culture/al-noor-mosque/) as an Ottoman-style landmark on the corniche, with cascading domes, two minarets and Quranic calligraphy inside. It says the mosque offers Emirati traditional dress for visitors in the entrance area, that guides talk about Islamic culture, Emirati history and the building's architecture, and that there is time for questions at the end. Photography is allowed inside, according to the same page, and the mosque is lit up after sunset.

A few habits make a mosque visit easy. Arrive in clothes that already meet the dress code rather than relying on what is provided at the door. Take off your shoes where others do, keep your voice low, and avoid walking in front of anyone who is praying. If a prayer is about to begin, wait or come back later.

## Behaving in public: affection, language and photos

Sharjah's public spaces are shared by families, and the rules on behaviour reflect national law that applies everywhere in the UAE.

### Affection

The Foreign Office says that showing affection in public is frowned upon and that [you can be arrested for kissing in public](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security). The Ajman tourism board puts it in everyday terms: a married couple holding hands is fine, but [kissing and other romantic behaviour is frowned upon](https://visit-ajman.ae/en/facts/culture-faq/) and could get you into trouble.

### Swearing and gestures

It is [illegal to swear and make rude gestures, including online](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), and the Foreign Office warns that you could be jailed or deported. That includes road rage. Keep calm in traffic and at the hotel desk.

### Photography

The Foreign Office advises that it is illegal to photograph some government buildings and military sites, and that you should [not photograph people without their permission](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security). It adds that men have been arrested for photographing women on beaches, and that hobbies such as birdwatching and plane spotting can be misunderstood near airports and official sites. In the souks and old quarter, ask before you point a camera at anyone, and take a polite no gracefully.

## Other national laws that apply in Sharjah too

The alcohol ban gets the attention, but several UAE-wide rules matter just as much on a Sharjah trip. The Foreign Office's [safety and security advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) covers them, and they are worth reading before you fly:

- **Drugs.** There is zero tolerance, and the advice says the presence of drugs in the bloodstream counts as possession. It warns that some skincare products and e-cigarette refills can contain ingredients such as CBD oil that are illegal in the UAE.
- **Crossing the road.** Only cross at designated pedestrian crossings. Crossing elsewhere is illegal, and the advice notes that vehicles often do not stop at zebra crossings.
- **Hotel check-in.** It is normal practice for hotels to take a photocopy of your passport, and under-18s cannot stay in a hotel unless accompanied by an adult.
- **Relationships.** Same-sex sexual activity is illegal in the UAE. The Foreign Office has a separate page of advice for LGBT+ travellers.
- **Equipment.** You may need a licence for items such as satellite phones, recording devices or powerful cameras and binoculars, so check before you pack specialist kit.

## Shopping in Sharjah's souks

The souks are where many visitors spend most of their Sharjah day, and they are relaxed places to browse if you follow local manners.

Visit Sharjah describes the [Central Souk](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/shopping/central-souk-blue-souk/), known for its blue-tiled facade, as having over 600 shops across two levels, with carpets, textiles and crafts upstairs and jewellery and gold along the lagoon side. It notes that the building appears on the five-dirham note. Nearby, [Souq Al Arsah](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/shopping/souq-al-arsah/) was once a market for traders from Persia, India and Bedouin tribes, according to the tourism board, and is now a covered market of coral-brick walls and wooden doors selling pearl chests, copper coffee pots, perfume and incense.

Bargaining is normal in traditional shops, but keep it friendly. Ask the price, offer a little less, smile and settle somewhere in the middle. If you are not interested, a simple thank you is enough. Shopkeepers will often chat, and asking about where a piece was made is a pleasant way to start. If you would like to photograph a stall, ask first, and never photograph women shoppers. ## Beaches and the waterfront

Sharjah has public beaches on its Gulf coast, and the rules there are simple. Visit Sharjah describes [Al Khan Beach](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/beaches/al-khan-beach/) as having a dedicated swimming area, water sports, showers, changing rooms and toilets, with the Sharjah Maritime Museum and Sharjah Aquarium a short walk away. Use the changing rooms, and put a layer on before you walk back to the car or into a café.

The Foreign Office warns that rip currents can occur at any beach and advises [swimming only from approved beaches with lifeguards and flags](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security). Red flags mean it is dangerous to swim. On the corniche and lagoon promenades, think of yourself as a guest in a family park: no music from speakers, no swimwear away from the sand, and patience with children and slow walkers.

## Sharjah during Ramadan

Ramadan changes the rhythm of Sharjah more than almost anywhere else in the country. Days are quieter, evenings come alive after iftar, and the old quarter can be lovely after dark.

The [UAE government portal](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/public-holidays-and-religious-affairs/ramadan) confirms that non-Muslims are not required to fast, and says that malls offer dining areas away from those who are fasting. It explains that people greet each other with Ramadan Kareem or Ramadan Mubarak, that malls stay open until late at night, and that charities set up tents offering free iftar meals where people of every background sit together to break the fast. It also notes that fundraising must go through licensed charities, so give through official donation points rather than informal collections.

The Foreign Office adds that it may cause offence to [eat, drink, smoke or chew gum in public in the daytime](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), including in your car, or to play loud music, and that dress codes matter even more during the month. It also warns that driving can be erratic around dusk as people hurry home. Read our full guide to [visiting the UAE during Ramadan](/stories/visiting-uae-during-ramadan/) before you travel then.

## What tends to catch visitors out

Most problems in Sharjah are small and avoidable. These are the ones we see come up most often:

- **Assuming hotel rules match Dubai.** A beach hotel in Sharjah will not serve alcohol, whatever its style or star rating.
- **Walking back from the beach in swimwear.** It feels harmless on a hot day, but swimwear belongs on the sand or at the pool.
- **Photographing people without asking.** Especially women and families, and especially on beaches.
- **Jaywalking across a busy road.** Walk to a crossing even if it takes longer.
- **Packing products you have not checked.** Vapes, CBD skincare and some medicines need checking before you fly.

## Visiting Sharjah: simple habits that keep the day easy

Most of it comes down to a handful of habits:

1. **Leave the alcohol behind.** Nothing in your bag, nothing in your hotel room, nothing in the car.
2. **Dress one notch more modestly than you would in Dubai.** Sleeves and longer hems, a scarf in your bag.
3. **Keep affection private.** Hand-holding is the limit in public.
4. **Ask before photographing people.** And skip official buildings.
5. **Book a ride home.** Never drive after drinking anywhere in the country.

Families tend to find Sharjah easy. The Foreign Office notes that there are [special taxis for women and children](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) that can be hailed or booked in advance, and the emirate's parks, beaches and waterfronts are built around family outings.

## Quick answers

**Can tourists drink alcohol in Sharjah?** No. The UK Foreign Office says drinking or possessing alcohol is illegal in the Emirate of Sharjah, so there are no licensed bars and you should not bring alcohol into the emirate.

**Can I stay in Sharjah and drink in Dubai?** Licensed venues in Dubai serve alcohol, but being drunk in a public place is illegal anywhere in the UAE and drink-driving is illegal at any level. Use a taxi and do not carry alcohol back with you.

**What should women wear in Sharjah?** The Foreign Office advises covering shoulders and knees in public areas and avoiding transparent clothing. For mosque visits, the UAE government portal says arms, shoulders and knees should be covered and women should cover their heads.

**Can couples hold hands in Sharjah?** A married couple holding hands is generally fine, according to the Ajman tourism board, but kissing in public can lead to arrest.

**Can non-Muslims visit a mosque in Sharjah?** Yes. Visit Sharjah says Al Noor Mosque on the corniche runs guided tours for visitors and offers traditional dress at the entrance.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Will any part of our route pass through Sharjah while we are carrying shopping or luggage from Dubai, and how can we plan around that?
- Which mosques and museums in Sharjah are open to visitors on our dates, and what dress do they ask for?
- Can you arrange a transfer back to a Sharjah hotel after an evening in Dubai so nobody needs to drive?
- Are there any sites on our itinerary where photography is restricted or where we should ask permission first?
- How will Ramadan, if it falls during our trip, change opening times and the best hours to visit the old quarter?
