You step out of the heat and onto the marble, and the noise of the road simply stops. Ahead, a courtyard so white it makes you squint stretches towards the prayer hall, its edges lined with columns inlaid with flowers.

Visiting the Sheikh Zayed Grand Mosque is, for many travellers, the moment Abu Dhabi stops being a stopover and starts being a place. It is also a working mosque, and that shapes everything about your visit. The short answer to what most people want to know: yes, non-Muslim visitors are welcome; you need to [pre-book an online access pass](https://www.szgmc.gov.ae/en/worshippers/en-v-visiting-hours) before you go; and there is a strict, clearly published [dress code](https://www.szgmc.gov.ae/en/worshippers/en-v-mosque-manners-details) that applies to everyone. Get those three things right and the rest of the day takes care of itself.

## A mosque first, a landmark second

It helps to know what you are walking into. The mosque is named after Sheikh Zayed bin Sultan Al Nahyan, the founder of the UAE, who the mosque centre notes was [born at Qasr Al Hosn in 1918](https://www.szgmc.gov.ae/en/about/the-mosque-and-the-founder) and passed away in 2004. According to the same page, construction began on 5 November 1996, the inner prayer halls first opened for Eid Al Adha worship in 2007, and the building can hold approximately 50,000 people. More than 3,000 workers and 38 contracting companies took part in building it, drawing on artisans and natural materials such as marble, stone, gold, semi-precious stones, crystals and ceramics from countries including Italy, Germany, Morocco, India, Turkey, China, the United Kingdom, New Zealand, Greece and the UAE itself.

The centre describes the mosque as a place meant to reflect Islamic moderation and to encourage tolerance and exchange between cultures. That is the spirit in which visitors are received. You are a guest in a place of prayer, and the rules below exist so that worshippers and visitors can share the space comfortably. The same page notes that certified reciters take turns to recite the Quran around the clock at the mausoleum of the founder, which sits within the mosque grounds; you may hear it as you pass, and it is a good moment to lower your voice.

If you want the story of where Sheikh Zayed was born, our guide to [Qasr Al Hosn](/stories/qasr-al-hosn-abu-dhabi/) covers the fort at the heart of the old city, and it makes a natural companion visit.

## What you are looking at

Much of the pleasure of a visit lies in knowing what to look for. The mosque centre publishes short notes on each architectural element, and a few are worth reading before you go.

### The courtyard

The open courtyard, or sahan, is used mainly for Eid prayers and for night prayers during Ramadan. The centre says it covers [17,400 square metres and can hold up to 31,000 worshippers](https://www.szgmc.gov.ae/en/islamic-architecture/The%20Courtyard), and that more than 30 types of marble were used in it, including Italian, Indian, Greek and Chinese stone. The flowing floral mosaics were designed by a British artist who chose flowers found in the Middle East, such as tulips, lilies and irises, and the centre suggests the courtyard may be the largest marble mosaic artwork in the world. Look down as you cross it: the colours curl in from the edges towards the plain white centre.

Inside the entrance foyers, the theme continues. The centre's note on the [Al Noor foyer](https://www.szgmc.gov.ae/en/islamic-architecture/the-al-noor-foyer) explains that the walls carry raised floral patterns, so the flowers stand out in relief, and that coloured marble from more than 37 countries was used on the floors and walls.

## How to book your visit

### The access pass

Worshippers can walk straight in without a ticket. Visitors cannot. The official visitors page asks you to [pre-book the visit and obtain an online access pass](https://szgmc.gov.ae/en/visiting-the-mosque/visitors) through the "Book Your Visit" link on the mosque centre's website. Do this before you leave your hotel, and keep the pass on your phone.

On the question of cost, information online varies, so rely on the source. The mosque centre's own [FAQ](https://www.szgmc.gov.ae/en/worshippers/en-v-faqs) sets out entry fees by visitor category, and states that worshippers enter free. Check the booking page for the current position when you reserve.

### Visiting hours

The mosque centre publishes its [visiting hours](https://www.szgmc.gov.ae/en/worshippers/en-v-visiting-hours). For visitors, outside Ramadan:

- **Saturday to Thursday:** 9:00 AM to 8:30 PM, with last entry at 8:00 PM.
- **Friday:** 9:00 AM to 11:30 AM (last entry 11:00 AM), then 2:30 PM to 8:30 PM (last entry 8:00 PM).

The Friday gap is for Friday prayers, so do not plan a late Friday morning visit. During Ramadan the hours change completely, with separate daytime and late-night windows for the first 20 days and shorter hours in the last ten. If your trip overlaps the holy month, read our guide to [visiting the UAE during Ramadan](/stories/visiting-uae-during-ramadan/) and check the official page again in the week you travel.

## The Sheikh Zayed Grand Mosque dress code, explained

This is where visits most often go wrong, so it is worth being precise. The mosque centre's [mosque manners page](https://www.szgmc.gov.ae/en/worshippers/en-v-mosque-manners-details) sets out the rules.

### For women

- Clothing should be loose-fitting down to the ankles.
- Sleeves should reach all the way to the wrists.
- Hair must be fully covered.
- Avoid transparent, tight-fitting or revealing clothing.

The mosque centre's FAQ sums it up as [long, loose, ankle-length clothing, with a headscarf for women](https://www.szgmc.gov.ae/en/worshippers/en-v-faqs).

### For men

Men have more latitude, but not unlimited. The manners page says covering the entire legs is preferred, and that transparent, tight-fitting or sleeveless shirts should be avoided. In practice, long trousers and a T-shirt or shirt with sleeves are the safe choice.

### For everyone

The same page asks all visitors to avoid excessive jewellery, heavy make-up and bright colours, and says clothing with slogans or inappropriate drawings is not permitted. A plain, loose outfit in a muted colour is ideal. Pack a light scarf even if you think you will not need it; it is the simplest insurance against being turned away. For a wider view of what to pack for the UAE, see our guide to [what to wear and local etiquette](/stories/what-to-wear-uae-etiquette/).

### What catches people out

The rules are simple, but a few things regularly trip visitors up. Leggings or skinny jeans under a long top count as tight clothing. A scarf that keeps slipping back will need constant adjusting, so a longer one that wraps fully is easier. Rolled-up sleeves on a hot day are another common problem. And remember that the dress code applies for your whole visit, including the walk back to the car park and any photos you take on the way out.

## Arriving and security

The mosque centre's [FAQ](https://www.szgmc.gov.ae/en/worshippers/en-v-faqs) says all visitors pass through an inspection before entering, with separate tracks for women and men, so groups should agree a meeting point beyond the checks. Security staff may also direct you along particular routes, especially when it rains, in bad weather or while maintenance work is under way, and the marble can be slippery, so watch your step. The manners page adds that you are responsible for your own belongings, and anything you find should be handed in at the information desk.

## Inside the prayer hall

Once you reach the main prayer hall, slow down. Most of the details that make the building special are easy to walk past.

### Domes and minarets

The centre counts [82 domes of various sizes](https://www.szgmc.gov.ae/en/islamic-architecture/The%20Domes), drawing mainly on Moroccan and Mughal styles. The largest, over the centre of the main prayer hall, is about 32.6 metres in diameter and 84 metres high. Inside, the domes are decorated with verses from the Quran in several calligraphy styles, including Naskh, Thuluth and Kufi. Outside, the four [minarets rise to about 107 metres](https://www.szgmc.gov.ae/en/islamic-architecture/the-minarets) from the corners of the courtyard, built up in square, octagonal and circular sections.

### Chandeliers and carpet

Look up, then look down. The mosque has [seven chandeliers](https://www.szgmc.gov.ae/en/islamic-architecture/the-chandeliers), with the three largest hanging from the domes of the main prayer hall. The centre explains that their design is inspired by the date palm: the column is the trunk, the curved parts are the fronds and the coloured glass globes are the dates. Below them, the hall is covered by what the centre describes as the [world's largest hand-knotted carpet](https://www.szgmc.gov.ae/en/islamic-architecture/the-carpet), made by about 1,200 artisans in nine pieces and stitched together on site. Its medallions sit beneath the chandeliers, as if reflecting them, and slightly raised lines in the pile mark where worshippers stand in rows to pray. You will be asked not to walk on some areas, so follow the barriers and staff.

## Inside: how to behave and what to leave behind

Once inside, the atmosphere is calm and the expectations are clear. The [mosque manners](https://www.szgmc.gov.ae/en/worshippers/en-v-mosque-manners-details) guidance asks you to:

- Keep your voice down and set your phone to silent.
- Avoid public displays of affection.
- Not smoke, sleep or lie down anywhere on the grounds.
- Not bring food, water or other drinks inside.
- Take care on the marble floors, which can be slippery.

The ban on water surprises people. The [FAQ](https://www.szgmc.gov.ae/en/worshippers/en-v-faqs) explains that it protects the delicate marble and carpets. Drink before you go in, and leave bottles in the car.

### Items you cannot bring

The mosque centre lists a range of prohibited items, including e-cigarettes, lighters, sharp objects, whistles, laser pointers, loudspeakers, dolls and stuffed animals, masks, flags and slogans. That last group catches families out, so leave the soft toys in the hotel.

### Photography

Personal photos are welcome, but only at [designated photo stops](https://www.szgmc.gov.ae/en/worshippers/en-v-mosque-manners-details), and you should not lie down or behave inappropriately to get a shot. Commercial or media photography and professional equipment need a permit from the centre, and devices used in breach of the rules may be confiscated. Be considerate of worshippers, too: nobody at prayer should end up in the background of your picture.

## Free cultural tours

You can explore on your own, but a guide adds a great deal. The mosque centre runs [free cultural tours](https://www.szgmc.gov.ae/en/worshippers/en-cultural-tours) daily, with start times of 11:00, 13:00 and 17:00, and no 13:00 tour on Fridays. During Ramadan, tours run Saturday to Thursday at 11:00 and 13:00 only. The tour page says a tour lasts between 30 and 45 minutes. According to the [FAQ](https://www.szgmc.gov.ae/en/worshippers/en-v-faqs), tours are offered in English and Arabic, start on the east side of the mosque, and you should arrive about 15 minutes early. You are equally welcome to walk round without a guide.

Visit Abu Dhabi notes that guides point out details many visitors miss, such as the [gold-leaf calligraphy around the mosque's 82 domes](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/things-to-do/culture/landmarks-and-architecture/sheikh-zayed-grand-mosque).

## When to go for the best light

Visit Abu Dhabi suggests that [dusk is particularly special](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/things-to-do/culture/landmarks-and-architecture/sheikh-zayed-grand-mosque), when the fading sun warms the white facade, and the building is lit up after sunset. Morning visits tend to feel quieter and cooler underfoot. If you can, arrive in late afternoon, take the 17:00 tour, and stay on as the lights come up.

The evening lighting has its own logic. The centre explains that its [lunar lighting system](https://www.szgmc.gov.ae/en/islamic-architecture/the-lunar-illuminations) projects soft bluish clouds onto the white walls and domes, starting darker when the new month begins and growing brighter as the moon approaches full, so no two nights look quite the same. The ten [reflective pools](https://www.szgmc.gov.ae/en/islamic-architecture/reflective-pools) around the mosque, tiled in different blues, double the effect after dark.

Remember that Friday is the main day of congregational prayer. Visit Abu Dhabi notes that the [main prayer hall is closed to visitors at Friday prayer time](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/things-to-do/culture/landmarks-and-architecture/sheikh-zayed-grand-mosque), which matches the gap in the official Friday hours.

## How a visit unfolds

A typical unhurried visit runs something like this. You arrive at the southern car parks, where the [FAQ](https://www.szgmc.gov.ae/en/worshippers/en-v-faqs) says signs near the entrances show where to park, and buggies can carry you towards the tour starting point. After the security checks you come up into the arcades and the courtyard, and then into the foyers and main prayer hall. Allow time to stop at the photo points, and plan to finish in the courtyard or beside the pools as the light changes.

Do not try to rush it. The distances on foot are longer than they look, the marble reflects a lot of heat in the warmer months, and the details reward a slow pace. If you are short of time, prioritise the courtyard and the main prayer hall over everything else.

### Children and families

Children are welcome but, the [FAQ](https://www.szgmc.gov.ae/en/worshippers/en-v-faqs) says, must stay under their parents' supervision at all times, and the manners page makes visitors fully responsible for the children with them. Talk to younger children beforehand about quiet voices and not running on the marble, and remember that soft toys are among the items you cannot bring in. A feed and a drink in the car before you go in make the no-water rule much easier.

## Getting there and practical details

The mosque centre says the mosque sits [between the three main bridges](https://szgmc.gov.ae/en/getting-to-the-mosque3) that link Abu Dhabi island to the mainland, the Maqta, Mussafah and Sheikh Zayed bridges, which makes it an easy first or last stop if you are driving in from Dubai. Its [FAQ](https://www.szgmc.gov.ae/en/worshippers/en-v-faqs) says it does not provide transport, so you will arrive by taxi, bus or your own car. There are restrooms for men and women, wheelchairs can be requested from security, and buggies carry visitors between the car parks and the tour starting point. The manners page notes that vehicles may not be left overnight in the mosque's car parks.

Many visitors combine the mosque with a day in the capital. Visit Abu Dhabi suggests pairing an evening visit with the [Wahat Al Karama memorial and dinner in the Al Qana district](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/things-to-do/culture/landmarks-and-architecture/sheikh-zayed-grand-mosque). ## Quick answers

**Do I need to book to visit the Sheikh Zayed Grand Mosque?**
Yes. Visitors must pre-book and obtain an online access pass through the mosque centre's website. Worshippers can enter without a ticket.

**What is the dress code for women?**
Loose clothing down to the ankles, sleeves to the wrists, and hair fully covered. Avoid transparent, tight or revealing clothes.

**Can men wear shorts?**
The mosque centre prefers men to cover their entire legs and asks them to avoid sleeveless shirts, so long trousers are the safe choice.

**Can I take photos inside?**
Personal photos are allowed at designated photo stops. Professional equipment and commercial filming need a permit.

**Is the mosque open on Fridays?**
Yes, but visiting is split into a morning window and an afternoon window, with a gap for Friday prayers.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Will you book our access passes, or should each of us book individually through the mosque centre's website?
- Can you time our visit to catch one of the free cultural tours, or the light at dusk?
- How will you handle Friday prayer times or Ramadan hours if they fall on our day?
- Can you check our outfits against the dress code before we set off, so nobody is turned away?
- Where will you drop us, and how far is it from the car park to the tour starting point for anyone with limited mobility?
- How long will we have at the mosque before moving on to the next stop?
