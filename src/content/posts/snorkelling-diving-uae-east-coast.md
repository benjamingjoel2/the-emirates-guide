Put your face in the water off a rocky islet on the east coast and the noise of the boat engine disappears. What replaces it is a soft crackle, the sound of fish nibbling at the rock, and a sudden flicker of colour as a shoal turns all at once below you.

For many visitors that moment comes as a surprise. The UAE is known for desert and skyline, not reefs. Yet snorkelling and diving on the UAE's east coast is one of the best ways to spend a day outside the cities, and it suits complete beginners as well as certified divers. The short answer to "is it worth it?" is yes, provided you go with a reputable operator, choose a calm day and treat the sea with respect.

## Why the east coast is the place to get in the water

The east coast faces a different sea from Dubai and Abu Dhabi. The [UAE government portal](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/the-seven-emirates/fujairah) describes Fujairah as the only emirate located completely on the east coast, along the Gulf of Oman, and notes that its position gives the UAE its only access to the Indian Ocean. Here the Hajar Mountains run almost into the water, and the rocky headlands and small islands that result are what make the snorkelling interesting.

Both Fujairah and Sharjah promote the coast for exactly this. The [Fujairah Tourism and Antiquities Authority](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-SportsAndCruises.html) lists swimming, diving, snorkelling, fishing, kayaking and parasailing, and says most hotels can arrange the water sport of your choice. [Visit Sharjah](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/regions/east-coast/) describes its east coast towns of Khorfakkan and Kalba as places to go scuba diving, kayaking and snorkelling, and suggests guided boat tours to spot dolphins.

Fujairah's tourism authority goes further on its [facts page](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-FactsAndStatistics.html), describing diving as one of the best sports in the emirate and pointing to diverse coral reefs. That matches what most visitors find: rocky reefs close to shore, plenty of fish and, on a good day, water clear enough to make a first snorkel memorable.

## Where to snorkel and dive on the UAE's east coast

The coast is short enough that most trips start from one of three areas.

### Around Al Aqah and the northern Fujairah coast

The stretch of coast between Dibba and Khorfakkan is where many visitors have their first snorkel. The best-known spot is a small rocky island just offshore, often called Snoopy Island, which you will see on hotel signs and boat-trip boards up and down the coast. Its shallow, rocky edges are good for beginners, but conditions vary with wind and tide, so never swim out to it on your own: go with a boat or a guided group.

### Dibba: boats to the bays

At the northern end of the coast, Dibba is a jumping-off point for boat trips. [Visit Sharjah's Dibba Al Hisn page](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/regions/east-coast/dibba-al-hisn/) describes a marina, snorkelling among its summer activities, and boat captains who take visitors to quieter diving and snorkelling spots, sometimes with dolphins seen on the way. It also notes that Dibba Al Baya, to the north, is part of Oman. Many trips from this area head towards the dramatic inlets of Musandam, which means crossing into Oman: check the entry rules and what documents the operator needs well before the day. Our guide to [a dhow cruise in Musandam](/stories/musandam-dhow-cruise-guide/) explains how those trips work.

### Khorfakkan: a beach town with a bay

Khorfakkan, an enclave of Sharjah surrounded by Fujairah, sits on a sheltered bay. [Visit Sharjah](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/regions/east-coast/khorfakkan/) lists sailing, kayaking, scuba diving and dhow cruises there, alongside a long beach and a corniche for the evening. It makes a good base if you want to mix a morning in the water with sightseeing on land. Our [Khorfakkan guide](/stories/khorfakkan-guide/) covers the town itself.

Visit Sharjah's page on [diving in Khorfakkan](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/sports/diving-in-khorfakkan/) describes seven dive sites strung along the bay, ranging from wreck dives to reef dives, among granite formations. It names them as Sira Khorfakkan, also known as Shark Island, Martini Rock, the Inchcape wreck, Coral Gardens, Anemone Gardens, Hole in the Wall and Almanure. The same page says:

- **Shark Island** is a good place to see the common blacktip reef shark, as well as moray eels, Arabian angelfish and turtles.
- **Martini Rock** sits within a reef and measures around 160 m long and 80 m across, with average visibility of 5 to 15 m and up to 20 m on a clear day.
- **The Inchcape** is a decommissioned boat that was sunk on purpose, where cardinal fish, emperor fish and barracuda are common and large rays have been seen.

### Sira Khorfakkan for snorkellers

<photo-set layout="inset-left" keys="snorkelling-diving-uae-east-coast-9" alts="Clear green water in Khorfakkan bay below rocky headlands, with a heart shape picked out in white stones on the far hill" caption="Khorfakkan's bay, where the dive sites are strung along the shore."></photo-set>

Snorkellers do not need to go far. [Visit Sharjah](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/nature-and-wildlife/sira-khorfakkan/) describes Sira Khorfakkan as a small rocky island just out in the bay, tucked behind the port at the southern end of the beach, a short boat ride from town. It mentions sea anemones and turtles for snorkellers, moray eels, rays and Arabian angelfish for divers, and kayaking for anyone who would rather look from the surface. For families, it recommends going with a nearby dive centre for guides, and notes that you can rent fins, masks and snorkels. The [Khorfakkan Beach page](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/beaches/khorfakkan-beach/) also mentions snorkelling near Shark Island.

### Kalba: for kayaking and wildlife

Further south, [Kalba](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/regions/east-coast/kalba/) is better known for its mangroves than its reefs, but Visit Sharjah lists scuba diving there too, along with kayaking and wildlife spotting. It is a good choice if someone in your group would rather paddle than snorkel. Visit Sharjah's page on the [Kalba Beach Corniche](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/beaches/kalba-beach-corniche/) adds that the sands and mangroves at the southern end form part of the Al Hefaiyah Conservation Area, an important nesting site for hawksbill turtles. If you see a turtle in the water anywhere on this coast, give it room and let it swim away on its own terms.

## Snorkelling or diving: which suits you?

### Snorkelling

You need to be a confident swimmer, but you do not need any training. Most boat trips provide a mask, snorkel and fins, and many also offer life jackets or buoyancy vests. Ask for one even if you swim well; it lets you float and look rather than tread water.

Good habits:

- Practise breathing through the snorkel in shallow water before you set off.
- Stay with your group and keep the boat in sight.
- Wear a rash vest or a T-shirt to protect your back from the sun.
- Do not stand on coral or rocks, and do not touch or feed marine life.

### Trying scuba for the first time

Many dive centres on the coast run introductory dives for people with no experience, where an instructor stays close to you in shallow water. These are a good taster, but they are not a qualification. If you enjoy it, a full beginner course run by a recognised training agency takes a few days and usually combines theory, pool or shallow-water practice and open-water dives.

### Certified divers

If you already dive, bring your certification card and logbook. A reputable centre will ask about your experience and when you last dived before putting you on a boat, and may suggest a check dive if it has been a while. Plan your diving so you leave a sensible gap before your flight home: Visit Sharjah's diving page advises that international visitors make sure their [next flight is more than 24 hours after a dive](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/sports/diving-in-khorfakkan/). The same page reminds divers that taster sessions do not count as certification, that many operators will ask for a diving certificate, and that you should always do a buddy check, double check your equipment and follow your guide's instructions.

## How a day on the water unfolds

Most snorkelling and diving trips on the east coast follow a similar pattern, and knowing it helps you choose well.

You usually meet early at a beach, a hotel or a small harbour. Mornings tend to be calmer, so many boats go out first thing. There will be a briefing on land or on the boat: where you are going, how to get in and out of the water, hand signals, and what to do if you get separated. This is the moment to say if you are a nervous swimmer or have not snorkelled before.

The boat ride to a site is often short. Once there, the crew will anchor or tie on to a mooring, and you get in with your group. Snorkellers usually spend time floating over the shallower edges of a rocky island, while divers descend nearby with their guide. Many trips visit two sites, with a break on the boat in between for water, fruit and a chance to warm up.

Back on shore around midday, you have the afternoon free. That is a good time for a late lunch, a rest out of the sun, or a visit to a fort or the old mosque at Al Bidya.

### What you might see

You will not see everything on every trip, and that is part of the appeal. Visit Sharjah's pages for Khorfakkan mention blacktip reef sharks, moray eels, Arabian angelfish, rays, turtles, sea anemones and shoals of reef fish. Take a simple fish identification card if your operator has one, and spend more time looking and less time chasing. Fish that are left alone come closer.

### Families and nervous swimmers

Children who swim confidently often love snorkelling, and a buoyancy vest makes a huge difference. Choose a calm morning, a short boat ride and a shallow site, and agree on a signal for "I want to get out". For younger children, a glass of water at the edge of the beach and a mask can be enough of an adventure. Ask the operator about minimum ages for diving and snorkelling from the boat before you book.

### Accessibility

Getting on and off small boats can be awkward, often with a ladder at the back. If you have limited mobility, ask the operator exactly how boarding works, whether there is a fixed ladder or a platform, and whether they have taken guests with similar needs before. Snorkelling from a gently shelving beach with lifeguards can be easier than a boat trip.

## When to go

The Fujairah Tourism and Antiquities Authority describes summer, from [May to September](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-Overview.html), as hot and sometimes humid, and October to April as very pleasant. Snorkelling and diving run through much of the year, but visibility and sea conditions change with wind, plankton and weather, so the same spot can be clear one week and murky the next. Ask the operator what to expect in your month, and be ready to change the day if the wind picks up. ## The safety checks that matter

The sea here is generally calm, but it is still the open sea.

### Boats and life jackets

The UK Foreign Office warns that the safety of tourist boats [may not be up to UK standards](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) and advises making sure life jackets are available for all passengers. Look at the boat before you get on: is there shade, a first aid kit, a radio, and enough life jackets for everyone?

The same advice notes that vessels in the Gulf of Oman may be at increased risk of maritime attack because of regional tensions. Check the latest Foreign Office advice before you travel.

### Swimming from the shore

If you snorkel from a beach, the Foreign Office warns that [rip currents can occur at any beach](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), that red flags mean swimming is dangerous, and that you should only swim from approved beaches with lifeguards and flags.

### Insurance and health

Read your policy carefully. The Foreign Office advises that travel insurance should [cover your itinerary and planned activities](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/warnings-and-insurance), and many policies treat scuba diving as an extra. Check whether yours covers diving and to what level. If you have a health condition or are pregnant, take medical advice before diving: the Foreign Office stresses that [appropriate insurance](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/health) is particularly important in those cases. In an emergency, [dial 998 for an ambulance](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/health); the Foreign Office lists [999 for the police](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/getting-help). Visit Sharjah also advises checking that you have [scuba insurance](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/sports/diving-in-khorfakkan/), since not all travel insurance covers it. Our [travel insurance guide](/stories/uae-travel-insurance-guide/) has a checklist.

### Photos on the beach

Underwater cameras are fine, but on the beach and the boat, follow the Foreign Office advice [not to photograph people without their permission](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security).

## What catches people out

- **Wind.** A calm morning can turn choppy by midday. Operators may cancel or change sites, and it is worth accepting that rather than pushing to go.
- **Sun on your back.** Floating face down for an hour is the quickest way to burn. Wear a rash vest or T-shirt.
- **Leaving it too late to book.** Weekends and public holidays are busy along this coast. Book ahead and confirm the meeting point the day before.
- **The Oman question.** Some trips from the Dibba area cross into Omani waters. Ask before you book, not on the jetty.
- **Flying too soon.** Divers should allow the gap their dive centre recommends before flying home.

## Leave the reef as you found it

Coral grows slowly and breaks easily. Keep your fins off the bottom, do not collect shells or coral, take all your rubbish home, and choose an operator who briefs you on how to behave in the water. ## Quick answers

**Where is the best snorkelling in the UAE?** Many visitors head for the east coast around Fujairah, Khorfakkan and Dibba, where the mountains meet the Gulf of Oman. Both Fujairah's tourism authority and Visit Sharjah promote snorkelling and diving there.

**Do I need to be certified to dive on the east coast?** Not for an introductory dive with an instructor, but you will need a recognised certification for regular dives.

**Is it safe to snorkel in Fujairah?** Generally yes, with a reputable operator. The UK Foreign Office advises checking that life jackets are available on boats and swimming only from approved beaches with lifeguards.

**Where can I dive in Khorfakkan?** Visit Sharjah describes seven dive sites along the bay, including Shark Island, Martini Rock and the Inchcape wreck.

**Does travel insurance cover diving?** Not always. The Foreign Office advises that insurance should cover your planned activities, so check your policy before you book.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Which site will we visit, and how will you decide on the day whether conditions are good enough?
- Are life jackets, a first aid kit and oxygen on board, and are your guides trained in rescue?
- What is the ratio of guides to snorkellers or divers in the water?
- For introductory dives, what medical questions do you ask, and what does the course include?
- If the trip goes into Omani waters, what documents do we need and who handles them?
- How do you brief guests on protecting coral and marine life?
