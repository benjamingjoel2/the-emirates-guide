You step out of an air-conditioned taxi and the heat lands on you like a weight. Your sunglasses fog over, the pavement glows white, and within a few minutes the walk that looked short on the map feels like a bad idea.

That moment is the best argument for planning around the sun in the UAE. The heat is part of the place, and with a few habits most visitors cope well. Ignore it, and it can turn a good day into a medical emergency. This guide covers UAE heat safety for travellers: when to be careful, how to shape your day and what the warning signs look like.

## UAE heat safety: the short version

The UAE government describes the country's climate as a desert climate, [hot and humid in summer](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/fact-sheet), warm and sunny in winter, and generally cooler in the eastern mountains. The key rules are simple:

- Do outdoor sightseeing early in the morning or after late afternoon, and keep the middle of the day for indoor plans.
- Drink regularly, before you feel thirsty, and carry water everywhere.
- Cover up with light, loose clothing, a wide-brimmed hat, sunglasses and sunscreen.
- Know the signs of heat exhaustion and heatstroke, and call **998** for an ambulance in an emergency.

The rest of this guide explains each one, and our [travel tips](/travel-tips/) section covers other practical planning.

## Why the middle of the day is different

There is a reason the country itself slows down at midday in summer. The UAE government's workplace rules say that outdoor work under direct sunlight is [not allowed between 12.30pm and 3pm, from 15 June to 15 September](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/jobs/Workplace-regulations/health-and-safety-at-workplace) each year, to protect workers during the hottest weather.

If it is considered too hot for people who are used to it, it is too hot for a first-time visitor to be walking around a fort or a souk. TravelHealthPro, the UK's travel health service, advises keeping out of the sun at the hottest part of the day, [typically between 11am and 3pm](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/factsheets/extreme-heat), and seeking shade outside. The NHS gives the same [11am to 3pm window](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/heat-exhaustion-heatstroke/) in its advice on preventing heat illness.

### Humidity and the city

Dry heat in the desert and sticky heat on the coast feel very different, and both take a toll. Near the sea, sweat does not evaporate as easily, so you can feel drained even on a day that does not look extreme on a forecast. TravelHealthPro also points out that the [effects of heat may be worse in cities](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/factsheets/extreme-heat), where pavements, roads and buildings hold the warmth, and that visitors who take part in strenuous activity before they are used to the climate are at particular risk. Give yourself a day or two to adjust before a long hike or a full day on foot.

## Shaping your day around the sun

The easiest way to stay safe is to plan the day, not to tough it out.

### Mornings for walking

Heritage districts, waterfront walks and outdoor markets are at their best in the early morning, before the heat builds. TravelHealthPro suggests that if you need to exercise, do it in [the coolest part of the day, usually early morning](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/factsheets/extreme-heat). The same goes for hikes, cycling and long walks.

### Middays indoors

Use the middle of the day for museums, galleries, long lunches and a rest at your hotel. Our [Louvre Abu Dhabi guide](/stories/louvre-abu-dhabi-guide/) is a good starting point for an indoor day.

### Evenings outside again

As the sun drops, the cities move outdoors: corniches, parks, souks and beaches fill up. This is the natural time for a waterfront stroll or a market visit.

### What a sensible hot day looks like

Put together, a day that respects the heat might run like this. Breakfast early, then out of the door while the light is still soft for a heritage walk or a souk visit. By late morning, head somewhere air-conditioned: a museum, a mall or your hotel. Eat a long lunch, rest, and drink steadily. Head out again in the late afternoon for a corniche walk, a dhow ride or a beach, and save the busiest market or the evening meal for after dark. It is a slower rhythm than many visitors are used to, and it is exactly how residents get through the hot months.

### Pick the season if you can

Heat is a factor in every month, but how much depends on when you go. Our [month-by-month guide](/stories/best-time-to-visit-uae-month-by-month/) explains how conditions change through the year, and if you are travelling in the hottest months, our guide to [visiting the UAE in summer](/stories/uae-in-summer/) is worth reading first.

## Sun protection is more than sunscreen

Heat and sunburn are separate problems, and you can get one without the other. TravelHealthPro's sun protection advice makes several points that catch visitors out in the UAE:

- **Wind and cloud do not protect you.** It says burning is [possible on cool, cloudy days](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/factsheets/sun-protection), and that a cool wind is falsely reassuring because UVB levels stay the same.
- **Sand and water reflect the sun.** The same factsheet notes that sand reflects UV radiation, and that rippling water and rough seas reflect more than calm water, so a day in the dunes or on a boat needs extra care.
- **Babies need full shade.** It says babies under six months should [never be left in direct sunlight](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/factsheets/sun-protection), and that children should be protected with high-factor sun cream, clothes, hats and sun shelters.
- **Some medicines matter.** It warns that certain medicines, including some antibiotics, diuretics and anti-inflammatory painkillers, can cause a skin reaction to sunlight. If you take regular medication, ask your pharmacist before you travel.

## What to pack and wear

TravelHealthPro recommends packing [lightweight, loose-fitting, light-coloured clothes](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/factsheets/extreme-heat), a wide-brimmed hat, sunglasses and sunscreen. It notes that the NHS recommends sunscreen with a sun protection factor of at least 30 and four- or five-star UVA protection, reapplied often, especially after swimming or sweating.

Two UAE-specific points are worth adding:

- **A light layer for indoors.** Visit Abu Dhabi suggests taking [a jacket or sweater](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/essential-info/climate-and-what-to-wear) for hotels and cinemas, because the air conditioning can be cold. Moving between hot streets and chilly interiors all day is tiring, and a layer helps.
- **Modest cover that is also cool.** Loose, long, breathable clothes suit both the sun and local dress norms. Our guide to [what to wear in the UAE](/stories/what-to-wear-uae-etiquette/) covers what is expected where.

A small day bag makes all of this easier: a refillable water bottle, sunscreen, a hat, sunglasses, a light scarf or layer, and your phone with emergency numbers saved.

## Water, food and alcohol

Carry cool water and sip it through the day. TravelHealthPro advises drinking plenty of fluids and [avoiding too much alcohol](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/factsheets/extreme-heat), and says everyone is at risk of dehydration in hot temperatures, with babies, young children and older people especially vulnerable.

Small habits help: a refill whenever you pass a shop, a drink with every meal, and a pause in the shade before you feel you need one. If you have been sweating heavily, the NHS suggests that an [isotonic sports drink or oral rehydration powder](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/heat-exhaustion-heatstroke/) can help replace lost salt when someone is overheated.

The NHS heatwave advice adds a few practical touches: have [cold food and regular cold drinks](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/seasonal-health/heatwave-how-to-cope-in-hot-weather/), avoid alcohol, caffeine and hot drinks, and take a cool shower or put cool water on your skin or clothes. A cold flannel on the back of the neck after a morning walk can make a real difference.

### Water during Ramadan

If you visit during Ramadan, drinking in public needs a little thought. The UK Foreign Office says it may cause offence to [eat, drink, smoke or chew gum in public](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) in the daytime, including in your car. That does not mean going without water in the heat. Drink in your hotel, in designated dining areas or discreetly out of sight, and keep outdoor plans short. The UAE government's Ramadan page also advises those who are fasting to drink [enough water during the night](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/public-holidays-and-religious-affairs/ramadan) and not to exert themselves physically while fasting, which is sensible guidance for anyone travelling with fasting companions or guides. Our guide to [bringing medicines into the UAE](/stories/bringing-medicines-into-uae/) is worth reading if you need regular medication through the day.

## Who needs to take extra care

Anyone can be affected by heat, but some people are more at risk. TravelHealthPro says older people, babies and very young children are [more vulnerable to higher temperatures](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/factsheets/extreme-heat), and that pregnant women and people with underlying health problems may also be more susceptible. It notes that heart problems, breathing problems, kidney disease and diabetes may get worse in hot weather, and that some medications can increase the risk.

The NHS gives a fuller picture of who is most at risk in a heatwave, including people [aged 65 and over](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/seasonal-health/heatwave-how-to-cope-in-hot-weather/), children aged five and under, people on multiple medicines, and anyone who spends a lot of time outside being physically active, such as runners, cyclists and walkers.

If that applies to anyone in your group, TravelHealthPro advises getting advice from a doctor or specialist [ideally 4 to 6 weeks before travel](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/factsheets/extreme-heat).

### Children and cars

TravelHealthPro is blunt about parked cars: [never leave anyone](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/factsheets/extreme-heat), including babies, young children or animals, in a closed car, as they can overheat very quickly. Even a short stop counts.

## Warning signs: heat exhaustion and heatstroke

Know these before you need them.

### Heat exhaustion

The NHS lists the symptoms of heat exhaustion as including [tiredness, dizziness, headache, nausea or vomiting](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/heat-exhaustion-heatstroke/), excessive sweating with pale, clammy skin, cramps, a high temperature, strong thirst and irritability. Its advice is to:

1. Move the person to a cool place.
2. Remove unnecessary clothing.
3. Give them plenty of water, or an isotonic or rehydration drink.
4. Cool their skin by spraying or sponging with cool water and fanning them.

The NHS says they should start to feel better [within 30 minutes](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/heat-exhaustion-heatstroke/). It also suggests that cold packs wrapped in a cloth and placed under the armpits or on the neck can help, and that you should stay with the person until they are better.

### Heatstroke

Heatstroke is a medical emergency. The NHS lists signs including [a very high temperature, hot skin that is not sweating](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/heat-exhaustion-heatstroke/), fast breathing and heartbeat, confusion, a seizure or loss of consciousness, as well as still being unwell after 30 minutes of cooling and resting.

In the UAE, the UK Foreign Office says to [dial 998 and ask for an ambulance](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/health). It also stresses the importance of travel insurance that covers local treatment. While you wait, the NHS advises wrapping the person in a cool, wet sheet or fanning and sponging them with cold water.

### Getting medical help in the UAE

The UAE government says visitors can obtain treatment at private or government hospitals, and that in an emergency, [treatment to stabilise the case is free](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/justice-safety-and-the-law/Safety/handling-emergencies), while other treatment must be paid for by cash, card or insurance. The same page lists two official emergency apps: the AD999 app from Abu Dhabi Police and the DCAS SOS app for ambulance requests in Dubai, which can share your exact location.

The Foreign Office adds that you may be stopped from using healthcare facilities [without travel insurance or the means to pay](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/health), and that you should contact your insurer quickly if you are referred to a medical facility. Keep your policy number and the insurer's emergency line on your phone.

## Desert, beach and outdoor adventures

Heat risks multiply away from the city.

In the desert, the UK Foreign Office advises that excursions can be dangerous unless you are in a properly equipped four-wheel-drive vehicle, and says to [travel with other cars, take a supply of water and a mobile telephone](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), and leave your travel plans with someone. Our guide to [whether a desert safari is worth it](/stories/is-a-desert-safari-worth-it/) covers what to ask before you book.

On a mountain hike, the heat bounces off bare rock and shade can be scarce. Start at first light, choose a short route if you are new to the climate, carry more water than you think you need, and turn back early rather than late. Tell someone where you are going and when you expect to return.

At the beach, cooling off in the sea is tempting, but the same Foreign Office page warns that rip currents can occur at any beach and advises swimming only from [approved beaches with lifeguards and flags](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security). Red flags mean swimming is dangerous.

## What catches visitors out

- **The first day.** Jet lag, a long flight and a full itinerary are a bad mix with heat. Keep day one gentle.
- **Short walks that turn long.** A walk between two sights can look close on a map and feel very different on hot pavement. Take a taxi.
- **Cold interiors.** Going in and out of chilly air conditioning all day is tiring. Carry a layer.
- **Thirst as a signal.** By the time you feel thirsty, you are already behind. Sip steadily.
- **Evenings that stay warm.** Even after dark, it can still be hot and humid, so keep drinking water at dinner.

## Quick answers

**What time of day is the heat most dangerous?**
Both TravelHealthPro and the NHS advise avoiding the sun between 11am and 3pm. In summer, UAE rules also ban outdoor work in direct sunlight from 12.30pm to 3pm between 15 June and 15 September.

**What number do I call for an ambulance in the UAE?**
The UK Foreign Office says to dial 998 and ask for an ambulance.

**What are the signs of heatstroke?**
The NHS lists a very high temperature, hot skin that is not sweating, fast breathing and heartbeat, confusion, seizures and loss of consciousness. Treat it as an emergency.

**What should I wear in the UAE heat?**
TravelHealthPro recommends light, loose, light-coloured clothes, a wide-brimmed hat, sunglasses and high-factor sunscreen. Visit Abu Dhabi suggests a light layer for cold air conditioning indoors.

## Questions to ask your operator

- What time does the tour start and finish, and how much of it is spent outdoors in direct sun?
- Is there shade, air-conditioned transport and cold drinking water available throughout?
- Can the itinerary be shortened or moved indoors if the heat is too much for someone in our group?
- Are your guides trained in first aid, and what is your plan if someone shows signs of heat illness?
- For desert trips, how many vehicles travel together, and what water and communications do you carry?
- Is this activity suitable for young children, older travellers or anyone pregnant in the month we are visiting?
