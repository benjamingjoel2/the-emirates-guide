A raised middle finger in a traffic jam. A cheeky photo of a stranger on the beach. An angry comment typed into a review site after a bad meal. At home, these are moments of bad manners. In the UAE, each one can be a criminal matter.

That is the heart of it. UAE laws for tourists are not complicated, and the vast majority of visitors never come anywhere near trouble. But a handful of things that feel trivial elsewhere are taken seriously here, and not knowing the rule is not a defence. The UK Foreign Office sums it up: there may be [serious penalties for doing something that might not be illegal in the UK](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) but is in the UAE.

Here is what you need to know, drawn from official UAE government and UK government advice. Laws and how they are enforced can change, so check the linked sources before you go.

## The spirit behind the rules

It helps to understand where the rules come from. The Foreign Office describes the UAE as a Muslim country and asks visitors to [respect local traditions, customs, laws and religions](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), taking particular care during the holy month of Ramadan and in religious areas. Visit Abu Dhabi adds that the UAE Constitution provides for [freedom of religion in accordance with established customs](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/faqs), and that people are free to practise their beliefs as long as they do not interfere with Islam.

Most of what follows flows from that idea of respect in shared, public spaces. Behaviour that stays private and considerate is rarely an issue. Behaviour that is public, loud, or aimed at someone else is where problems start.

## Drugs: the rule with no wiggle room

Start here, because the consequences are the most severe. The Foreign Office says there is [zero tolerance for drugs-related offences](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), and that penalties for using or possessing illegal drugs, including residual amounts, are severe. It says possession of even the smallest amount, including cannabis, can lead to a minimum three-month prison term or a fine of between 20,000 and 100,000 UAE dirham. The authorities count the presence of drugs in the bloodstream as possession. So something taken legally in another country before you fly could still be a problem on arrival.

The UAE government describes a [zero-tolerance policy for recreational use of drugs](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/health-and-fitness/Health-and-wellbeing/drugs-and-controlled-medicines). The Foreign Office adds that some herbal highs and products containing CBD are illegal, as are some skincare products and e-cigarette refills that contain CBD oil, and that airport scanning technology is also used on the baggage of transiting passengers.

Prescription medicines have their own rules, and some common ones are controlled. Our guide to [bringing medicines into the UAE](/stories/bringing-medicines-into-uae/) explains how to check yours.

## Alcohol: where, when and who

Alcohol is available, but within clear limits. The Foreign Office says it is [illegal to drink or be under the influence of alcohol in a public place](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) anywhere in the UAE. The legal age to buy and drink is 21 where alcohol is allowed, and drinking or possessing alcohol is illegal in the emirate of Sharjah. Drink-driving is illegal no matter how small the amount.

In practice that means drinking in licensed venues, getting home by taxi, and keeping the evening's high spirits indoors. Our guide to [alcohol in the UAE](/stories/alcohol-rules-in-the-uae/) covers the details emirate by emirate.

## Words and gestures, online and off

This catches out more visitors than almost anything else. The Foreign Office says it is [illegal to swear and make rude gestures, including online](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), as they are considered obscene acts, and that you could be jailed or deported. It specifically mentions offensive gestures and bad language directed at other drivers, and advises taking particular care when dealing with the police and other officials.

The online part matters. The UAE has a cybercrime law, [Federal Decree Law No. 34 of 2021](https://u.ae/en/media/media-in-the-uae/media-regulation), which the government says aims to combat rumours and fraud through electronic communication. The same page says this law and other laws protecting privacy and reputation, including defamation, apply while using social media. Think before posting an angry review, an insulting message or a photo of someone else. Respect for religion is also protected by law: the government says [Federal Decree Law No. 34 of 2023](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/justice-safety-and-the-law/Safety/maintaining-safety-and-security) prohibits contempt of religions and disrespectful behaviour towards places of worship, and penalises hate speech.

The Foreign Office also warns that fundraising and acts of charity, including online and social media activity, are [heavily regulated](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security).

### A practical rule for heated moments

If something goes wrong, such as a bad meal, a dented hire car or a delayed transfer, keep the complaint polite, factual and private. Ask for a manager, take notes and photos of the problem rather than the people, and leave reviews until you have calmed down. A measured complaint gets results here as well as anywhere, and it keeps you well clear of the swearing and defamation rules.

## Dress and affection in public

### What to wear

The Foreign Office advises [dressing modestly in public areas](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) like shopping malls. Women should cover shoulders and knees, underwear should not be visible, and transparent clothing or clothing with offensive images or slogans should be avoided. Swimwear belongs on beaches and at pools. It also notes that cross-dressing is illegal, and that some venues, events or buildings have their own dress codes. Our guide to [what to wear in the UAE](/stories/what-to-wear-uae-etiquette/) has practical outfit ideas.

### Holding hands, kissing and relationships

The Foreign Office says showing affection in public is frowned upon, and that [you can be arrested for kissing in public](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security). On relationships, it says consensual sexual relationships between a man and a woman outside marriage are generally legal if both are aged 18 or over, though a complaint by a spouse or guardian can still lead to prosecution in some circumstances.

For LGBT+ travellers, the Foreign Office says [same-sex sexual activity is illegal](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) and same-sex marriages are not recognised. It notes that private life is generally respected, but that there have been reports of people being punished, particularly where there is a public element.

## Cameras, people and sensitive places

A camera is welcome almost everywhere, with two clear exceptions. The Foreign Office says it is [illegal to take photos of some government buildings and military installations](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), and warns: do not photograph people without their permission. It notes that men have been arrested for photographing women on beaches.

Visit Abu Dhabi adds that it is polite to [ask permission before taking photos of people](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/faqs), particularly women, and that cameras may be banned in public areas set aside for women and children. Drones have their own strict regime. See our guide to [photography and drone rules](/stories/photography-and-drone-rules-uae/).

## On the road, on foot and in taxis

### Crossing the road

It sounds minor, but the Foreign Office says you should [only cross roads using designated pedestrian crossings](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), and that crossing elsewhere is illegal and can lead to a fine or even imprisonment. It also cautions that vehicles often do not stop at zebra crossings, so take care.

### Taking taxis

The Foreign Office advises using only licensed taxis or other recognised public transport, including ride-hailing apps. It notes that in Dubai authorised taxis are cream with different coloured roofs, with pink roofs for female drivers, and that in Abu Dhabi they are silver or grey. Do not accept lifts from strangers.

### If you drive

Driving brings its own set of rules, and the penalties can be heavier than visitors expect. The Foreign Office says police have the power to [impound your car for traffic violations](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) that might be considered minor in the UK, with a release fee of 50,000 UAE dirham plus the traffic fine. Offensive gestures and bad language aimed at other drivers can lead to fines, jail and possibly deportation, and flashing headlights can mean a driver is coming through rather than giving way.

After a crash, every driver involved must stop when it is safe to do so, and it is illegal to leave the scene before the police arrive. The rules on moving your car differ by emirate, so check them before you set off. Visit Abu Dhabi adds that you [cannot file an insurance claim without a police report](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/faqs). Our guide to [driving in the UAE as a visitor](/stories/driving-in-the-uae-as-a-visitor/) covers licences and road rules in more depth.

## Money, bills and hotels

### Paying your bills

The Foreign Office warns that financial crimes, including fraud and the [non-payment of bills, hotel bills included](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), can result in prison, a fine or both, and that bank accounts and other assets can be frozen. It adds that a non-resident arrested for a financial crime is unlikely to be granted bail. Settle up before you leave, keep receipts, and query any disputed charge calmly at the time rather than walking away from it.

### Hotel rules

The Foreign Office says it is normal practice for hotels to take a photocopy of your passport or Emirates ID, and that you cannot stay in a hotel if you are under 18 and not accompanied by an adult. That second rule matters for families whose older teenagers might want a room of their own elsewhere.

### Leaving the country

Legal and financial problems can follow you to the airport. The Foreign Office says you [may be prevented from leaving the UAE](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/entry-requirements) if you are subject to a travel ban, involved in legal proceedings or have unpaid debt.

## Ramadan, smoking and other everyday courtesies

### Ramadan

During the holy month, the Foreign Office says it may cause offence if you eat, drink, smoke or chew gum in public in the daytime, including in your car, or if you play loud music, dance or swear in public. It also suggests following local dress codes closely, as clothing that falls short may cause more offence at this time, and being patient with people who are fasting. Our guide to [visiting during Ramadan](/stories/visiting-uae-during-ramadan/) explains how the month works for visitors.

### Smoking

Visit Abu Dhabi says that some restaurants and cafes allow smoking inside while others are smoke-free, and that most business, shopping and leisure places have [designated smoking areas](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/faqs). It asks visitors to refrain from smoking between dawn and dusk during Ramadan.

### Knives and weapons

The UAE government says people found carrying a tool capable of [cutting, piercing or stabbing without a valid professional reason](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/justice-safety-and-the-law/Safety/maintaining-safety-and-security) may face imprisonment or fines. It adds that under Federal Decree Law No. 17 of 2019, nobody may carry, import or transport weapons, ammunition or hazardous substances without a licence, and that licences to possess or carry weapons are only available to UAE citizens. Leave the camping knife at home.

### At the beach

Swimming rules are about safety rather than law, but they are worth following. The Foreign Office says rip currents can occur at any beach, advises checking for warning signs, especially red flags, and suggests swimming only from approved beaches with lifeguards.

## What not to pack

Customs rules matter before you even reach the city. The Foreign Office says it is [illegal to bring pork products or pornography](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/entry-requirements) into the UAE, and that videos, books and magazines may be scrutinised. You need permission to bring in weapons and related equipment, even items such as gun belts and cleaning kits.

The UAE government lists banned items including [controlled or recreational drugs, items used in black magic or sorcery](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/visiting-and-exploring-the-uae/Tourist-services-and-information/Customs-clearance), pirated content, counterfeit currency, publications that challenge Islamic teachings, and gambling tools. Restricted items, such as medicines, transmission devices and e-cigarettes, need prior approval from the relevant authority. The same page says anyone entering or leaving with more than AED 60,000 in cash, or the equivalent, must declare it.

The Foreign Office also warns that you may need a licence for equipment such as satellite phones, radio transmitters, powerful cameras or binoculars, so check with the UAE embassy before packing specialist kit.

## If something goes wrong

Most problems on holiday are small, and they are best handled calmly and early. For emergencies, the Foreign Office lists the numbers: [ambulance 998, fire 997 and police 999](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/getting-help). It suggests contacting your travel provider and insurer if you are involved in a serious incident, and says its own consular guidance covers finding English-speaking lawyers, translators and interpreters, being arrested, and getting help as a victim of crime.

In the capital, Visit Abu Dhabi says Abu Dhabi Police runs a dedicated Tourism Police section that can advise visitors, reachable on [+971 2 699 9999](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/faqs).

## Quick answers

**Is it illegal to swear in the UAE?** Yes. The Foreign Office says swearing and rude gestures, including online, are illegal and can lead to jail or deportation.

**Can tourists drink alcohol in the UAE?** Yes, in licensed venues and where alcohol is permitted, from age 21. Drinking or being drunk in public is illegal everywhere, and alcohol is illegal in Sharjah.

**Can I kiss my partner in public in the UAE?** The Foreign Office says public affection is frowned upon and you can be arrested for kissing in public.

**Can I take photos of people in the UAE?** Only with their permission. The Foreign Office warns against photographing people without consent and certain government and military sites.

**Is jaywalking illegal in the UAE?** Yes. The Foreign Office says crossing anywhere other than a designated pedestrian crossing is illegal and can lead to a fine or imprisonment.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Is there a dress code at any of the places on our itinerary, and what should we bring to cover up?
- Are there places on our tours where photography is restricted or where we should ask before taking pictures?
- If our trip crosses into Sharjah, how does the alcohol law affect our plans and transfers?
- Can your guides give us a short briefing on local customs at the start of the trip?
- If something goes wrong, such as a dispute or a minor accident, what should we do and who should we call?
