Your mask fogs for a moment, you clear it, and there it is: a turtle hanging in the blue just below you, one flipper lazily sweeping, utterly unbothered. Then it tilts and glides off over the coral, and you realise you have been holding your breath.

Most visitors come to the UAE for deserts and skylines and never look under the surface. That is a mistake. Marine life in UAE waters includes sea turtles, one of the world's largest dugong populations, several kinds of dolphin, sharks and reefs full of fish. The simplest way to see it is to snorkel or dive on the east coast at Khorfakkan or Fujairah, or to join a kayak, snorkel or eco-boat trip in one of Abu Dhabi's marine reserves. Dugongs are rarely seen by casual visitors; turtles and reef fish are far more likely.

## Marine life in UAE waters: two seas, two worlds

The UAE faces two bodies of water. The Arabian Gulf, along the west coast from Abu Dhabi to Ras Al Khaimah, is shallow and warm, with wide seagrass beds and mangroves. The Gulf of Oman, on the east coast around Fujairah and Khorfakkan, is deeper, backed by mountains, with rockier reefs.

The UAE government portal explains why the habitats matter: [seagrass beds are particularly important for dugongs and green turtles](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/environment-and-energy/Nature-and-wildlife/topography-and-ecosystems), while coral reefs support a wide range of life including the endangered hawksbill turtle.

In practice, the two coasts suit different trips. The Gulf side, with its calm, shallow water and long beaches, is good for kayaking, paddleboarding and gentle snorkelling, and it is where the big seagrass meadows and most of the dugongs are. The east coast suits snorkellers and divers who want rock, coral and fish in clearer water, and it is closer to the mountains if you want to combine sea and land in one trip.

## Dugongs: the sea's quietest giants

Dugongs are big, shy, seagrass-grazing mammals, cousins of the manatee. The UAE government portal says Abu Dhabi has [the world's second-largest dugong population, with around 3,000 animals](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/environment-and-energy/Nature-and-wildlife/topography-and-ecosystems), found mainly around the Marawah Marine Biosphere Reserve and Al Yasat Marine Protected Area.

Marawah is the heartland. Visit Abu Dhabi describes it as the UAE's [first marine biosphere reserve, covering 63% of Abu Dhabi's marine protected areas](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/where-to-go/marine-protected-areas), and says it is accessible on specialised eco-tours. The Environment Agency Abu Dhabi calls it [a site of global importance](https://www.ead.gov.ae/en/Discover-Our-Biodiversity/Sheikh-Zayed-Protected-Areas-Network) because of its dugongs.

Be realistic: these are remote waters, and a dugong sighting is a privilege rather than an itinerary item. If seeing one matters to you, ask an operator honestly how often they do.

Visit Abu Dhabi gives a sense of why Marawah matters so much. It describes a reserve of [4,255 square kilometres](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/where-to-go/marine-protected-areas) with 120km of coastline and more than ten islands, holding seagrass beds, coral reefs, macroalgae and mangroves, and more than 150 species of fish. Al Yasat, in the far west of Al Dhafra, is described on the same page as a group of historically important islands where regulated boat tours, snorkelling, and dolphin and turtle watching are possible.

### What a dugong looks like from a boat

If you are lucky enough to be in dugong waters, know what to look for. Dugongs surface briefly to breathe, often showing only a rounded back or the top of the head before sinking again. In calm water, a guide may spot the feeding trails they leave across seagrass, or a swirl at the surface. Keep voices down, keep the engine slow, and accept that a brief glimpse is the normal experience.

## Turtles: where they feed and nest

Turtles are the animal you are most likely to meet. The portal notes that Marawah has [important foraging and nesting areas for green and hawksbill turtles](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/environment-and-energy/Nature-and-wildlife/topography-and-ecosystems), and that the beaches of Sir Bu Na'air island in Sharjah are nesting grounds for both species.

Closer to the cities:

- **Saadiyat, Abu Dhabi.** Visit Abu Dhabi says endangered hawksbill turtles [nest on the island](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/where-to-go/marine-protected-areas), and that Saadiyat Marine National Park is open for non-motorised activities such as snorkelling, kayaking and paddleboarding.
- **Ras Ghanadah, Abu Dhabi.** The same page describes it as the largest coral reef in the UAE and Gulf region, where you are very likely to spot turtles, dugongs, sea snakes and clownfish.
- **Jabal Ali, Dubai.** Dubai Municipality says its beaches are [the last remaining breeding grounds in Dubai of the hawksbill turtle](https://www.dm.gov.ae/dubai-protected-areas/wildlife/), and that green, hawksbill and loggerhead turtles have all been recorded there.
- **Kalba, Sharjah.** Visit Sharjah says you may see [hawksbill and green turtles nesting on the beach](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/nature-and-wildlife/al-qurm-protected-area/) near the Al Qurm mangroves, and the [Khor Kalba Mangrove Centre](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/nature-and-wildlife/khor-kalba-mangrove-centre/) shows green turtles, crabs and fish. Our guide to [Kalba and its mangroves](/stories/kalba-mangroves-guide/) has more.

If you come across a nesting turtle or hatchlings, keep well back, stay dark and quiet, and never touch them.

### Meeting a turtle in the water

<photo-set layout="inset-left" keys="uae-marine-life-guide-2" alts="A sea turtle swimming towards the camera above a rocky seabed off Dibba" caption="Turtles are the marine animal visitors are most likely to meet while snorkelling on the east coast."></photo-set>

Turtles are usually calm around snorkellers who behave well. Float on the surface, keep your arms by your sides, and let the turtle choose the distance. Avoid swimming directly above one, because a turtle needs to rise to the surface to breathe, and a snorkeller blocking its path can make it bolt. Never follow a turtle as it swims away. A minute of quiet watching from the side is worth more than a chase that ends with a stressed animal.

Green turtles graze on seagrass and algae, while the hawksbill, with its narrow pointed beak and overlapping shell plates, is more often seen around reef and rock. You do not need to identify them to enjoy the encounter, but knowing the difference helps you understand why a reef dive turns up one species and a seagrass bay the other.

## Dolphins from a boat

The portal says Abu Dhabi's waters support [more than 700 dolphins](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/environment-and-energy/Nature-and-wildlife/topography-and-ecosystems), including the world's largest population of Indian Ocean humpback dolphins. Visit Abu Dhabi notes that in-water swims with wild dolphins are [not widely offered because of conservation regulations](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/where-to-go/marine-protected-areas), but that humpback and bottlenose dolphins are often spotted from boat tours, kayaks or the shore around Saadiyat and Sir Bani Yas.

That is the right way to see them: from a distance, engine slowed, on their terms.

## The east coast: reefs and sharks

### Khorfakkan

For underwater life, the east coast is where most visitors start. Visit Sharjah says [seven dive sites](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/sports/diving-in-khorfakkan/) are strung along Khorfakkan bay, from reefs to a purposely sunk wreck. Around Shark Island it lists blacktip reef sharks, moray eels, Arabian angelfish and turtles, and on the wreck, barracuda and the occasional large ray. Our [Khorfakkan guide](/stories/khorfakkan-guide/) covers the town.

The same page names sites including Martini Rock, Hole in the Wall, Anemone Gardens and Coral Gardens, and describes granite formations and large coral heads that make the bay unusual underwater. A typical dive or snorkel trip here leaves by boat in the morning, visits one or two sites close to the shore, and is back before the afternoon wind picks up.

### Fujairah and Dibba

The government portal says Fujairah has [around 130 coral species recorded](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/environment-and-energy/Nature-and-wildlife/topography-and-ecosystems), and marine protected areas along the coast of Dibba. The local tourism authority lists [snorkelling, diving and kayaking](https://www.fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-SportsAndCruises.html) among Fujairah's water sports. For spots and logistics, read our guide to [snorkelling and diving on the east coast](/stories/snorkelling-diving-uae-east-coast/).

### How a snorkel trip usually unfolds

Most east coast snorkel trips follow the same pattern. You meet the boat in the morning, get a short briefing and fit masks and fins on board, then motor out to a sheltered site. The crew chooses where to stop based on wind and visibility that day, so a site named in the brochure is never guaranteed. Expect around an hour in the water at each stop, with time back on board to warm up and move on. Good operators keep the group together, put a guide in the water and count everyone back on.

Ras Ghanadah, on the Abu Dhabi side, is a different proposition. Visit Abu Dhabi describes it as a 55 square kilometre protected area about 90km from the capital, where around 40% of the coral species in the Arabian Gulf can be found. Trips there are more of a planned day out than a casual morning swim.

## Without getting wet

Not a swimmer? You still have options:

- **Kayak the mangroves.** At Jubail Mangrove Park, Visit Abu Dhabi says you can glimpse [turtles, herons and gazelles](https://www.visitabudhabi.ae/en/things-to-do/nature-and-wildlife/parks/jubail-mangrove-park) from boardwalks or by kayak. See our guide to [kayaking the Abu Dhabi mangroves](/stories/abu-dhabi-mangrove-kayaking/).
- **Visit an aquarium.** Visit Sharjah says Sharjah Aquarium has [20 tanks recreating local habitats](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/nature-and-wildlife/sharjah-aquarium/), from reefs and rock pools to mangroves, which is a good primer before you snorkel. The same page says the aquarium holds more than 150 species of fish, has an indoor walkway for close viewing, runs scheduled feeding times and talks, and that the ticket includes the maritime museum next door.

## Seasons, visibility and conditions

Conditions vary through the year and from day to day. Wind is the main enemy of snorkelling: it stirs up the water, reduces visibility and makes boat rides uncomfortable, so flexible plans pay off. Early mornings are usually calmer than afternoons. The cooler months are more comfortable for time on the water, while summer brings very warm sea temperatures and intense sun, which means rash vests, reef safe habits and plenty of water on board.

Turtle nesting and hatching happen at particular times of year on protected beaches, which is one reason some coastal sites restrict access. If you hope to see nesting, ask a licensed operator or the managing authority rather than heading onto a beach at night on your own.

## Families and less confident swimmers

Children and weaker swimmers can still enjoy UAE marine life. Start with an aquarium visit or a mangrove boardwalk, then try a calm, shallow bay with a guide and a buoyancy vest. Ask operators whether children's masks and fins are available, whether a guide stays in the water with the group, and whether there is shade on the boat. For divers, the advice is simple: Visit Sharjah says [taster sessions do not count as certification](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/sports/diving-in-khorfakkan/), and many dive operations ask to see a certificate.

## What catches people out

- **Expecting dugongs on a casual trip.** They live mainly in remote western waters and are rarely seen.
- **Booking a dolphin swim.** In Abu Dhabi, watching from a boat or kayak is the norm.
- **Ignoring the wind forecast.** Choppy days can mean poor visibility and cancelled trips.
- **Touching the reef.** Coral is alive, fragile and often sharp; float above it.
- **Assuming travel insurance covers diving.** Visit Sharjah warns that not all travel policies cover scuba.

## Respect the reef

These animals are protected by law. The UAE government portal lists a [national plan of action for marine turtles](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/environment-and-energy/Nature-and-wildlife/Biodiversity) and regulations on shark fishing and trade. In the water:

- Never touch, chase or ride turtles, dolphins or sharks.
- Do not stand on coral or take shells, coral or rocks.
- Use a boat or operator that keeps its distance and slows down near wildlife.
- Take every scrap of rubbish home.

In Abu Dhabi, the Environment Agency coordinates a [wildlife response network](https://www.ead.gov.ae/en/Discover-Our-Biodiversity/Abu-Dhabi-Wildlife-Response-Network) for animals that need rescue and care, so if you find a stranded or injured animal, tell your guide or hotel so it reaches the right people. ## Staying safe in the water

The UK Foreign Office warns that [rip currents can occur at any beach](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), and advises swimming only from approved beaches with lifeguards and flags. It also says the safety of tourist boats may not match UK standards, so check life jackets are available for everyone.

For diving, Visit Sharjah advises being [properly certified, diving with a certified school, checking that your insurance covers scuba](https://www.visitsharjah.com/en/activities/sports/diving-in-khorfakkan/), and leaving more than 24 hours between your last dive and your flight.

## Quick answers

**Can you see dugongs in the UAE?** Yes, but rarely. Abu Dhabi has around 3,000, mainly around Marawah and Al Yasat, which are remote and reached on specialised eco-tours.

**Where can I snorkel with turtles in the UAE?** Khorfakkan's Shark Island and Saadiyat Marine National Park are both places where turtles are listed among the marine life.

**Can you swim with wild dolphins in Abu Dhabi?** Visit Abu Dhabi says in-water swims with wild dolphins are not widely offered because of conservation rules, but dolphins are often seen from boats and kayaks.

**Is there marine life to see without swimming?** Yes. Mangrove boardwalks and kayak trips, boat tours for dolphins, and Sharjah Aquarium all show local species without getting in the sea.

**When can I fly after diving?** Visit Sharjah advises leaving more than 24 hours between diving and your next flight.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Is the trip inside a marine protected area, and are you licensed to operate there?
- What marine life have you actually seen on this route recently, and how often do you see turtles or dugongs?
- How close do your boats go to dolphins and turtles, and do you slow down near them?
- Are life jackets provided for everyone, and what happens if the sea is rough?
- Do you check diving certification and insurance before we go in?
- Is the snorkelling suitable for weak swimmers or children, and is a guide in the water with us?
