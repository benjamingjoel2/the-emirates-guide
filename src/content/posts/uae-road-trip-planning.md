The city thins out faster than you expect. One moment you are in wide lanes of traffic between glass towers; not long after, the verges have turned to sand, a line of camels is ambling along a fence, and the road ahead runs straight towards a smudge of mountains on the horizon.

That shift is what a UAE road trip gives you. The country is compact, the main highways are fast and well signed, and many of its best places, from the Liwa dunes to the Jebel Jais switchbacks and the fishing towns of the east coast, are far easier to reach with your own wheels than without.

Driving here is not like driving at home, though. The rules on licences, accidents and behaviour behind the wheel are strict, and the desert and mountains need respect. Planning a UAE road trip well comes down to three things: choosing a route that suits your time, getting the paperwork right, and knowing the rules before you turn the key.

## Can visitors drive in the UAE?

Usually, yes. For British visitors, the UK government says you can [use a UK photocard driving licence to drive in the UAE](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security). If you still have a paper licence, you may need to update it to a photocard or get the correct international driving permit as well.

For other nationalities, the UAE government notes that [some visitors have the right to drive with their national driving licence](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/transportation/Driving-and-licensing/get-a-driving-licence), and points to its Markhoos initiative for the list of eligible countries. Check your own case before you book a car, and ask the hire company what it accepts, as rental firms set their own conditions.

Our guide to [driving in the UAE as a visitor](/stories/driving-in-the-uae-as-a-visitor/) goes deeper on licences, tolls and hire cars.

### Hiring the car

Book the car for the days you actually need it. On many trips the cities are easier by taxi, and the car only earns its keep once you head for the mountains, the dunes or the east coast. When you collect it, check the agreement for three things: who is insured to drive, what it says about off-road use, and what you must do after an accident. The UAE government's road safety page says insurers [must provide full civil liability coverage](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/justice-safety-and-the-law/Safety/road-safety), but your own cover for the car and for yourselves depends on the policy you buy.

A four-wheel drive is not essential on sealed roads. Ras Al Khaimah's tourism board, though, [highly recommends one](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/faqs/) for mountain or desert areas, so think about where you are really going before you choose.

## The rules of the road that catch visitors out

The UK government is frank that [driving standards are not always as disciplined as in the UK](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), that speeding is common despite widespread speed cameras, and that flashing headlights can mean a driver is coming through rather than giving way. Drive defensively and leave plenty of room.

A few rules matter more than any others:

- **No alcohol at all.** It is [illegal to drink and drive, no matter how small the amount](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), and your insurance is likely to be invalidated after an accident.
- **Keep your temper.** Offensive gestures and bad language towards other drivers can lead to fines, jail and possible deportation, according to the same advice.
- **Police can impound cars** for violations that might be considered minor in the UK.
- **Watch for pedestrians, and be one carefully.** The UAE government's road safety page says [pedestrians are prohibited from crossing roads where the speed limit exceeds 80 km/h](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/justice-safety-and-the-law/Safety/road-safety), and that horns within city limits are for emergencies only.
- **Do not pull over for a photo.** The same page says parking, stopping or repairing vehicles on roads or pavements is prohibited. Wait for a proper lay-by or pull-in.

### If you have an accident

The rules differ by emirate, which is worth knowing on a cross-country trip. The UK government sets them out: [in Abu Dhabi](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), if no one is hurt and damage is minor, move your vehicle to the side of the road; in Dubai, only move it if it is obstructing other motorists; in the other emirates, only move it if the accident is minor and both parties agree who is responsible. In all cases, call the police, and do not leave the scene before they arrive.

The UAE government adds that [minor accidents can be reported online](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/justice-safety-and-the-law/Safety/road-safety) through apps including the MoI UAE, Abu Dhabi Police and Dubai Police apps, and the Rafid app in Sharjah. Ask your hire company which they expect you to use. If anyone is hurt, the UK government gives 998 as the number to [call for an ambulance](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/health).

## Three routes for a UAE road trip

### West to Liwa and the Empty Quarter

The biggest dunes in the country lie in Abu Dhabi's Al Dhafra region. Visit Abu Dhabi says driving there typically takes [around two to three hours from Abu Dhabi city](https://www.visitabudhabi.ae/en/where-to-go/regions/al-dhafra), and that a car is the most convenient way to explore, as attractions and towns are spread out. Give it at least one night. Our [Liwa and Empty Quarter guide](/stories/liwa-empty-quarter-guide/) covers where to go once you are there.

The same page notes that the Liwa International Festival fills the Empty Quarter each December with three weeks of desert motorsports, concerts and fireworks, so roads and rooms are busier then. For Sir Bani Yas Island, it points visitors to separate ferry and access information, so check that before you set out.

<photo-set layout="wide" keys="uae-road-trip-planning-3" alts="ALT3" caption="CAP3"></photo-set>

Al Ain makes a good inland add-on. Visit Abu Dhabi puts it [a 90-minute drive from Abu Dhabi city](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/article-hub/essential-things-to-do-in-al-ain), with the oasis, Al Jahili Fort and the road up Jebel Hafit all close together. The same page describes Jebel Hafit as the second-highest peak in the UAE, popular with hikers and cyclists as well as drivers, so take the bends slowly.

### North to the Ras Al Khaimah mountains

Ras Al Khaimah's tourism board says the emirate is [easiest to get around by car](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/faqs/), and that a four-wheel-drive vehicle is highly recommended for mountain or desert areas. The highlight is the road up Jebel Jais, which the tourism board describes as having [hairpin bends and regular pull-ins](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/location/place/jebel-jais/) where you can stop safely to look at the view. The road to Jais Viewing Deck Park is, it says, [accessible by cars, 4x4s, motorbikes and cycles](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/discover/attractions/jais-viewing-deck-park/), so watch for cyclists on the bends.

Drive down in daylight if you can, take it slowly, and use a low gear on the descent rather than riding the brakes. The summit is usually cooler than the coast, so pack a layer for the viewpoints.

### East across the Hajar to the coast

Crossing to the east coast is one of the most rewarding drives in the country, with mountains closing in before the road drops to the Gulf of Oman. The Fujairah tourism authority describes the Hajar as [the highest mountain range in the eastern Arabian Peninsula](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-NaturalSites.html), running through both the UAE and Oman.

<photo-set layout="mosaic" keys="uae-road-trip-planning-4,uae-road-trip-planning-5,uae-road-trip-planning-6" alts="ALT4|ALT5|ALT6" caption="CAP4"></photo-set>

Along the coast, Al Bidya Mosque, which the Fujairah tourism authority calls [the oldest mosque in the UAE](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-PlacesOfWorship.html), sits on the road to Dibba north of Fujairah city. To the south, Khorfakkan's beach lies [about halfway between Dibba and Fujairah](https://www.visitsharjah.com/activities/beaches/khorfakkan-beach/). Our [east coast road trip](/stories/uae-east-coast-road-trip/) plans the route stop by stop.

Wadis here can change fast. The Fujairah tourism authority notes that Wadi Ham [remains dry for most of the year](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-NaturalSites.html), but when it rains the dry riverbed turns into a rushing wadi. The same page describes it as the largest and longest wadi in the UAE, with much of it running alongside a main road, so you will see it from the car even if you do not stop.

## Mixing the car with other transport

A road trip does not have to mean driving every day. In the cities, the UK government recommends [only licensed taxis or recognised apps](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), and many travellers only pick up a car on the day they leave town. In Abu Dhabi and Al Ain, the free [Experience Abu Dhabi Shuttle Bus and Al Ain Shuttle Bus](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/getting-around-abu-dhabi/shuttle-bus) link the main sights, with air conditioning and wheelchair access.

In Ras Al Khaimah, the tourism board says [metered taxis and the main ride-hailing apps](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/faqs/) are available, and that the emirate's bus network links the city with Dubai, though it is more limited than in the bigger cities. That makes a hybrid trip possible: taxis in town, a driver for the mountain day, and a hire car only for the long open roads west or east.

On the east coast, give yourself time for the protected landscapes as well as the beaches. The Fujairah tourism authority describes Wadi Wurayah as a [protected wadi with unique flora and fauna](https://fujairahtourism.ae/index_action-NaturalSites.html), so follow any signs and stay on marked routes.

## A week-long loop that links all three

If you have a week and want to see every kind of landscape, the three routes join into a loop. Start in Abu Dhabi and head west for two nights in Al Dhafra. Come back east by way of Al Ain for a night under Jebel Hafit. Then drive north past Dubai to Ras Al Khaimah for two nights and the Jebel Jais road. Finish by crossing the Hajar to the east coast for the last night or two before returning to the airport.

That is a lot of road, so keep each day to one main drive and one or two stops. Arrive before dark, especially on the mountain days, and leave at least one day with no driving at all. If that sounds like too much, cut the far west and spend the extra night on the east coast instead.

## Desert driving and the weather

### Leave dunes to the professionals

Driving on a sealed road through the desert is one thing. Driving on sand is another. The UK government warns that desert excursions [can be dangerous unless you are in a properly equipped 4-wheel-drive vehicle](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), and advises always travelling with other cars, taking a supply of water and a mobile phone, and leaving a copy of your plans with someone. Most hire agreements also limit off-road use, so check yours. If you want to drive in the dunes, join a guided trip instead.

### Heat and the car

In summer, the heat itself becomes a road risk. Ras Al Khaimah's tourism board says summer temperatures, from June to September, [can exceed 45°C, especially inland](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/faqs/). Carry more water than you think you need, never leave anyone in a parked car, and plan stops at places with shade and air conditioning. Outside the cooler months, keep the long drives to the morning.

### Rain, fog and sand

The National Centre of Meteorology [issues weather warnings](https://www.ncm.gov.ae/) and safety advice for heavy rain and floods, sandstorms and thick fog, all of which affect driving. Abu Dhabi's tourism board says rain falls [mainly in winter, from November to March](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/essential-info/climate-and-what-to-wear), and is more common around Al Ain because it is close to the Hajar Mountains.

<photo-set layout="inset-right" keys="uae-road-trip-planning-7" alts="ALT7" caption="CAP7"></photo-set>

The UAE government's road safety page lists [causing death through dangerous driving in situations such as flash floods](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/justice-safety-and-the-law/Safety/road-safety) among serious offences. Never drive into a flooded wadi or underpass. The UK government suggests following the authorities and local media for [flood updates](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security). Our guide to [rain and flooding in the UAE](/stories/uae-rain-and-flooding-advice/) has more.

### Driving in Ramadan

If your trip falls in Ramadan, the UK government warns that [driving may be erratic, particularly around dusk](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), when people are hurrying home to break their fast. It also says eating, drinking or smoking in public in the daytime, including in your car, may cause offence. Plan to be parked up by sunset.

## Before you set off

- **Check your insurance** covers you as a driver and for any activities you plan.
- **Read the latest travel advice.** The UK government asks travellers to keep [departure plans under review](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/warnings-and-insurance) given the regional situation.
- **Plan short driving days.** Aim to arrive before dark, especially in the mountains.
- **Know where you can photograph.** The UK government says it is [illegal to take photos of some government buildings and military installations](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), so keep the camera down near them.
- **Crossing into Oman?** That needs separate paperwork and insurance. Our guide to [combining the UAE and Oman](/stories/uae-and-oman-one-trip/) explains the options.

## Quick answers

**Can tourists drive in the UAE?** Often, yes. The UK government says British visitors can [use a UK photocard licence](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security); other nationalities should check the UAE government's guidance.

**Is there any drink-driving limit?** No. It is [illegal to drink and drive, no matter how small the amount](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security).

**What should I do after a minor accident?** Follow the rules for the emirate you are in, call the police and do not leave the scene before they arrive.

**Do I need a 4x4?** Not for the main roads. Ras Al Khaimah's tourism board [highly recommends one](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/faqs/) for mountain or desert areas.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Does my licence work for driving here, and does the hire company accept it?
- Which parts of my route are better with a driver than self-drive?
- Is the Jebel Jais road open on my dates, and what time should I head down?
- What does the hire agreement say about off-road driving and accidents, and which reporting app should I use?
- How would you change the route if heavy rain or fog is forecast?
- Can the car be taken into Oman, and what extra insurance would that need?
