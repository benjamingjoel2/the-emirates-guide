It starts with something small. A twisted ankle on a heritage walk, a child with a fever that will not break, a stomach bug that turns into a night of dehydration. Suddenly you are sitting in a clinic waiting room, and the receptionist is asking a question you had not thought about: how will you be paying?

That is the moment travel insurance stops being paperwork. The UAE has good hospitals, but they are not free for visitors, and the consequences of being unable to pay can be more serious than you might expect.

So what should travel insurance for the UAE cover? At a minimum: medical treatment and emergency evacuation, every activity you plan to do, any pre-existing conditions, and the full length of your trip, including any side trip to Oman. Then check the gaps that catch people out: quad bikes, hire cars, alcohol and regional disruption. Here is what to look for and why.

## Why travel insurance for the UAE starts with medical cover

### Good hospitals, but not free

The UK Foreign Office says healthcare facilities in the UAE are [similar to those in the UK](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/health), but warns that you may be stopped from using them without travel insurance or the means to pay medical fees. It adds a warning that surprises many visitors: you may be prevented from leaving the UAE if you are unable to pay your medical bill.

It advises having adequate travel health insurance and accessible funds to cover medical treatment abroad and repatriation, and says to contact your insurer or medical assistance company quickly if you are referred to a medical facility.

### Extra care for pregnancy and health conditions

The Foreign Office says it is particularly important to have [appropriate travel insurance for local treatment or unexpected medical evacuation](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/health) if you have a health condition or are pregnant. Tell your insurer about any pregnancy before you buy, and check how far into a pregnancy you are covered.

## What a good UAE policy should include

The Foreign Office sums it up simply: insurance should [cover your itinerary, planned activities and expenses in an emergency](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/warnings-and-insurance). The UK government's wider travel insurance guidance gives a fuller checklist. It suggests your policy should cover:

- [the full length of your trip](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/foreign-travel-insurance), since many policies have a maximum trip length
- treatment in state or private hospitals
- emergency transport, such as an ambulance, which is often charged separately
- pre-existing medical conditions, declared up front
- every activity you plan to do, including sports or adventure tourism
- every place you will visit, even only in transit
- repatriation, and getting home after treatment if you cannot use your original ticket
- reasonable costs for someone to stay with you or travel out to accompany you home
- a 24-hour assistance helpline

The same guidance advises buying insurance as soon as possible after booking your trip, and reading the small print for exclusions.

### Declare everything

Pre-existing conditions are a common trap. The UK guidance says you should declare existing conditions or pending treatment or tests so you are covered for related complications, and that [failing to declare something may invalidate your travel insurance](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/foreign-travel-insurance). The same applies to mental health conditions, which it says you must declare or risk invalidating your policy.

## The gaps that catch people out

### Desert driving and quad bikes

Dune driving is one of the most popular things visitors do in the UAE, and one of the most likely to fall outside a basic policy. The Foreign Office says that if you join a tour for desert driving or quad biking you should go with a reputable company, and [always check that such activities are covered by your insurance](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security).

The UK government guidance is blunter: the [use or hire of quad bikes or mopeds](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/foreign-travel-insurance) is not usually covered, and sports such as jet skiing or skydiving are not usually included in standard policies. If your plans include any of these, look for an add-on or a specialist policy. Our [dune sports safety guide](/stories/dune-sports-safety-uae/) explains what each activity involves.

### Hire cars

If you are hiring a car, the UK guidance says to [check what you are covered for with the hire company](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/foreign-travel-insurance). Travel insurance and car hire insurance are different things, and neither automatically covers the other.

Alcohol matters here too. The Foreign Office says it is illegal to drink and drive in the UAE, [no matter how small the amount](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), and that your insurance is likely to be invalidated in the event of an accident. For more, see [driving in the UAE as a visitor](/stories/driving-in-the-uae-as-a-visitor/).

### Alcohol and drugs more generally

The UK guidance notes that most travel insurance policies [do not cover events after you have drunk excessive alcohol](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/foreign-travel-insurance) or taken recreational drugs. In the UAE, where the drug laws are especially strict, that is one more reason to be careful.

### Pregnancy outside marriage

This is a sensitive point the Foreign Office raises directly. It says that if you become pregnant outside marriage, [your medical insurance may not cover you](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), and advises consulting your medical insurance provider before getting pregnant or giving birth in the UAE. This mainly affects residents and long stays, but it is worth knowing.

## Regional tensions and travel disruption

Read this section even if you have travelled to the Gulf many times before. The Foreign Office says the context in the Middle East remains [deeply unpredictable and the risk of sudden escalation is high](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/warnings-and-insurance), and advises travellers to be prepared for possible flight cancellations, airspace closures and travel disruption.

What this means for your policy:

- **Check what disruption is covered.** The UK guidance notes that some policies offer only [limited cover for terrorist acts](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/foreign-travel-insurance), civil unrest or natural disasters, especially if the event was publicly known when you bought the policy.
- **Check the FCDO status of your destination.** The same guidance warns that if you travel somewhere the Foreign Office advises against all but essential travel, or all travel, your insurance may be invalidated.
- **Buy early.** Cover bought after an event is known may exclude it.
- **Know your supplier failure cover.** The UK guidance notes that cover if an airline or travel agent goes out of business is typically not included, and suggests an ATOL-protected holiday or a policy with supplier failure cover.

## Adding Oman? Check your policy covers it

Many UAE trips include a day in Musandam or a few nights in Muscat. Make sure your policy covers Oman as well as the UAE. You will need proof of it anyway: the Foreign Office says that at Omani border control you must be able to show [proof of your travel health insurance](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/oman/entry-requirements). See our guide to [combining the UAE and Oman in one trip](/stories/uae-and-oman-one-trip/).

## Before you fly: a short checklist

- Take your policy number and your insurer's 24-hour emergency number with you, and save them on your phone.
- Share your policy details with your travel companions and someone at home, as the UK guidance suggests.
- Keep a card or other accessible funds available in case treatment must be paid upfront.
- If a tour operator asks about insurance, take it as a good sign. Our guide to [booking a UAE tour operator safely](/stories/booking-a-uae-tour-operator-safely/) explains what else to check.

## If you need to use it

In a medical emergency in the UAE, the Foreign Office says to [dial 998 and ask for an ambulance](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/health), then contact your insurer quickly. Keep every receipt, report and medical note, and ask the clinic for itemised bills. More planning advice is on our [travel tips page](/travel-tips/).

## Quick answers

**Do I need travel insurance to visit the UAE?**
It is strongly advised. The Foreign Office warns that you may be stopped from using healthcare facilities without insurance or the means to pay, and may be prevented from leaving if you cannot pay a medical bill.

**Does travel insurance cover desert safaris?**
Check your policy. The Foreign Office advises confirming that desert driving and quad biking are covered, and UK guidance says quad bike use is not usually included in standard policies.

**Do I need insurance to enter Oman?**
The Foreign Office says you must be able to show proof of travel health insurance at Omani border control.

**Will drink-driving void my insurance?**
The Foreign Office says drink-driving in the UAE is illegal at any level and that your insurance is likely to be invalidated in an accident.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Which activities on this trip involve vehicles, boats, quad bikes or other equipment that my insurer may treat as adventure sports?
- Do you hold your own liability insurance for the activity, and what does it cover?
- What is your procedure if a guest is injured or falls ill, and which hospital or clinic would you go to?
- Does the trip cross into Oman, even briefly, so I can check my policy covers it?
- What happens to my booking if flights are disrupted or travel advice changes before the trip?
