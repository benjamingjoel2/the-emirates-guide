In the last half hour before sunset during Ramadan, restaurant tables across the UAE are laid, full and untouched. Everyone is waiting for the same sound.

Outside, traffic thickens as people hurry home, and volunteers hand out water and dates near mosques. Then the call to prayer for Maghrib rings out, glasses are lifted, a date is eaten, and the whole country exhales. Within an hour the streets are busy again, and they stay that way late into the night.

Ramadan is the most important month of the Islamic year, and in the UAE it changes the rhythm of daily life. For visitors that can sound daunting. It does not need to be. Hotels, attractions and malls stay open, and many people who travel during Ramadan come away saying the evenings were the best part of their trip. What it asks of you is awareness: of what not to do in public during daylight, of how to dress, and of how the shape of the day shifts.

This guide sets out what changes, drawing on official UAE government and UK government guidance. Rules and their enforcement can change, so check the linked sources before you travel.

## Why the dates move every year

The UAE government portal explains that Ramadan is the ninth month of the Islamic calendar and that [capable Muslims abstain from eating and drinking from dawn to dusk](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/public-holidays-and-religious-affairs/ramadan). The month begins with the first sighting of the new crescent moon and lasts 29 or 30 days, depending on when the next crescent is seen. In the UAE, the start is confirmed by an official announcement from the moon-sighting committee under the UAE Council for Fatwa.

Because the Islamic calendar has 354 days, the same page notes that Ramadan [arrives about 11 days earlier each year](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/public-holidays-and-religious-affairs/ramadan) in the Gregorian calendar. That is why it moves through the seasons over the years, and why last year's dates are no guide to this year's. The UK government's advice also notes that [Ramadan dates vary by year and country](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security).

For the next Ramadan, [The National reports](https://www.thenationalnews.com/news/uae/2026/10/06/ramadan-2027-uae-dates-eid-al-fitr-holiday/) that the UAE's Astronomy Centre has projected a start date of 8 February 2027, with Eid Al Fitr expected on 9 or 10 March 2027. These are projections, not confirmed dates: the final word comes with the moon sighting. If you are booking a trip for February or March 2027, assume Ramadan may overlap and plan for it. For how this fits into the wider year, see our guide to [the best time to visit the UAE, month by month](/stories/best-time-to-visit-uae-month-by-month/).

### The weeks before

Ramadan does not arrive out of nowhere. The government portal says that UAE Ramadan traditions start around the middle of Shaaban, the month before, with a day known as [Hagg Al Layla](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/public-holidays-and-religious-affairs/ramadan), when Emirati children dress in traditional clothes and go from house to house reciting songs and poems, collecting sweets and nuts in cloth bags. If your trip falls in the weeks before Ramadan, you may notice shops stocking dates, lanterns and decorations, and supermarkets filling up as families prepare.

## The water bottle in your hand

It is mid-morning, you are walking between sights, and you reach for your water without thinking. This is the habit to break. Non-Muslims are [not required to fast](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/public-holidays-and-religious-affairs/ramadan), but the UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) warns that during Ramadan it may cause offence if you [eat, drink, smoke or chew gum in public in the daytime, including in your car](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security).

In practice:

- Keep water out of sight while walking around during the day, and drink discreetly in a private or designated space.
- Do not snack on the metro, in a taxi, in your hire car or on a park bench in daylight.
- Eat in your hotel or in restaurants that are serving during the day.

You will not go hungry. The UAE government says that in shopping malls, [restaurants and food courts provide designated areas away from those who are fasting](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/public-holidays-and-religious-affairs/ramadan), and that some restaurants may adjust their hours or close during the day but open fully at iftar. The FCDO adds that hotels and restaurants serving food in fasting hours [may separate you from Muslim guests, for example with screens](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security). Eating breakfast behind a partition feels a little unusual the first time. It is simply how places make room for everyone.

A small tip that saves a lot of awkwardness: eat a proper breakfast before you head out, so a long morning of sightseeing does not leave you searching for somewhere to snack.

### Staying well while others fast

Being discreet about drinking does not mean going without. Drink properly at your hotel before you leave, carry water in a bag, and step into a designated dining area or back to your room when you need it. The government portal's health advice for those fasting is to [drink enough water during the night and not exert yourself physically when fasting](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/public-holidays-and-religious-affairs/ramadan), a reminder that the whole country slows down in the afternoon. It is a sensible time for an indoor museum or a rest rather than a long outdoor walk.

The same page notes that people who are ill or pregnant are not obliged to fast, and that fasting is not obligatory for young children. No one will expect your family to fast, but older children travelling with you will find it easier if you explain the basics before you arrive.

## Turning the volume down

The FCDO also lists [playing loud music or dancing, and swearing in public](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) as behaviour that may cause offence during Ramadan. Keep car stereos and speakers low, especially near mosques and residential areas.

Two more pieces of FCDO advice are worth taking to heart. Fasting can cause tiredness, particularly in the late afternoon and early evening, so be patient and show tolerance. A slow queue or a short answer at that time of day is rarely personal. And driving may be erratic, particularly at dusk, when people are trying to get home to break their fast. If you are driving, this is the time to slow right down, or simply stay put until after iftar.

## Dressing for the month

Dress codes apply all year, but the FCDO says that [clothing that does not meet local dress codes may cause more offence during Ramadan](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security). Its general guidance is to dress modestly in public areas such as shopping malls: women should cover shoulders and knees, underwear should not be visible, and transparent clothing or clothing with imagery or phrases that could offend should be avoided. Swimwear belongs on beaches and at pools.

Visit Abu Dhabi gives similar advice, saying it is [especially recommended that you dress more conservatively during Ramadan](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/essential-info/climate-and-what-to-wear). Loose, light layers that cover shoulders and knees work for both men and women and stay comfortable in the heat. A light scarf or overshirt in your bag means you are ready for a mall, a souk or a mosque without going back to the hotel. Our guide to [what to wear in the UAE](/stories/what-to-wear-uae-etiquette/) goes into more detail.

## A slower day, a later night

The cities feel different by day: calmer, with fewer people on the streets and a quieter hum in the malls. Then, after sunset, everything stretches late. The UAE government portal says that:

- [Supermarkets and grocery shops are open as usual, and malls stay open until late at night](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/public-holidays-and-religious-affairs/ramadan).
- Amusement parks, restaurants, cultural and tourist sites remain open throughout the day.
- Under UAE labour law, working hours are reduced by two hours per day for Muslim and non-Muslim employees.
- Paid parking hours change during Ramadan, with details shown on parking meters.
- Taxis can be hard to find in the evenings, when many drivers are breaking their fast, so it is better to book in advance.

The shorter working day means offices, government service counters and some businesses keep different hours from usual. The FCDO advises that you [check opening hours of shops and restaurants](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), and we would add attractions and tours to that list. Confirm times directly with the venue or your operator, rather than relying on hours you found online before Ramadan began.

### How a visitor's day tends to unfold

A Ramadan day works best if you plan it in three parts. Mornings are for sightseeing that needs energy and daylight: a museum, a heritage district, a drive to another emirate. Early afternoon is the quiet stretch, when many people are resting and you may want to as well. From late afternoon, think about where you want to be at sunset, because roads fill and taxis thin out in the run-up to iftar, and it is easier to already be at your restaurant, hotel or a waterside spot than to be crossing the city.

After iftar the day starts again. Malls, souks and promenades fill with families, and many visitors find this the most sociable time of their trip.

## Where the evenings come alive

The two meals of Ramadan are suhoor, eaten before dawn, and iftar, which breaks the fast at sunset. According to the UAE government, [the fast is traditionally broken with dates and laban (buttermilk)](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/public-holidays-and-religious-affairs/ramadan). The same page describes the iftar cannon, fired in many places across the country to signal the moment the fast ends, as an integral part of the culture, and says it can be heard around 8 to 10 km away. If you hear a deep boom roll across the city at dusk, that is what it is.

This is when Ramadan opens up to visitors. The government notes that cities become especially lively after sunset and that visitors can enjoy iftar and suhoor at many restaurants. It also describes the tradition of free iftar meals at mosques, malls and other public places, and of Ramadan tents set up by charities where people of different nationalities and backgrounds sit together to break their fast. These tents exist for those fasting and those in need. If you are curious, be respectful, and do not treat them as a tourist attraction.

### What is on the table

The government portal describes dates as the [bread of the desert](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/public-holidays-and-religious-affairs/ramadan) in the UAE and the wider Gulf, and names a few dishes you are likely to meet. Alqurs is a bread-like crumble made with dates and cardamom, a popular Emirati sweet during the month, and harees and threed are common savoury dishes. On the first evening of Ramadan, families traditionally gather at the home of the head of the family for their first iftar together. Our guide to [joining an iftar as a visitor](/stories/how-visitors-can-join-iftar/) covers the etiquette in more depth.

### Two ways in for visitors

For a hosted introduction, Dubai's Sheikh Mohammed bin Rashid Al Maktoum Centre for Cultural Understanding, in the Al Fahidi Historical Neighbourhood, runs a [traditional Ramadan iftar programme](https://cultures.ae/program/ramadan-iftar-program/) that includes a guided mosque visit and cultural conversation. The programme's page says it will come back for the next Ramadan, so check it nearer the time for dates and booking. It is a good way to ask the questions you might feel shy about asking elsewhere.

In Abu Dhabi, the Sheikh Zayed Grand Mosque publishes Ramadan information for worshippers and asks [non-worshipping visitors to book an access pass online in advance](https://szgmc.gov.ae/en/worshippers/ramadan-kareem). Check its website for current visiting arrangements before you go.

### After iftar

Many Muslims attend Tarawih prayers in the mid-evening, and during the last ten nights of the month many spend long hours in mosques, in anticipation of Laylat Al Qadr, the night the government portal describes as that of the first revelation of the Quran. The portal also mentions night markets and community events across the emirates. Expect busy roads around mosques at prayer times, and a late, sociable buzz in malls and public spaces, with families out together long after you might expect them to be home.

A simple greeting goes a long way. People wish each other ["Ramadan Kareem" or "Ramadan Mubarak"](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/public-holidays-and-religious-affairs/ramadan), both meaning a blessed Ramadan, and visitors are welcome to say it too. If you want to know what will be on the table when you sit down to eat, our guide to [Emirati food: what to eat and where](/stories/emirati-food-what-to-eat-where/) is a good primer.

## Giving, and what to avoid

Ramadan is a month of charity, and you will see it everywhere: boxes of meals, dates and water handed out near mosques and malls before iftar, and donation counters in public places. If you want to give, the government portal points to registered charitable organisations and designated donation boxes, and notes that under the UAE's donations rules [individuals are not allowed to organise their own fund-raising](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/public-holidays-and-religious-affairs/ramadan); it must go through a licensed charity. The same page says begging is illegal and that visitors may notice more of it during Ramadan. The simplest approach is to give through an established organisation rather than handing money to people in the street.

## Eid Al Fitr: the celebration at the end

Ramadan ends with Eid Al Fitr, a celebration of family, new clothes, visits and food. The UAE's official list of public holidays gives [Eid Al Fitr as 1 to 3 Shawwal](https://u.ae/en/information-and-services/public-holidays-and-religious-affairs/public-holidays), and adds that if Ramadan lasts 30 days, the 30th day is also an official holiday. Like all Islamic holidays, it is determined by moon sighting, so the exact days are confirmed only shortly beforehand.

For visitors, Eid means a festive atmosphere, busier attractions, beaches and parks, and heavier traffic as residents travel or visit family. Book accommodation and popular activities early if your trip overlaps, and keep your plans for those days flexible in case the dates shift. Our guide to [Eid in the UAE for visitors](/stories/eid-in-the-uae-for-visitors/) explains what to expect over the holiday itself.

## Small habits that make a big difference

- Carry a bottle in your bag rather than in your hand, and drink in private.
- Plan sightseeing for the morning, and save outdoor activities and markets for after iftar.
- Avoid driving in the hour before sunset where you can.
- Book taxis in advance for the evening.
- Confirm tour and attraction timings during the week you travel.

## Quick answers

**Do tourists have to fast during Ramadan in the UAE?** No. The UAE government says non-Muslims are not required to fast, but you should not eat, drink, smoke or chew gum in public during daylight hours.

**Are attractions and malls open during Ramadan?** Yes. The government portal says attractions, cultural and tourist sites stay open during the day and malls stay open late into the night, though some hours change, so check before you go.

**Can I eat in restaurants during the day?** Yes, in hotels and in restaurants that are serving. Malls have designated dining areas, and some places use screens to separate diners from those who are fasting.

**When does Ramadan start in 2027?** The UAE's Astronomy Centre has projected 8 February 2027, according to The National, but the start is confirmed only by the official moon sighting.

**Can visitors join an iftar?** Yes. Many restaurants serve iftar, and Dubai's Centre for Cultural Understanding runs a hosted iftar programme during Ramadan.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Will any of my tours or activities run at different times during Ramadan, and how and when will you confirm the new timings?
- Where can I eat and drink discreetly during daytime on tours, for example on a desert trip or a long drive between emirates?
- Can you recommend a respectful way to experience an iftar, and what is expected of guests?
- How will the shorter working day and evening traffic affect transfers and pickups, especially around sunset?
- If my trip overlaps with Eid Al Fitr, which sights are likely to be busiest, and what should I book ahead?
- Is there a dress code for any of the places on my itinerary during Ramadan?
