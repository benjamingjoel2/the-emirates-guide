Step off a plane in the UAE and the first thing you notice is how mixed the crowd is. In the arrivals hall, in the metro, in the food court of any big mall, you will see crisp white kanduras and flowing black abayas alongside jeans, sundresses, office suits and gym kit. It can make the dress code feel invisible, and that is exactly what catches first-timers out: the rules are relaxed in some places and firm in others, and the difference is not always signposted.

The good news is that the etiquette here is mostly common sense dressed in local clothes. Cover up a little more than you would at home in public, keep affection private, ask before you photograph anyone, and accept hospitality when it is offered. Get those right and you will find people warm, patient and quick to forgive an honest mistake.

What follows is a practical walk through the questions we hear most often, from what to pack for a mall day to how to hold your cup in a majlis. Laws and venue rules do change, so treat this as a starting point and check the official sources linked throughout before you travel.

## What to wear in the cities and malls

On a normal day in Dubai or Abu Dhabi, nobody expects visitors to dress like Emiratis. The expectation is simply modesty. The [FCDO's advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) is the clearest summary we have found: dress modestly in public areas like shopping malls, avoid transparent clothing or anything with imagery or phrases that could offend, and wear swimming attire only on beaches or at swimming pools. It also notes that some venues, events or buildings have their own dress codes, and that cross-dressing is illegal.

In practice, that means:

- **For women:** loose trousers, midi or maxi skirts, dresses that reach the knee or below, and tops that cover the shoulders. A light scarf or cardigan in your bag solves most problems, and it doubles as protection against fierce air conditioning.
- **For men:** longer shorts or trousers with a T-shirt are a safe choice; save sleeveless vests and bare chests for the beach or pool.
- **For everyone:** skip slogan T-shirts. A joke that reads fine at home may land very differently here.

The tourist board in Ras Al Khaimah puts it plainly: there are [no official rules for visitors' attire](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/plan-your-trip/cultural-awareness/), but visitors are expected to dress in a modest way that respects local citizens and their culture. That spirit applies across all seven emirates. If you are heading to more traditional areas, older souks or the northern emirates, lean towards the more covered end of your suitcase.

Evenings out are a slight exception. Visit Ras Al Khaimah's guide for women travellers notes that [in bars or nightclubs you can wear what you would wear at home](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/blog/all-social-customs-and-etiquette-every-female-traveler-should-know/), but suggests carrying a scarf or shawl to cover up when moving between venues through public spaces.

## Beaches, pools and the walk in between

Hotel pools and public beaches are where the dress code relaxes, and bikinis and swim shorts are normal there. The catch is the bit in between. The [FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) is clear that swimwear belongs on beaches and at pools, and the [Visit Ras Al Khaimah guide](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/blog/all-social-customs-and-etiquette-every-female-traveler-should-know/) adds the practical point: don't keep it on when you leave.

So pack a cover-up: a kaftan, shirt dress, linen shirt or pair of shorts you can throw on before walking through a hotel lobby, into a beach café or along a corniche. Public beaches are family spaces too, which is worth remembering when you choose where to lay your towel.

## Visiting a mosque

This is where the rules are firmest, and the [Sheikh Zayed Grand Mosque](/destinations/abu-dhabi/) in Abu Dhabi is the visit most travellers plan for. The mosque's own [visitor code of conduct](https://www.szgmc.gov.ae/en/mosque-manner) sets out what to wear:

- **Women:** clothing that is loose-fitting down to the ankles, with sleeves reaching all the way to the wrists, and full hair coverage. Transparent, tight-fitting or revealing clothes should be avoided.
- **Men:** clothing that covers the entire legs is preferred, and transparent, tight-fitting or sleeveless shirts should be avoided.
- **Everyone:** the centre asks visitors to avoid excessive jewellery, heavy make-up and vibrant colours, and clothing with slogans or inappropriate drawings.

The same page covers behaviour: keep your voice down and your phone on silent, take photographs only at the designated photo stops, and no public displays of intimacy. Food and drinks other than water are not allowed inside, and personal photos with non-professional equipment are permitted while commercial or media photography needs a permit.

Our advice is to arrive already dressed for it rather than counting on borrowing anything at the door. A long, loose dress or wide trousers with a long-sleeved top and a scarf you can wrap securely over your hair works for women. Long trousers and a T-shirt with sleeves works for men. Rules and visiting arrangements change, so check the [Sheikh Zayed Grand Mosque Center's page](https://www.szgmc.gov.ae/en/mosque-manner) close to your visit. Other mosques that welcome visitors generally follow the same spirit, even if the details differ.

## Photos of people

Few things cause more avoidable trouble. The [FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) is direct: do not photograph people without their permission, and it notes that men have been arrested for photographing women on beaches. It also warns that photographing some government buildings and military installations is illegal, and that hobbies like plane spotting can be misunderstood near airports and military sites.

The line is when a person, especially a woman or a family, becomes the subject. Ask first, accept a no with a smile, and take the same care before posting anyone's face online.

## Affection, language and behaviour in public

Keep affection between couples discreet. According to the [FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security), showing affection in public is frowned upon and you can be arrested for kissing in public. The same advice says it is illegal to swear and make rude gestures, including online, and that you could be jailed or deported. That includes a gesture at another driver in traffic.

## Alcohol

Alcohol is available, but only in the right places. The [FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) sets out the main points:

- It is illegal to drink or be under the influence of alcohol in a public place anywhere in the UAE.
- The legal age for buying and drinking alcohol is 21 in Abu Dhabi, Dubai and the northern emirates where it is permitted.
- Drinking or possessing alcohol is illegal in the [Emirate of Sharjah](/destinations/sharjah/).
- Visitors can drink in licensed venues such as hotel bars, restaurants and clubs.
- It is illegal to drink and drive, however small the amount.

Plan your route home before the evening begins: the street and the taxi queue count as public places.

## Ramadan

During the holy month the expectations rise a notch. The [FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/united-arab-emirates/safety-and-security) notes that eating, drinking, smoking or chewing gum in public during daytime may cause offence, as can loud music, dancing and swearing, and that clothing which does not meet local dress codes may cause more offence at this time. We cover the month in detail in [visiting the UAE during Ramadan](/stories/visiting-uae-during-ramadan/).

## Friday

Friday is the main congregational prayer day in Islam, and around the midday prayer you will notice mosque car parks filling and some smaller shops pausing. If you are planning a mosque visit, a souk trip or a heritage site on a Friday, check that day's visiting times on the official site first.

## Greetings and the right hand

Emiratis are generous hosts. The UAE government portal describes them as [warm hosts who treat their guests with utmost honour](https://u.ae/en/about-the-uae/culture/theaspectsofuaessociallife), and notes that Emirati men may greet each other by touching noses, followed by a handshake, an embrace and greetings of peace. That greeting is between Emirati men; visitors are not expected to copy it.

For handshakes between men and women, follow the lead of the other person. [Visit Abu Dhabi](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/majlis) explains that interaction between men and women is more reserved, that a woman will offer her hand if she wishes to shake, and that when you are unsure a friendly smile is always appreciated.

The right hand matters more than many visitors expect. [Visit Ras Al Khaimah](https://visitrasalkhaimah.com/blog/all-social-customs-and-etiquette-every-female-traveler-should-know/) advises taking plates, cups and food with your right hand, and notes that using the left is considered disrespectful. The same guide says hosts may be offended if food or drink is refused, so if you have a medical or dietary reason, explain it kindly.

## In a majlis, with Arabic coffee

If you visit a heritage house, a cultural centre or a family home, you may be welcomed into a majlis. UNESCO, which lists the majlis as intangible cultural heritage, describes it as a [sitting place where the community gathers](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/majlis-a-cultural-and-social-space-01076), typically with carpets on the floor and cushions against the wall, and a stove or fire to prepare coffee. Sitting on the floor is normal, so loose, longer clothes are more comfortable as well as more appropriate.

Coffee and dates will almost certainly appear. UNESCO's entry on Arabic coffee notes [rules of etiquette passed down between generations](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/arabic-coffee-a-symbol-of-generosity-02111): older people and guests are served first, the coffee is poured with the right hand, and the person drinking shakes the cup to signal they have finished. [Visit Abu Dhabi](https://visitabudhabi.ae/en/plan-your-trip/culture-and-traditions/coffee-and-dates) adds that the coffee is poured just a quarter full into tiny handleless cups, with guests enjoying one to three cups as a sign of respect.

In practice: take the cup with your right hand, sip, and when you have had enough, give the cup a gentle side-to-side shake as you hand it back. Otherwise it will keep being refilled, which is hospitality, not a hint. For more on what lands on the table alongside the coffee, read our guide to [Emirati food and where to eat it](/stories/emirati-food-what-to-eat-where/).

## A packing list that covers it all

- Two or three pairs of loose, lightweight trousers or long skirts
- Tops in breathable fabrics with sleeves that cover the shoulders
- One long-sleeved, ankle-length outfit for mosque visits, plus a scarf for women
- A cover-up for beach and pool days
- A light layer for air-conditioned malls, taxis and the metro
- Comfortable shoes you can slip off easily at a majlis or mosque

## Questions to ask your operator

- Which places on my itinerary have their own dress codes, and what exactly do they ask for?
- Does your guide accompany visitors into the Sheikh Zayed Grand Mosque, and what should we wear on the day?
- If we visit a heritage house or majlis, how should we greet our hosts and is it fine to ask questions?
- Are there any places on the route where photography is restricted or where we should be extra careful with our cameras?
- Does our trip fall during Ramadan or on a Friday, and how does that change the plan?
- If we are visiting Sharjah, is there anything about the itinerary we should know regarding alcohol or dress?
